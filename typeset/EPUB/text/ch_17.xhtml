Chapter 17

The barometer dropped before breakfast.

Mara felt it in her knuckles first—the ache that came with pressure change, the way her joints remembered weather the way other people remembered names. She had been eating her porridge when the change arrived, standing at the window with the bowl in her hands, watching the harbor through the gray morning light. The boats were still at their moorings. The water was calm. And then it wasn’t.

The ache moved up her arms. She set the bowl down on the table and crossed to the barometer on the wall, the old brass instrument her father had hung there forty years ago and that she had never once moved. The needle was falling. Had been falling, she realized, for hours. She had been watching the harbor and not watching the instrument and the needle had dropped a quarter inch while she wasn’t looking and now it was still dropping, still falling, and she understood what that meant.

November. The sea was never quiet in November.

She finished her porridge standing up, washing it down with cold tea, and dressed in the clothes she kept by the door for weather like this. Wool trousers. A heavy sweater her mother had made, the one with the reinforced elbows. The boots that had seen thirty years of shore walks. She didn’t need to check the window again. She knew what she would see.

The wind had shifted. Northwest now, the direction that brought weather from the open Atlantic, the direction that meant the harbor was protected and the north shore was not. The boats would be fine in the harbor. The boats on the north side of the island, the fishing boats that stayed out until they had to come in, those boats would be watching the horizon now, wondering whether to run for home or ride it out.

Mara pulled on her coat and stepped outside.

The air was cold and it tasted different—saltier, heavier, the way it always tasted before a storm came in from the open water. She could see the clouds building in the northwest, gray pushed against gray, layers of weather stacked on top of each other like sediment. This was not the quick weather that blew through in an afternoon. This was the slow storm, the big storm, the kind that came in November and stayed for days.

She walked down toward the harbor, not hurrying, because hurrying would make people ask questions and she wasn’t ready to answer questions yet. She had a picture in her head of what the message had said, and she had been trying for two days now to decide whether the picture was real or whether she had invented it in the night, whether the words she remembered were the words the reading had given her or the words she had written herself in the dark, half-asleep, her hand moving without her permission.

She did not know which it was. She had never been unsure like this before.

The message had said: The sea is coming. It means you can run or you can stay.

The message had said: A boat that should not float will float, and a child of the island will be the only hand that reaches the shore.

The message had said: The storm comes with the tide, and the tide comes with the storm, and if the child of the island is on the water when the two meet, the sea will keep what it has marked.

She had written those words in her own hand, in a script that was hers and not hers, and she had watched the ink appear on the paper as though the paper itself were bleeding, and she had known what it meant. She had known, and she had tried to un-know it, and she had failed, and now the barometer was falling and the wind was shifting and she could taste the storm on the air the way she had tasted it a hundred times before, when she was the keeper and the light was hers to tend and the sea was a thing she could warn people about if they were willing to listen.

No one had ever been willing to listen.

She reached the harbor and stopped at the top of the slope that led down to the water. The boats were bobbing at their moorings, the green paint bright against the gray water, the rigging sounds sharp in the cold air. A gull screamed somewhere behind her. The Inn sat across the small square, its windows dark, its chimney sending up a thin line of smoke.

The ferry was in port. She could see it from here, the flat-bottomed vessel that made the crossing twice a week, that carried the mail and the supplies and sometimes the people who needed to leave or needed to come home. It would make the crossing again tomorrow, if the weather allowed. That was what Finlay had said. That was what Finlay believed.

Finlay believed that tomorrow was a day like any other, and that the ferry would come unless the weather stopped it, and that the weather was something you watched and planned around and did not have to fear unless it was worse than you expected. Finlay believed this because Finlay had to believe it. Finlay ran the Inn and the League and the schedules and the lists, and if Finlay did not believe that tomorrow could be managed, Finlay would have to believe something else, and what Finlay believed instead was worse.

She crossed the square and pushed open the door of the Inn.

The common room was empty at this hour. The fire was low in the grate, banked for the day, and the tables were wiped clean and waiting for the evening trade. Finlay was behind the bar, as he always was, doing something with papers and a ledger, his reading glasses perched on the end of his nose. He looked up when she came in.

“Mara.”

“The barometer’s dropping.”

He nodded toward the window. “I know. Tam from the north shore was in earlier. He’s running his traps early. Says the birds are moving south.”

“The birds are right.”

“Middle of November. Happens every year.”

“This isn’t every year.”

He set down his pen. He took off his glasses. He had the look he got when he was about to disagree with her, which was most of the time, because she was the keeper who had watched eleven men die and he was the innkeeper who had watched his wife die and neither of them knew how to talk about the things they had watched.

“You’re going to tell me about the message again.”

“I’m going to tell you what the weather is going to do.”

“Then tell me.”

She crossed to the bar and stood across from him, the polished wood between them, the smell of old beer and old smoke in the air. “The storm that’s coming. It’s not the one we expected. The one we expected was another week off, maybe two. This one is forming now. The pressure’s dropping faster than it should. The wind’s already shifted. By tonight it’ll be blowing fifty knots from the northwest, and the waves on the north shore will be fifteen feet high, and the ferry won’t be able to make the crossing.”

“Tomorrow’s a week day. Ferry runs every Tuesday and Friday.”

“I know when the ferry runs.”

“Then you know tomorrow’s not a ferry day.”

“It is now. She’s coming home for the funeral anniversary. You said she was coming tomorrow.”

Finlay’s face changed. Not much—a tightening around the eyes, a stillness in the jaw. He did not like to talk about his daughter. He did not like to admit that he had asked her to come home. He did not like to admit that he had been the one to suggest the timing, the week of the anniversary, when the island would be quiet and there would be time to remember and to talk about things that were hard to talk about.

“She’s coming on the Tuesday ferry. She’s already booked the passage. I confirmed it myself.”

“Cancel it.”

“Mara.”

“Cancel it. She needs to stay on the island.”

“That’s not something you get to tell me about my daughter.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you telling me?”

She made herself hold his eyes. “Because the reading named her. Because the sea has marked her. Because if she gets on that ferry tomorrow and the weather turns the way it’s turning, she will not come home. She will not come home, Finlay. She will not come home, and there is nothing either of us can do about it unless she is here when the storm hits.”

The silence stretched between them. The fire crackled in the grate. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed again.

“You don’t believe that.”

“I believe it.”

“You don’t believe it. You found a bottle with some words in it, words you wrote yourself without remembering writing them, and now you’re standing here telling me my daughter is marked for drowning.” He shook his head. “I’ve known you for forty years, Mara. You’ve never said anything like this before. What happened to you?”

“Things have happened.”

“Since the bottle?”

“Since the message.”

“What message? You’ve told me there was a message. You’ve told me it said something about a child of the island. You’ve told me it was a warning. But you haven’t told me what it actually said, word for word, the way it came to you.”

She had not told him. She had been avoiding telling him because once she told him, it would be real for him the way it was real for her, and she did not know what he would do with that reality. She did not know if he would believe her or if he would fight her or if he would simply refuse to hear it, the way he had refused to hear anything about his wife’s illness until there was nothing left to hear.

But the barometer was dropping. And the wind was shifting. And tomorrow was too close.

“It said a boat that should not float will float.”

Finlay looked at her. His face was unreadable.

“It said a child of the island will be the only hand that reaches the shore.”

“That’s not what you told me before.”

“I told you what I could. I told you what I understood. This is what it said. Word for word. My hand wrote it and I watched.”

“A boat that should not float.”

“Yes.”

“And Eilidh is the only hand that reaches the shore.”

“Yes.”

“That means she’s the survivor. Doesn’t it? It means she lives.”

“It means she’s marked. It means the sea has claimed her. It means the ferry that should bring her home will not bring her home. It means the storm that should wait will not wait. It means every piece of luck and timing and chance that stands between her and the water has already been decided, and the only variable is whether she’s here when it happens or whether she’s out on the water when it finds her.”

Finlay stared at her. His hands were flat on the bar, pressing down, as though he was trying to hold something in place. “You’re saying she’ll die if she comes tomorrow.”

“I’m saying the reading says she will die if she’s not here when the storm hits. I’m saying the storm is coming early and I don’t know why and I don’t know what it means, but I know that she’s supposed to be on the water tomorrow and the water is going to be wrong tomorrow and I don’t know what happens when the reading and the weather disagree, but I know that I don’t want to find out.”

Finlay was quiet for a long moment. Then he picked up his phone from behind the bar.

“What are you doing?”

“I’m calling her. I’m telling her not to come.”

“Do that. Tell her to stay on the mainland until this passes.”

He dialed. He put the phone to his ear. He listened. He pulled the phone away and looked at the screen.

“No answer.”

“Call again.”

“She’s probably at work.”

“Call again.”

He called again. He listened again. He pulled the phone away and shook his head.

“Voicemail again.”

“Tell her to stay.”

“I am.” He set the phone down on the bar. “I’m telling her. But she doesn’t have to listen. She’s thirty-two years old. She makes her own choices.”

“She chose to come home. You asked her to come home.”

“I asked her to come for the anniversary. That’s different.”

“Is it?”

Finlay didn’t answer.

“The ferry comes at what time?”

“Two in the afternoon. If it comes.”

“If. You said if.”

“I said if the weather allows. The ferry comes unless the weather stops it.”

“And if the weather stops it?”

“Then it doesn’t come.”

“Then she stays on the mainland.”

“Then she’s safe. That’s what you said. That’s what you wanted.”

“I want her to be here. I want her to be on the island when the storm hits. I want her away from the water.”

“She’ll be safe on the mainland.”

“The reading doesn’t care where she is.”

The words hung in the air between them. Finlay’s face had gone hard, the way it went hard when someone told him something he didn’t want to believe, and she recognized that look because she had seen it before, the look of a man who had built his life on the assumption that the world was manageable and who did not want to hear that some things could not be managed.

“What do you mean, it doesn’t care where she is?”

“I mean the reading doesn’t stop because she’s not on the island. I mean the clause is working. The sea has marked her, and the mark follows. If she’s on the mainland when the storm hits, the storm will find her there. If she gets on the ferry tomorrow, the ferry will fail. If she drives to her flat and locks the door and doesn’t come out until the weather clears, something will happen. Something will bring her to the water. The readings don’t care about geography.”

Finlay was staring at her. “That’s not possible.”

“It’s happened before. In the Registry. There are cases where the survivor was removed from the island before the wreck. They brought the wreck to her. The sea doesn’t forget what it’s claimed.”

“That’s—Mara, that’s insane. That’s not how the world works.”

“I know.”

“You can’t believe that.”

“I know I can’t prove it. I know it sounds insane. I know you think I’ve lost my mind.” She held his gaze. “But I’m asking you. I’m asking you to call her again. I’m asking you to tell her not to come. Not because of the weather. Not because of the storm. Because of what the message said. Can you do that? Can you tell her the truth?”

Finlay picked up his phone. He looked at the screen. He set it down again.

“I can’t tell her that.”

“Finlay.”

“I can’t tell my daughter that the sea has marked her for death because of a message in a bottle. I can’t tell her that the reason she shouldn’t come home is because a prophecy says she’s going to drown. She’s going to think I’ve gone insane. She’s going to think you’re insane. She’s going to think the whole island has lost its mind.”

“She already thinks the island has lost its mind. She left, didn’t she?”

Finlay flinched. It was a small movement, barely visible, but she saw it and he knew she had seen it, and for a moment neither of them said anything.

“I’ll tell her the weather’s bad. I’ll tell her not to travel. I’ll tell her to wait a few days.”

“That’s not enough.”

“It’s what I can do.”

Mara looked at him. She had known this would happen. She had known he would not believe, would not understand, would do the practical thing and not the hard thing and call it a choice when it was a failure. She had known, and she had come anyway, because the barometer was dropping and the wind was shifting and there was no one else who could tell him what she was telling him.

She had done what she could. Now she would have to do what came next.

She turned and walked out of the Inn.

The storm hit that night.

Mara felt it coming the way she always felt storms coming—through the walls of the cottage, through the sound of the sea that never stopped, through the way the air pressure changed and her bones remembered what was coming before her eyes could see it. She did not light the lamp. She sat in the dark by the window and watched the harbor through the rain, the boats jerking at their moorings, the water white with foam.

The wind screamed around the cottage. The rain came horizontal. The sea she could not see in the darkness was moving the way it always moved when it was angry, and she could hear it—could feel it—through the stone walls that had held for a hundred and thirty years, through the windows that rattled in their frames, through the door that she had learned to brace against the worst of the weather.

She did not sleep. She watched.

Somewhere out on the water, the ferry was in port or it was not. Somewhere on the mainland, Eilidh was listening to her father’s message or she was not. Somewhere, the reading was working or it was not, and the storm was gathering or it was dispersing, and the tide was coming in or it was going out, and all the choices that had been made and not made were aligning themselves toward a single point where the weather and the prophecy would meet and something would be decided.

She did not know what that something would be. She did not know if the reading could be changed or if it was fixed the way the tide was fixed, coming in whether you were ready or not, taking what it had claimed whether you had tried to save it or not. She had spent fifteen years believing that witnessing was enough, that watching was enough, that if she could not stop the sea from taking things then she could at least be there when it did, could at least remember what had been taken and honor the taking.

But the reading named someone she knew. The reading named a child she had watched grow up, a woman who had left the island and tried to make a life somewhere else, a person who was not supposed to be part of this, who was supposed to be safe, who was supposed to be free of the things that happened on the water.

The reading had not freed her. The reading had claimed her.

And now the storm was here, and the ferry could not come, and the island was cut off from the mainland the way it had always been cut off in bad weather, alone on its rock in the North Atlantic with nothing but the weather and the water and the old stories about what happened when the sea decided to keep what it had marked.

Dawn came gray and cold.

The storm had passed in the night, or rather it had settled in, the worst of the wind dropping to something that was merely dangerous rather than catastrophic, the rain becoming a steady drizzle that would last for days. Mara walked down to the harbor in the gray light. The boats were still there, still at their moorings, some of them knocked against each other in the chop, their paint scratched, their rigging tangled. The ferry dock was empty. No ferry.

The harbor was empty in a different way than usual. The early-morning fishermen were out—the ones who had stayed, the ones who had not run for home when the weather turned. They were out on the water now, working the morning tide, and the harbor was quiet except for the sound of the waves against the stones and the cry of the gulls fighting over the offal from last night’s catch.

Mara stood at the water’s edge and looked out toward the horizon. The mainland was there, somewhere beyond the gray water, but she couldn’t see it. The weather was too thick, the sky too low, the world contracted to the range of what she could touch and no more. She was alone on her island, the way she had always been alone, and the world beyond was gone.

The ferry would not come today. Perhaps not tomorrow either. The weather was set in, the kind of November weather that could last a week or a month, the kind that made the island what it was and kept people away or drove them away, the kind that had killed the eleven men in 1998, when the rescue boat couldn’t make the crossing and the keeper stood on the tower and watched the water take them one by one.

She had not been able to save them. She had lit the signal fires. She had sent up the rockets. She had done everything the protocols required, and it had not been enough, and she had learned that the sea did not care about protocols, did not care about effort, did not care about the people who watched from shore and wanted to help and could not.

The sea took what it took. It did not negotiate.

She turned and walked back up the slope from the harbor. The street was empty in the early light, the Inn dark, the cooperative building closed. She walked until she reached the shore path that led north along the coast, the path that followed the edge of the island toward the rocks where the boats had come in and the boats had gone down and the sea had given back what it wanted to give and kept the rest.

The path was slick with rain and covered in dead grass. She walked slowly, her boots finding the footing, her eyes on the ground and on the sea at the same time. The waves were high still, the swells coming in from the northwest, and she could hear them before she could see them—the crash and roar and thunder that was the sound the sea made when it was still angry, still unspent, still carrying the memory of the storm that had blown through in the night.

She walked until she reached the place where the path ended and the rocks began. This was where the boats came in, the sheltered cove that faced east, the place where the water was deep enough for the bigger vessels and the harbor master had once kept his office. The office was empty now. The dock was gone, washed out in a storm ten years ago and never rebuilt. But the boats still came here when they could, tying up to the mooring rings set into the granite, unloading their catch onto the stones where the women waited to carry it to the cooperative.

No boats were here now. The water was too rough, the surge too high, the rocks too slick with rain and spray. Anyone who tried to land here in this weather would be dashed against the stones, and everyone on the island knew it, and no one tried.

Mara stood on the rocks and watched the sea.

She did not know what she was waiting for. She did not know what she expected to see. The ferry could not come. The weather had stopped it, and Finlay had told her not to come, and Eilidh was either listening or she was not, and there was nothing left for Mara to do except wait and watch and see what the sea had decided.

The waves came in. They broke against the rocks. They pulled back and came again. The water was the color of the sky, gray and formless, and she could not see where the sea ended and the horizon began, could not see anything beyond the range of her own island, could not see what was happening on the mainland or in the harbor of the island itself or in any of the places where people lived their lives and made their choices and hoped for things they could not control.

She stood on the rocks and watched the sea.

And then she heard footsteps behind her.

She turned. Eilidh was walking down the path toward her, her coat pulled tight against the drizzle, her hair wet, her face pale in the gray light. She was alone. She was here.

She had not listened to her father’s message. Or she had listened and come anyway. Or she had never received it, the phone dead or the message undelivered, and she had gotten on the morning ferry that should not have run and somehow had, and now she was here, on the rocks, in the weather, where the reading said she should be.

The sea rose behind her. The waves came in higher than before.

Mara did not move. She did not call out. She watched Eilidh walk toward her across the wet stones, and she felt the wind on her face, and she tasted the salt in the air, and she knew that this was the moment the reading had been waiting for, the moment when the weather and the prophecy aligned, the moment when the sea would take what it had marked or when it would not.

The question the chapter needed to end on was not whether the reading would come true. The question was whether the reading could be wrong.

Mara stood on the shore and watched the sea rise.
