Chapter 13

The morning had brought her back to him, and Finlay Gunn had not slept. He had watched the clock on the mantel behind the bar, its second hand dragging across the face the way all second hands do, and he had listened to her footsteps overhead—the pause at the dresser, the creak of the bed frame, the shuffle that meant she was still there and had not left again before he could speak to her. He had made coffee three times. The first pot he drank. The second he poured away when it went cold. The third sat on the bar in front of him, untouched, cooling in the gray light that came through the Inn’s front windows.

The Aul迆 Inn was quiet at this hour. The ferry had docked at half seven and Eilidh had walked up the hill from the harbor with her bag over her shoulder, and Finlay had met her at the door, and they had embraced, and the embrace had lasted exactly as long as it needed to—long enough to prove something, short enough to exit cleanly. She had showered and changed and come downstairs, and now she sat at the corner table by the window where she had always sat, even as a girl, and she was eating toast with the deliberate focus of someone who did not want to talk.

He had spent the morning rehearsing. He had mapped it out on the back of a delivery schedule—a mental list, step by step, the way he planned the Inn’s weekly orders. He would tell her about the bottle. He would tell her about Mara Callaghan and the message. He would tell her what it said. He would then tell her what he had done about it: the ferry ticket, the booking on the Wednesday sailing, the three days between now and then. He had even called the mainland office to confirm the crossing and given them her booking reference, and the woman on the phone had been professionally bored, and he had hung up and felt, for exactly four minutes, like he had done something.

Eilidh looked up from her toast. “You’re hovering.”

“I’m not hovering.”

“You’ve come past the table four times since I sat down.”

“I was wiping it down.”

“The table’s been wiped.”

He stopped. He pulled out the chair across from her and sat. The legs scraped against the floorboards. Above them, the ceiling beams held the weight of the building the way they had held it for a hundred years, and Finlay had replaced three of them in his time as owner, and the new ones never quite matched the old ones in color, and he did not look up at them now because he did not want to think about the parts of things that did not match.

“There’s something I need to tell you,” he said.

“Dad.”

“Let me finish.”

She set her toast down. She folded her hands on the table the way she did when she was being careful, and he recognized the posture from the years after her mother died, when she would sit across from doctors and nod and ask the right questions and then walk out and cry where he could not see her. She had learned to hold herself still when she needed to.

Finlay told her about the morning. The walk to the lighthouse. The bottle in the rocks below the high-water mark. Mara’s hand in the water and the message that appeared in her skin, written in a hand she said was not her own. He told her what the message said.

Eilidh listened. She did not interrupt. Her face did the thing it always did when she was processing something—she went very still and very quiet, and the stillness was so complete that it frightened him more than any reaction would have. When he finished, she picked up her toast and ate another bite.

“Bottles in the ocean,” she said. “Messages in bottles. That’s what we’re doing here.”

“I know how it sounds.”

“You’re right. You know how it sounds.”

“The message names you, Eilidh.”

“I heard you.”

“The message names you as the sole survivor. It says the ship goes down. It says you live.”

“That part I didn’t hear. Tell me again?”

“It says you live.”

“Mm.” She chewed. Swallowed. Took a sip of tea from the cup beside her plate. “And Mara Callaghan believes this.”

“She’s lived by the sea for thirty years. She knows what the readings mean.”

“She knows what they meant. There’s a difference.”

“There’s not.”

“Dad.” She looked at him. Her eyes were her mother’s eyes, and he had never been able to look at them for long without feeling the thing he felt, the thing he had learned to carry by carrying it sideways, in the space beside the feeling rather than through it. “You’re a rational person. You are the most rational person I have ever met. You do not believe in prophecy.”

“I believe in patterns.”

“Patterns. Fine. Call it patterns. Patterns of what, exactly? Messages that wash up in bottles? That’s your pattern?”

“I believe in the sea,” he said. “I believe in what it does. And I believe that Mara Callaghan did not spend thirty years watching ships and counting bodies just to invent a joke.”

He said it harder than he meant to. The words came out with an edge that surprised them both. Eilidh sat back in her chair.

“Okay,” she said. “Okay. So what do you want me to do?”

The question landed the way he had planned for it to land—straight, clean, the way she asked questions she already knew the answers to. He had prepared for argument. He had prepared for disbelief, for mockery, for the particular dry tone she used when she was being superior about something she had decided in advance was beneath her. He had not prepared for this. The calm question. The clean surrender.

“I want you to leave,” he said. “I’ve booked you on the Wednesday ferry. That’s three days from now. The boat is scheduled to sail at noon, and I’ve already given them your name and the cabin preference.”

“You booked me.”

“I did.”

“Ahead of time.”

“Before you even got on the ferry from the mainland.”

Eilidh looked at him for a long moment. He could see her working through it, the calculation behind her eyes that was exactly the same calculation she used when the hospital called with news about her mother—the rapid assessment of what was true, what was false, what was possible, what had already been decided by someone who had decided it without her.

“You booked me on a boat,” she said.

“I did.”

“To leave the island.”

“Wednesday. Noon.”

“And you thought this was something I would agree to.”

“I hoped.”

“Hoped.” She turned the word over. “That you woke up this morning, called the ferry company, booked your daughter on a boat, and then waited at the door to see if she’d walk through it of her own accord. That’s what you hoped.”

“Yes.”

“That’s not a plan, Dad. That’s a fantasy.”

The word hit him. He felt it land in the center of his chest, not because it was wrong but because it was right, and the rightness of it was the thing he could not stand. He gripped the edge of the table.

“Your mother died,” he said.

The room changed. He could feel it change—a sudden stillness in the air, the way the Inn went quiet when someone opened a door that had been closed too long. Eilidh’s face closed. Not angry. Not hurt. Closed the way a door closes when the person on the other side has decided you are no longer worth the effort of letting in.

“Don’t,” she said.

“Your mother died, and I couldn’t do anything. I watched her go. I sat in that hospital and I watched her go, and there was nothing I could do, and I have—” He stopped. He pressed his hands flat against the table and breathed. “I have thought about that every single day since. I have thought about what I should have done, what I could have done, whether I should have fought harder or sooner or differently. And I don’t know. I will never know. But this—” He leaned forward. “This I can do something about.”

“Dad.”

“I couldn’t stop it for her. I can stop it for you.”

The words hung between them. Finlay heard them as they were—as the truest thing he had ever said, and as the thing that would hurt her most, and he said it anyway because he did not know how to say true things without saying them in ways that broke the people he was trying to protect.

Eilidh looked down at her hands. She was quiet for a long time. Outside, the harbor sounds drifted through the window—boats against their moorings, the clank of rope on metal, a gull complaining about something no one else could see. The morning was brightening. The gray was pulling back from the edges of the sky, and the harbor lights were going out one by one, and Finlay watched his daughter and waited.

“I’ll go,” she said.

He blinked. “You’ll—”

“Wednesday. The noon ferry. I’ll go.”

“You agree?”

“I didn’t say agree. I said I’ll go.”

“What’s the difference?”

“The difference is that I don’t believe any of this is real. I think Mara Callaghan found a bottle and a piece of paper and decided she wanted to feel important, and I think you have decided to believe her because believing her gives you something to do that isn’t sitting in this pub by yourself pretending you’re fine.” She picked up her toast again. “I’ll get on the boat because you’ve already booked it and because it will be easier than arguing, and because I don’t want to be here any longer than I have to. That’s not agreement. That’s exhaustion.”

He absorbed this. The logic was clean and it was hers, and he recognized it because it was the same logic she had used to leave the island the first time—exhaustion as reason, the weariness that came from carrying something you could not set down. He had felt it himself, in the months after the funeral. He had felt it every time he looked at her across this table and saw her mother’s face in her daughter’s face and could not say the thing he needed to say.

“At least get on the boat,” he said. “Just get on the boat.”

“I’m getting on the boat.”

“And stay away from the harbor. Until Wednesday. Stay away from the water.”

Eilidh laughed at this. It was not a cruel laugh. It was the laugh of someone who has been told something so precise that it loops back around to being absurd. She shook her head.

“Stay away from the harbor,” she repeated.

“Just until Wednesday.”

“Dad. The harbor is where the boats are. The harbor is the only part of this island that’s not a hill or a bog. You’re asking me to stay away from the harbor.”

“Stay on the hill. Stay in the village. Just stay dry.”

“I’ll stay dry.”

“Promise me.”

She looked at him. The humor was gone from her face. What was left was something he could not name—a tiredness that was more than the tiredness of travel, older than the morning, deeper than the argument they had just had. She pressed her hand flat against the table the way he had, and she held it there, and she did not look away from him.

“I’ll stay dry,” she said. “I’ll get on the boat. I’ll go back to Edinburgh and I’ll call you when I get there and you’ll pick up on the first ring because that’s what you do. And then you’ll go back to running this pub and pretending you don’t need anyone, and I’ll go back to not being here, and we’ll keep doing that until one of us is dead, and that’s just what it is. But I’ll get on the boat. I promise.”

He wanted to say something. He wanted to tell her that it was not pretended, that he did need someone, that he had needed her mother and then he had needed her and she had left and he had learned to need quietly, the way you learn to walk with a stone in your shoe. But he did not say any of this. He nodded. He stood. He took her plate and her cup and he carried them behind the bar and he set them in the sink and he turned on the water and he stood there with his hands under the stream and he breathed.

When he turned back, she was standing by the window. She had her bag over her shoulder again. She was not leaving—not yet—but she had picked it up the way she always picked it up when she was about to decide something, and Finlay watched her hold it and set it down and hold it again.

“Three days,” he said.

“Three days.”

“And then you’re gone.”

“And then I’m gone.” She paused. “Dad. What do you think is going to happen to the boat?”

He did not answer. He did not know what he thought was going to happen. He knew only what Mara had told him and what the message had said, and he had built his plan on the foundation of that knowing, and the foundation was not solid—it was the kind of foundation you built on sand, the kind you built in places where the ground looked solid until you put weight on it and then it gave. But it was what he had. He had a ticket. He had a plan. He had three days.

“I think you’re going to be fine,” he said.

She nodded. She did not smile. She picked up her bag and she walked to the door and she stopped with her hand on the latch and she looked back at him, and her face was the face of someone who had made a decision she did not believe in and was going to follow through on it anyway.

“Mara Callaghan,” she said. “What was she like? Before.”

“Before?”

“When she was still the keeper. When she was up in the tower every night. You must have seen her.”

Finlay thought about this. He thought about Mara Callaghan in the years before the automation, when she came down to the harbor once a week for supplies and he would see her on the street and she would nod and he would nod and that was all. He thought about her in the tower, visible at night as a point of light that moved when the mechanism turned, and he thought about the ships that passed that light, and he thought about the ones that did not come back.

“She kept to herself,” he said. “Like she does now. She was—” He stopped. He was going to say careful, but careful was not the word. Mara Callaghan had been precise. She had been exact in the way of people who had learned that the sea did not forgive imprecision. “She knew things,” he said. “She knew how to read the weather. She knew how to read the water. She knew things that weren’t in any book.”

“She still knows things that aren’t in any book.”

“That’s what I’m afraid of.”

Eilidh opened the door. The morning air came in, cold and salt, carrying the sound of the harbor and the distant complaint of gulls. She stepped out into it. She did not look back.

Finlay stood behind the bar. He wiped it down. The wood was old, scarred by glasses and knives and the marks of decades of people who had stood where he stood and said what needed to be said or not said it, and he moved the cloth in slow circles and he did not think about the Wednesday ferry because he had done everything he could about the Wednesday ferry and there was nothing left but waiting.

The door opened again.

Mara Callaghan stood in the doorway, framed by the light from outside, and she looked at him the way she always looked at him—directly, without warmth, without coldness, the way a person looks at a thing they are measuring.

“She’s agreed,” he said.

Mara did not move from the doorway. She stood with her hand on the frame and her coat still buttoned to the throat, and she was watching him the way she watched the sea when it was calm—too calm, calmer than it should be, the kind of calm that meant something was gathering.

“I know,” she said.

“How do you know?”

“Because I watched her from the hill. I saw her come off the ferry. I saw her come up here.” She stepped inside. She closed the door behind her. The sound of the harbor faded. “The ferry for Wednesday is delayed.”

“Delayed?”

“Weather. They’re saying the system is moving in faster than expected. They’re reviewing the schedule.”

Finlay felt the floor shift beneath him—not literally, not yet, but in the way that floors do when you have built your life on a plan and the plan has just changed its shape. “Wednesday’s three days away.”

“They’re looking at it now. The boat may not sail until Friday. Maybe later.”

“Then she stays until it goes. She stays dry. She stays away from the harbor.”

Mara said nothing. She walked to the window and she looked out at it—the harbor, the boats, the water that sat calm and flat in the morning light, the surface absolutely still in the way that water is still only when it is about not to be.

“The sea knows,” she said.

“The sea doesn’t know anything. It’s water. It moves because gravity tells it to.”

“The reading names her. She’s marked. Ships that should have brought her home, boats that should have carried her—she’s marked, Finlay. The sea doesn’t forget what it has claimed.”

“It hasn’t claimed her yet.”

“It will try.”

He stared at her. “You told me to get her off the island.”

“I told you what the message said.”

“You said—”

“I told you what the message said, and I told you what the readings mean, and I told you what the survivor clause does. I did not tell you it would work. I did not tell you that getting her on a boat would get her off. Because it doesn’t work that way. It never has.”

The silence that followed was long and hard, the kind of silence that has weight to it, the kind that pushes back when you try to fill it. Finlay heard the clock on the mantel. He heard the gull outside. He heard his own pulse in his ears, the thing he heard when he was afraid and could not admit it.

“You’re telling me,” he said, “that it doesn’t matter what I do.”

“I’m telling you what it costs.”

“What’s the difference?”

Mara turned from the window. She looked at him, and in her face he saw the thing he had been refusing to see since she walked into his bar that morning—the thing he had been refusing to see since the hospital, since the funeral, since the morning he woke up and his wife was gone and he was still there and the distance between those two facts was a distance he could not cross.

“The difference,” she said, “is that you can still choose.”

She did not say anything else. She walked to the door. She opened it, and the salt air rushed in, and she stepped out into it, and the door swung shut behind her, and Finlay Gunn stood alone in his bar with his hands on the edge of the counter and the harbor visible through the window and the water lying flat and still and patient under the morning sky.

Wednesday. The noon ferry. She had agreed. She had promised.

But the ferry was delayed, and the sea was calm, and somewhere in the harbor the boats rocked at their moorings, and the water held its breath, and Finlay Gunn stood with his hands on the counter and the morning light coming in through the windows, and he did not know what the sea was going to do, and he did not know what he was going to do, and he stood there, and the harbor waited.
