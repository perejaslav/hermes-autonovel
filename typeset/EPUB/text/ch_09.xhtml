Chapter 9

The Inn was quiet. The League meeting had broken up an hour ago, and Finlay had stayed behind to clean the back room, stacking chairs against the wall with the mechanical care of a man who had done it every week for years. Mara watched him from the doorway before she entered. His back was to her. He moved slowly, and she noticed what she had not noticed before—how thin he had gone since his wife died, how the shape of him seemed to have collapsed inward like a building whose foundation had given way.

She stepped inside. The smell of the Inn reached her: stale beer, peat smoke, the particular mustiness of a room that was never quite aired out. The fire was low, embers in the grate, and the light came from a single lamp on the bar.

“Finlay.”

He turned. Surprise crossed his face, then settled into something more cautious.

“Mara. The meeting’s over.”

“I know.”

“You weren’t on the agenda anyway.”

“No.”

He set down the chair he was carrying. He wiped his hands on his trousers—a habit, she remembered, from when they were younger and he was building the business. He had always been wiping his hands of something.

“What is it, then?”

She crossed the room. Took the chair nearest the bar, the one that sat in the same spot it always sat, the one that had held generations of islanders who came to drink and talk and forget they were on a rock in the North Atlantic. She sat. He stayed standing.

“You should sit down,” she said.

“I’ve been sitting down for fifteen years.”

It was not what she expected him to say. She let the silence hold for a moment, then said: “I’ve found a reading.”

Finlay went still. Not the stillness of shock—something more practiced. The stillness of a man who had learned not to show what he felt.

“A reading.”

“A bottle. Found it this morning below the light. The message was there.”

“Readings are nonsense.”

“Every reading in the Registry has come true.”

“The Registry.” He said it like he was naming a disease. “The clerks who come out from the mainland and ask questions no one wants to answer. The files they keep on people who’ve done nothing wrong except be in the wrong place when a bottle washes up.” He shook his head. “I’ve seen what happens to the people the clerks visit. The questions follow them. The neighbors look at them different. They become something in the island’s mind, something marked, and it never ends well.”

“I’m not talking about the clerks.”

“You’re talking about what they believe. What they write down in their books and send back to Edinburgh where someone files it away and calls it knowledge.” He leaned against the bar. “I know what readings are, Mara. I’ve heard the stories. I’ve heard what the Daughters say and what the Cairn families whisper and what the old people tell the children to keep them from swimming at the wrong coves. And I also know that most bottles that wash up don’t have messages in them. Most bottles are just bottles. And the ones that do have messages—” He stopped.

“The ones that do have messages?”

“Most people destroy them.”

“I know.”

“Then you know what happens when they don’t.”

Mara said nothing. She had learned, over thirty years, that some silences were more honest than words. She sat with her hands in her lap, the left one resting where the name had appeared, and she waited.

Finlay watched her. His face was the face she had seen in the harbor years ago, when the rescue boat came back empty and the families were waiting, when the men who went out to look for the bodies came back with nothing and someone had to stand on the dock and tell the women it was over. He had been the one to tell them, that time. He had been twenty-eight years old and newly married to a woman who would later become his wife, and he had stood on the dock and told eleven families that their men were gone.

She had watched him do it. She had been in the tower, keeping the light, and when the rescue boat came back she had climbed down and walked to the harbor because someone had to witness. She had watched him speak, watched the way his voice stayed steady even as his hands shook, watched the women receive the news and go still in the way that people go still when the world has tilted and they are trying to find a new footing.

That was the face he wore now. She recognized it. She had not expected him to wear it so quickly.

“Where is it?” he said.

She reached into her coat pocket and drew out the bottle. Green glass, caught between the rocks, now dry and ordinary-looking. She set it on the bar between them. The room was quiet enough that she could hear the fire settling, the tick of embers shifting in the grate.

Finlay looked at the bottle. He did not touch it.

“Show me,” he said.

She opened her hand. The name was still there—EILIDH GUNN, written in a hand that was hers and was not hers, that had formed itself without her permission and would not fade. She held her hand out, palm up, and let him see.

He stared at it. She watched his face change—watched the disbelief move across it like weather, like the shadow of a cloud passing over water. He looked at the name. He looked at her. He looked at the bottle. He looked at the name again.

“This isn’t possible.”

“The readings don’t care about possible.”

“This is—” He stopped. Started again. “You’re saying someone wrote my daughter’s name. In a bottle. In your hand.”

“The message came to me. I did not choose it.”

“But the words. The name. That was you. Your hand. Your writing.”

“Yes.”

“And you believe—” He shook his head. “No. No. I don’t accept this.”

“Finlay.”

“Readings are nonsense. Bottles wash up. Messages appear. And someone—a clerk, a scholar, someone who makes their living off other people’s fear—they write it down and they call it prophecy and they build systems out of coincidences. I’ve heard this my whole life. My grandmother believed. My mother believed. They saw patterns in everything, saw omens in every storm, saw the hand of something larger in every death. And it never made them wiser. It never made them safer. It just made them afraid.”

“I found the bottle.”

“You found a bottle.”

“I found a bottle with a message that named your daughter.”

“And what does that mean? That she might drown? That a ship might sink? That something might happen somewhere, someday, to someone who shares a name with her?” He was talking faster now, his voice climbing, the way it did when he was working himself up to a place he couldn’t come back from. “People drown. Ships sink. Things happen. The world is dangerous. That’s not prophecy, Mara. That’s Tuesday.”

“The message says she’s the only survivor.”

The words landed. She watched them land. She watched him take them in and saw the way his face changed, saw the way the color went out of it, saw the way his jaw tightened as though he were bracing for something he couldn’t name.

“The only survivor of what?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“You don’t know.”

“The readings don’t come with schedules. They name who survives. They don’t name when, or where, or how.”

“But Eilidh—my daughter—she’s in Edinburgh. She’s been in Edinburgh for eight years. She’s got a job and a flat and a life and she hasn’t been on a boat since she was eighteen years old and swore she’d never come back to this island.” He was gripping the edge of the bar now, his knuckles white against the dark wood. “She’s not going to drown. She doesn’t go near the water. She wouldn’t—”

“I know.”

“Then what are you telling me this for?”

Mara looked at him. He was fifty-eight years old. He had run the Inn for thirty years. He had buried his wife and raised his daughter and fought for the ferry and argued with the mainland and kept the island’s heart beating through sheer stubbornness and care. He was not a man who believed in prophecy because belief would mean his life was already determined, and he could not afford to believe that. He had too much to protect.

“I found the bottle,” she said. “The message named her. And the readings don’t care where she is, Finlay. The readings mark who survives. The sea brings the wreck to them, or brings them to the wreck. I’ve seen it. I’ve watched it happen. You can’t outrun a reading.”

“Watched it happen.” His voice was flat. “You’ve watched someone survive a wreck because a reading told you they would?”

“No. I’ve watched the ones who didn’t know. The ones who went out not knowing they were marked, and the sea took them anyway, and afterward someone found the bottle and read the message and understood what had happened. The readings don’t fail, Finlay. They come true. Every one of them. The only question is whether anyone knows in time to try.”

“To try what?”

“I don’t know yet.”

He laughed. It was not a good laugh. It was the sound of a man who had reached the end of his patience and found nothing there.

“You don’t know. You don’t know what the message means. You don’t know when it will happen. You don’t know where, or how, or what it will take to stop it. And you’re standing in my bar telling me my daughter is going to die.”

“I’m telling you she is marked. I’m telling you the sea has claimed her. And I’m telling you that if there’s a way to change that, I need to find it.”

“And why you? Why did you have to be the one to find the bottle? Why couldn’t it wash up somewhere else, find someone else, leave someone else to deal with it?” He was pressing his palms flat against the bar now, leaning forward, his whole body tense. “Why you, Mara?”

The question hung in the air. She could have answered it. She could have told him about the tower and the years and the eleven men she had watched drown because the rescue came too late. She could have told him about the faces she saw when she closed her eyes, the names she could not say without the names becoming real. She could have told him she was not chosen, that the bottle had found her the way the sea finds its own, that she had been marked by the water long before she ever found the message.

She said none of this.

“Because I was the one standing there,” she said.

Finlay stared at her. His breathing was hard, visible in the way his chest moved against his shirt. The fire crackled behind them. Outside, somewhere, a dog barked—the sound traveling across the harbor, carrying the ordinary noise of the island into this room where nothing was ordinary anymore.

“Where is she?” he said.

“Edinburgh.”

“Then she’s safe.”

“The reading doesn’t care where she is.”

“Edinburgh is three hundred miles from the sea.”

“Some of the people in the Registry were nowhere near water when the wreck happened. The message finds them. The sea finds them.” Mara paused. “I don’t know how it works. I know that it does.”

Finlay turned away. He picked up the bottle from the bar—held it in his hand, turning it, looking at the glass as though the answers might be hidden in its green depth. He looked at the cork, still sealed, still holding whatever small breath the message needed to stay alive. He looked at the label, or what was left of it, the original markings worn away by salt and time.

“My daughter,” he said quietly.

“Yes.”

“She left because of me.”

“I know.”

“She thinks I smothered her. Her mother—the way she died—we never talked about it. I didn’t know how to talk about it. I just tried to keep Eilidh safe. Keep her here. Keep her close. And she wanted out. She wanted to be somewhere the sea couldn’t reach her. She wanted to be away from all of it—the island, the stories, the way the past sits on everything here like a weight.” He set the bottle down. “She wanted to be free of me.”

“And?”

“And I called her last week. After you found the bottle. After you told me.” He was not looking at her now. He was looking at the fire, or through it, at something she couldn’t see. “She didn’t answer. I left a message. Just—hello, Eilidh, it’s your father, call me when you can.” He swallowed. “She hasn’t called back.”

“Finlay.”

“I know what you’re going to say.”

“You’re going to need to bring her home.”

“I know.”

“And you’re going to need to tell her why.”

He turned then, and faced her, and she saw what she had seen in 1998—the face of a man watching the sea take someone. He was not the same man he had been then. The years had worn him down, carved new lines into his face, taken some of the strength out of his shoulders and some of the certainty out of his eyes. But the face was the same. The fear was the same. The terrible knowledge that something was coming that he could not stop.

“I’ll call her again,” he said.

“And tell her what?”

“What you told me. That a reading came. That my name is in it.” He stopped. Corrected himself. “Her name. That she’s marked. That she needs to come home.”

“And if she doesn’t believe you?”

Finlay looked at the bottle on the bar. He looked at Mara’s hand, still open, still bearing the name that would not fade. He looked at the fire, and the empty chairs, and the room that had held so many conversations but had never held one like this.

“Then I’ll drive to Edinburgh and drag her back myself,” he said. “I don’t care what she thinks of me. I don’t care if she never speaks to me again. If there’s a chance—any chance—that knowing this changes what happens, I have to take it.”

Mara nodded. She did not tell him what she suspected—that knowing would not be enough. That the readings had their own logic, their own weight, their own terrible momentum. That the sea did not negotiate and the marked did not escape by simply being warned.

She did not tell him that she had seen, in the moment when the message appeared in her hand, something else too. A second line of text, faint, almost invisible, that had vanished before she could read it. She did not know what it meant. She did not know if it was part of the reading or part of her own fear.

She would tell him. Later. When she understood it herself.

“For now,” she said, “call her. Tell her what you know. And tell her I’m sorry.”

“Sorry for what?”

Mara stood. The chair scraped against the floor, the sound loud in the quiet room. She took the bottle and put it back in her pocket, felt its weight against her hip, the small solid presence of the thing that had started all of this.

“For what comes next,” she said. “For whatever we have to do to try.”

She walked to the door. Stopped. Turned back.

“Finlay.”

“Yes?”

“The meeting tonight. The League. What did you decide?”

He was still standing behind the bar, his hands flat on the wood, his face carrying the weight of everything she had told him. He blinked, as though the question had come from very far away.

“We voted. Two more ferries a week. If we can get the funding.”

“And if you can’t?”

“Then we keep fighting. We always do.” He almost smiled. It did not reach his eyes. “The ferry doesn’t matter anyway. Not this week. This week I have a daughter to call.”

Mara opened the door. The night was cold, the air carrying the salt and damp that never quite left the island. The harbor was visible below, the boats rocking on their moorings, the dark water keeping its secrets.

She stepped out into it.

Behind her, she heard Finlay pick up the phone.

She walked down toward the harbor, past the Inn, past the closed post office, past the cooperative building where the looms sat silent. The water was black and still, and she could see the reflection of the stars, and somewhere out there beyond the horizon the sea was waiting, and the wreck was coming, and the three weeks had begun to count down.

She reached the shore and stopped. The rocks were slick beneath her boots. The tide was rising, slow and inevitable, and she stood there and listened to the water talk to itself in the dark, and she thought about Finlay calling his daughter, and she thought about the name on her hand, and she thought about the second line of text she had not yet told anyone about.

She did not know what it said.

She did not know if she wanted to know.

The sea waited.

Mara waited with it.

She had three weeks. She had a bottle and a name and a man who would do anything to save his daughter. She had Agnes and the rituals and the cove that bore a dead woman’s name.

She had what the sea had not taken yet.

She turned and walked up from the shore, toward the path that led to her cottage, and the question followed her all the way home: Will Finlay help or hinder?

She did not have an answer.

The sea did.
