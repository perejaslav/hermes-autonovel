Chapter 16

The phone call was three days ago. Three nights she had not slept without seeing the harbor.

She had not told her father about the dreams. She had not told anyone. What would she say? I’m dreaming about boats that shouldn’t be here. I wake up and I can still see them, the way they come into the harbor wrong, the way the water takes them. Her father would say it was grief. Her father would say she was tired. Her father would say a great many things and none of them would be the truth, because her father did not know how to say the truth about anything except the Inn and the weather and the ferry schedule.

She walked down to the harbor in the morning because she did not know where else to go.

The air was cold enough to taste. November on Torran—she had forgotten what it felt like in her lungs, this particular sharpness that was not quite frost and not quite salt but something in between, something that made her think of her childhood and then made her stop thinking about her childhood. The village was waking around her. Smoke rose from the Inn’s chimney. A gull cried somewhere above the harbor, the sound flat and purposeful, the way gulls sounded when they were claiming something.

She did not go to the Inn. She walked past it, down the track that led to the rocks, and she was not thinking about where she was going until she was already there.

Mara was sitting on the flat stone where the boats moored closest to shore. She was not watching the boats. She was not watching anything. She was sitting with her hands in her lap and her face turned toward the water, and she looked like a woman who had been sitting there so long the island had stopped noticing her.

Eilidh did not know why she sat down.

She just did. One moment she was standing on the track, the next she was lowering herself onto the stone beside Mara, her legs folding under her without her permission. The rock was damp. Her boots were not meant for this—she had worn her city boots, the ones with the flat heels that slid on wet stone, and she had to brace her feet against the edge to keep from sliding toward the water.

Mara did not look at her.

Eilidh waited. The harbor stretched below them, the boats moored in their rows, the water the color of slate. The light was thin this morning, the sun somewhere behind the clouds that had been sitting on the horizon since she arrived, and the harbor looked the way it always looked in November—gray and patient and waiting.

“I’m dreaming,” Eilidh said.

The words came out before she had decided to say them. She had not meant to say them. She had come down here to sit, not to confess, not to anything, but the words were out now and she could not take them back.

Mara turned her head. Just slightly. Just enough that Eilidh could see her profile against the gray sky.

“About what?”

“The harbor.” Eilidh’s voice sounded strange in her own ears. Flat. Not like her voice at all. “Boats coming in that shouldn’t float.”

She waited for Mara to ask a question. To laugh, maybe, or to say something practical about dreams being dreams and the mind processing what the mind processed. But Mara did not laugh. Mara did not say anything. She just looked at Eilidh with those eyes that had seen too much and knew too much and had stopped expecting anyone to believe them.

“The reading is not about knowing,” Mara said. “It’s about what you do with the knowing.”

Eilidh shook her head. “What does that mean?”

“It means the sea is coming. It means you can run or you can stay.”

The sea is coming. Eilidh felt something cold move through her chest, not her heart but something deeper, something that had nothing to do with the weather or the water or anything she could name. She thought of the boats in her dreams. She thought of the way they came into the harbor wrong, the way the water took them, the way she stood on the rocks and watched and could not move.

She thought: How does she know.

She thought: What does she know.

She thought: I should not have come here.

“You don’t understand,” she said. Her voice came out wrong, too loud for the quiet morning, too sharp. “I came home because my father called me. I didn’t—I wasn’t going to stay. I’m not staying. I have a job. I have a flat. I have a life.”

“I know.”

“You don’t know anything about my life.”

“I know what the message said.”

The words hung in the air between them. Eilidh opened her mouth to argue, to say something about messages in bottles being nonsense, about prophecy being what people believed in when they did not have the courage to face reality. But she looked at Mara’s face and she did not say any of that. Mara was not looking at her. Mara was looking at the harbor, at the boats, at the water that was the color of slate.

“Message,” Eilidh said. The word tasted like rust in her mouth.

“You came here to tell someone about the dreams. You sat down without knowing why.” Mara’s voice was quiet. Not kind—Mara did not say kind things—but not unkind either. Just true. “The dreams are not nothing. They are never nothing.”

“Then what are they?”

Mara was silent for a long moment. The harbor stretched below them. A boat rocked at its mooring, the motion slow and patient.

“They are the sea telling you what it has claimed.”

Eilidh stood up. She did not decide to stand up; her legs simply unfolded and she was on her feet, and she was looking down at Mara, and she did not know what she was feeling except that it was too big for her body.

“I need to think,” she said.

She walked back up the track toward the village. Her legs felt strange, not quite under her, and she had to watch her feet to keep from stumbling. The harbor fell away behind her. The Inn came into view ahead, its white walls and its smoke-stained chimney, and she knew her father was inside, probably standing at the bar doing the thing he did with the papers and the schedules, pretending the world was something that could be managed with the right list and the right plan and the right number of ferry runs.

She did not go inside.

She stopped in the middle of the street, halfway between the harbor and the Inn, and she stood there with the cold air on her face and the gray light in her eyes and the words Mara had said turning over and over in her head. The sea is coming. It means you can run or you can stay.

She could run. She could call the mainland office and say she needed emergency leave. She could get on the ferry tomorrow and go back to her flat and her job and her life that was not here and had never been here and did not have to be here. She could run, and the dreams would stop, and she would never have to think about boats that shouldn’t float again.

She could stay. She could go into the Inn and talk to her father and make him explain what message, what reading, what any of this was supposed to mean. She could stay and find out whether Mara was right about what the sea had claimed, or whether Mara was wrong, or whether it did not matter whether she was right or wrong because the boats came anyway and the water took them anyway and standing on the rocks and watching was all anyone could ever do.

The street was empty. The Inn sat in front of her, patient and solid and full of things she did not want to face. Behind her, the harbor spread out toward the horizon, the boats at their moorings, the lighthouse standing on its rock.

The light had not turned on yet. The sun was still behind the clouds.

Eilidh stood in the middle of the street and did not go inside.
