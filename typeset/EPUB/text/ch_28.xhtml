Chapter 28

The fire had burned down to embers when she woke. Not the deep sleep of exhaustion this time, but the ordinary waking of a body that had rested enough. Mara lay still and listened to the silence. The wind had dropped. The sea was murmuring the way it did when it was tired too.

She got up. The floorboards were cold under her feet. She put on her boots without thinking, pulled her coat from the hook by the door, and went outside.

The light came gray through the east, the way it always did in the morning before the sun decided whether to show itself. The world was quiet. The kind of quiet that used to frighten her, years ago, when the silence after a storm meant she had to go down to the shore and count.

She didn’t count anymore. Not like that. Not with dread.

The path to the harbor was the same path she had walked for fifteen years. She knew every stone, every place where the turf dipped, every stretch where the heather had crept over the edge and onto the track. Her body knew it well enough that her mind could float, could watch the sky, could notice the way the light was changing.

Four boats in the harbor. She had expected three. The McLeod boat was back—the one with the cracked hull, the one that had sat on the slip for two years waiting for parts that never came. Someone had found the parts. Someone had done the work. The boat sat in the water with its new paint still bright, looking like a thing that intended to stay.

She counted them again to be sure. Once for habit. Twice because she could.

Four.

The harbor was quiet. A few of the fishing crews were already moving, checking lines, preparing for the day. Finlay would be in the Inn, probably. Making the tea he made every morning, thinking the thoughts he thought every morning. She had not seen him since the night on the headland, since he had understood that his daughter was coming back, since she had watched something move behind his eyes that she could not name.

She started up the path to the headland, the way she always did, and she saw him at the far end of the harbor wall. He was standing with his hands in his pockets, looking at the water. Looking at the boats. Looking at nothing.

He saw her. He raised his hand.

She raised hers.

He did not come to her. She did not go to him. The wave hung between them for a moment, a gesture that said: I see you. I know what you know. I will not bother you with it today.

She dropped her hand. He dropped his. They each turned back to their own directions, and that was enough.

The path to Ellen’s Cove was longer, winding down through the moor where the grass grew thin and the rocks showed through. The cove was sheltered from the north wind, which meant it was always a few degrees warmer than it should have been, which meant the air there felt wrong, heavy and still. Mara had avoided it for years. She did not avoid it now.

Agnes was sitting on the flat stone at the cove’s edge, the one the Daughters used for their observances. She was not doing anything visible. Just sitting. Watching the water.

Mara stood at the top of the path and lifted her hand.

Agnes looked up. She lifted hers in return.

They did not speak. They did not need to. Agnes knew what Mara knew. Agnes knew what the night had cost, what the light had taken, what the names had meant. They had stood together at the edge of the water while the eleven were remembered, and that standing had been enough.

Mara let her hand fall. Agnes turned back to the cove. The distance between them was the right distance. It had always been the right distance.

The lighthouse stood on the headland, sixty-two feet of granite and iron, the same as it had been for a hundred and thirty years. The light was turning. She could hear the mechanism from here, the slow hum of gears that had been turning since before she was born, that would keep turning after she was gone. It did not need her anymore. It had never really needed her. She had only been the one who was there.

The light turned. The beam swept across the water, across the rocks, across the harbor where four boats sat waiting for the day. It passed over the cove where Agnes sat. It passed over the headland where Finlay had been standing. It passed over everything the island was and everything it had been and everything it would be, and it did not stop, because that was not what light did.

Mara watched it turn.

She had thought, once, that the light was hers. That the tower was hers. That the keeping was a thing she owned because she had given it so much of her life. She had been wrong. The light was the light. The tower was the tower. She had only been the hands that tended them, and now other hands tended them, and that was right. That was as it should be.

She turned and walked back to the cottage.

The fire wanted building. She knelt by the hearth and stacked the peat, coaxed the embers with the bellows until the new fuel caught. The smoke rose and curled and filled the small space with warmth. She stood and put the kettle on the iron hook over the flames.

The cottage smelled of salt and smoke. It always had. The rosemary on the windowsill was the same rosemary she had planted years ago, when the cottage was still new to her and she thought she could make it something other than what it was. It had not become something else. It had become exactly what it was, and she had become part of it, and the two of them fit together now the way a harbor fits the boats that shelter there.

She poured the water. She waited for the tea to steep. She poured it into the cup that had belonged to her grandmother, the one with the chip on the rim that she always felt but never looked for anymore.

The eleven were with her. They had been with her since the night in the tower, since she had said their names and meant them. She did not see their faces anymore, or she saw them differently now—not as the faces of the dead but as the faces of the witnessed. They had been there. They had lived. They had gone into the water, and she had seen them go, and that seeing was not a wound anymore. It was a keeping. It was what she owed them.

She drank her tea. She sat by the window and watched the light move across the harbor.

The boats counted themselves. Four. One new. One repaired. One that had decided to stay.

Finlay was out there somewhere, doing the things Finlay did. Making tea. Managing the Inn. Thinking about his daughter on the mainland, safe on solid ground, building a life that had nothing to do with the sea. He did not need to believe anymore. He knew.

Agnes was at the cove, keeping her watch, doing the work that the Daughters had always done. Honoring what could not be changed. Preparing for what might. She knew the cost of the readings. She had paid it. She would help anyone who needed help, and she would not ask for thanks, and she would not explain herself to anyone who did not already understand.

The light turned. The mechanism hummed. The world went on.

Mara put down her cup. She looked at the eleven chairs around the table that was not a table, at the eleven names she had written on the page she had folded and put in her pocket and taken to the tower. She did not need the paper anymore. She had said the names. They were heard. They were kept.

She stood. She stretched. She put the cup in the basin by the door.

Outside, the day was deciding to be ordinary. The clouds were thin enough to let through some sun. The wind was from the west, which meant fair weather. The boats would go out, and they would come back, and the island would be the island for another day.

Mara put on her coat. She opened the door.

She was not alone. She would never be alone again, not the way she had been alone before. The eleven were with her. The light was with her. The island was with her, in all its stubborn, dying, refusing-to-die persistence. She was part of it. She had always been part of it. She had just remembered how to feel it.

She walked down the path toward the harbor. Not to count. Not to watch. Just to walk. Just to be in the world that was still there, still moving, still turning toward whatever came next.

The light turned behind her. The mechanism hummed. The tea cooled on the windowsill of the cottage that was her home, and would be, and had always been, and she was not afraid of the silence anymore because the silence was not empty.

It was full of names. It was full of keeping.

It was full of what witness owes.

Mara Callaghan walked down to the harbor, and the boats counted themselves, and the day was ordinary, and that was enough. That was more than enough. That was everything she had thought she lost and found again in the space of a single night, in the speaking of eleven names, in the turning of a light that did not need her but let her stay anyway.

She waved to Finlay across the harbor.

He waved back.

The light turned. The sea murmured. The island held.
