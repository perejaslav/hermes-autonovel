Chapter 8

The light from the keeper’s cottage was a pale smear behind her when she reached the path that led down to Ellen’s Cove. The afternoon had thinned into evening, and the sky was the color of wet slate, and the wind came off the water with the patience it always had at this hour—not cold yet, but not warm either, the kind of air that asked what you were doing out here and did not expect an answer.

Agnes was waiting at the fork where the path split, one branch going to the harbor and one going to the rocks. She was dressed for walking, her coat buttoned to the throat, a cloth bag over her shoulder that held the things she would need. She did not ask if Mara was ready. She turned and began the descent.

The path to Ellen’s Cove was narrow and steep, cut into the hillside by feet that had worn it smooth over generations. Mara knew every stone of it—had walked it in the dark, in the rain, in the light that came off the water when the sun dropped below the horizon and the sea turned the color of copper before it went black. She knew the place where the roots of an elder tree had cracked the stone, and the place where the path turned sharp around a boulder that had come down in some storm no one remembered, and the place where you had to step over a gap that was wider than it looked. She had walked this path as a girl, and as a woman who had kept the light, and as a woman who kept nothing now but the weight of what she knew.

Agnes walked ahead, her pace unhurried. She did not look back.

The cove opened below them like a wound in the rock—not a dramatic opening, not a classic inlet with a beach and a cliff, but a shallow curve in the granite where the land had simply given way to the water over centuries. The water here was not deep. Waist-high thirty feet out, according to the fishermen who had measured it and then stopped coming here. The floor was sand and stone, clean and pale, and the light that came through it at certain angles made the bottom look closer than it was.

Mara had not been to Ellen’s Cove in eleven years. The last time she had walked this path, it had been to stand at the water’s edge and watch the tide come in while she decided whether to go back to the cottage or walk into the water. She had chosen the cottage. That time.

The evening light was flat and even, the kind that made everything look the same distance away. The air here was different from the air on the other side of the hill—still, somehow, even when the wind was up everywhere else. The sea did not fight here. It waited.

“That was Ellen’s spot,” Agnes said, nodding toward a flat stone at the water’s edge, half-submerged at high tide. “She used to sit there and watch for her father’s ship coming in from the north.”

“I know.”

“You were young.”

“Young enough.”

Agnes did not push it. She walked to the edge of the water and set down her bag and began to unpack it. Salt in a cloth wrap, tied with twine. A small brazier, iron, the kind that fishermen used to warm their hands on cold mornings. Charcoal. A box of matches. A piece of cloth that looked old, stained in patches that might have been rust or might have been something else. A small knife with a bone handle.

Mara stood a few feet back and watched.

“The blessing of boats,” Agnes said. “It is not what people think.”

“What do they think?”

“That it protects them.” Agnes struck a match, set the charcoal in the brazier, cupped her hand around the flame until it caught. “The blessing asks the sea to remember the boat. To know it. To treat it as something that belongs here, not something that wandered in from somewhere else.”

“Does it work?”

“It works the way all the old ways work.” Agnes poured salt into her palm, held it out over the water. The words she spoke were in Gaelic—not the tourist Gaelic you heard on the radio, but the older kind, the kind that came from the throat and the chest and made the sounds that English could not hold. The salt fell into the water and dissolved and the sea did not change, but the air seemed to thicken, just for a moment, like the world was listening.

Mara felt her breath catch.

“The blessing does not stop the storm,” Agnes said. “The storm will come whether the boat is blessed or not. But the blessed boat is known. The sea knows it. And sometimes—” She stopped. Reached for another handful of salt. “Sometimes knowing is enough.”

“Enough for what?”

“For the fishermen to feel they have a chance. For the widows to sleep the night before their men go out. For the men to go out without feeling like they are asking the sea to take them.” She turned to face Mara. “The rituals are not for the sea. They are for us.”

Mara did not answer. She was looking at the water, which held no reflection of the sky, which was just dark now with the last of the light going out of it.

“The naming of wrecks,” Agnes said. “Come here.”

She led Mara to the far edge of the cove, where the rocks rose up and the water was shallow enough to stand in. The stones here were dark, covered in seaweed that came up black and heavy, and some of them had names carved into them—dates, mostly, and sometimes initials, and once in a while a word that Mara could not read from this distance.

Agnes waded in until the water was at her knees. She stopped at a stone that was larger than the others, smooth from centuries of tide, and she placed her hand on it as if she were greeting someone she had known a long time.

“This is the Harbinger,” she said. “1849. Thirty-two men. The rope that held the anchor gave way in a storm and the boat turned over before anyone knew what was happening. They were all below deck. No one saw it happen.”

“How do you know?”

“The ferryman who found them counted the bodies.” Agnes ran her fingers along the carved letters. H.A.R.B.I.N.G.E.R. “His grandson was a fisherman here for forty years. His great-grandson still runs the supply boat from the mainland.”

Mara stood on the shore and felt the damp of the air on her skin and watched Agnes perform the ritual that had no ritual to it—just the words and the touch and the names spoken so the dead could be heard, so they could rest, so the sea would know that someone still remembered what it had taken.

The naming was not magic. Mara understood that now. The dead did not rise because someone spoke their names. The sea did not give back what it had claimed because someone said the right words in the right order. But the naming mattered. The naming was how the living carried the weight of what had happened. The naming was how they put it down, just for a moment, just long enough to breathe.

“Does it help?” Mara asked.

“Help what?”

“The dead. The families. You.”

Agnes stepped out of the water, her boots squelching on the stones. She did not wipe her legs. The seaweed stain would come out. Or it wouldn’t.

“It helps the way a prayer helps,” she said. “Which is to say, it does not change what happened. It changes what happens next.”

Mara wanted to argue. She wanted to say that she had seen what happened next, and the prayers had not stopped it, and the rituals had not saved anyone, and the whole apparatus of belief was just a way of making the unbearable bearable without actually making it bearable at all. But she did not say this. She had learned, over thirty years, that some silences were more honest than words.

“The burial of things,” Agnes said. “That is what people fear the most.”

She went back to her bag and took out the old cloth, stained and worn, and a small bundle that Mara could not identify from this distance. The brazier had gone out, but the embers were still glowing, and Agnes coaxed them back to life with a patience that Mara recognized—the patience of someone who had done this many times, who knew that the fire would come back when it was ready and not before.

“When the sea gives something back,” Agnes said, “we do not leave it on the shore and hope it goes away. We do not bury it in the ground where it came from. We give it back to the sea.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means we take what the sea has returned—flotsam, jetsam, the things that wash up with the tide—and we bury them properly. In the water. In the place where they belong.” She untied the bundle. Inside was a piece of wood, waterlogged and grey, with something carved into its surface that Mara could not read from where she stood. “This came up last month. The ferryman found it tangled in his anchor line.”

“What is it?”

“Part of a boat that sank in 1931. Eleven men. This piece of the hull had their names on it.” Agnes held it up to the light, the last of the light that was left. “The sea has been giving this back in pieces for ninety years. We keep burying it. The sea keeps returning it.”

“That’s not—that seems like it means something.”

“It means the sea has not forgotten.” Agnes carried the wood to the water’s edge and waded in until the water was at her waist. She held the wood above her head and spoke more words in Gaelic, and then she let it go, and it sank, and the dark water closed over it, and it was gone.

Mara watched the place where the wood had gone under. She thought about the eleven men whose names were carved into that piece of hull, who had been on a boat that sank in 1931 and whose names were still coming back, still trying to rest, still waiting for the sea to be finished with them.

“Does it work?” she asked again. The same question as before, but she meant it differently now.

“The burial?”

“Any of it.”

Agnes came back to the shore, her coat heavy with water, her boots filled with sand. She did not seem to notice. She sat down on a dry rock and looked out at the cove, at the water that did not fight, at the place where Ellen Coulter had drowned in 1901 because she had tried to save her father’s ship and the tide had been stronger than she was.

“The rituals don’t stop the sea,” Agnes said. “I want you to understand that. Nothing stops the sea. Not prayers, not blessings, not the names of the dead or the burial of things. The sea takes what it takes, and the rituals are not about stopping that.”

“Then what are they about?”

Agnes was quiet for a long time. The wind had died completely now, and the cove was perfectly still, and the only sound was the tiny lapping of water against stone, the sound of the sea waiting.

“They give the living something to do while they wait.”

Mara felt the weight of it settle into her chest—the same weight she had felt in the morning, standing by the cold fire, knowing what the message said, knowing she could not unknow it. She had thought the rituals might be a way out. She had thought, if she learned what Agnes knew, if she performed the old observances correctly, perhaps she could change what was coming. Perhaps she could stop the sea from taking Eilidh Gunn, could give the reading a different ending than the one it promised.

But the rituals could not stop the sea. The rituals could not change what was coming. The rituals were just—a way of standing at the edge and meaning it. A way of being present for what could not be prevented. A way of carrying the weight without being crushed by it.

“What do you do,” Mara asked, “when the rituals aren’t enough?”

Agnes looked at her. The light was gone now, and the cove was dark, and Mara could not see Agnes’s face clearly, but she could hear the answer in her voice.

“I do them anyway.”

“That doesn’t seem like enough.”

“It isn’t.” Agnes stood, shook the water from her coat, reached for her bag. “Nothing is enough. That is what the sea teaches us, if we let it. Nothing is enough, and we do it anyway, and that is the only victory we get.”

Mara stayed by the water while Agnes packed up what was left of the ritual implements. The salt was gone, used in the blessing. The cloth was gone, used in the burial. The brazier was cold, the charcoal spent, the matches burned down to the box. Agnes had given what she had to give, and the sea had received it, and the water was still dark, and the cove was still waiting, and the name of the survivor was still written in Mara’s hand where it had appeared and would not fade.

“Agnes.”

The old woman turned.

“In 1973,” Mara said. “The reading you received. The one that named your brother.”

Agnes did not move. The bag hung at her side, forgotten.

“How did you stop it?”

“You can’t stop it.”

“But you tried. You must have tried.”

“I tried.” Agnes’s voice was flat. “I tried everything. The rituals. The prayers. The old ways and the new ways and the ways that had no name. I tried to make him stay on the island. I tried to make him go further away. I tried to make him understand that the sea had claimed him and nothing would change that.”

“And?”

“And he went to sea anyway. He came back in a box.” Agnes walked to the edge of the water and stood there, looking out at the darkness. “The reading named him as a survivor, Mara. Did you know that? It named him as the sole survivor of a wreck that would happen in fifteen years. Not the wreck that killed him. A different wreck. A wreck that would happen when he was thirty-four, and he would be the only one who lived, and the sea would take everyone else.”

Mara felt the ground shift under her feet, though the stone was solid, though nothing had moved.

“A different wreck.”

“That’s what the reading said. A wreck in fifteen years, and he would survive, and everyone else would die, and the sea would keep him marked.” Agnes’s voice was steady, but Mara could hear the old wound in it, the kind that never healed all the way. “He was seventeen when I received the reading. He was thirty-two when he died. He had fifteen years to wait for a wreck that never came, because he died in a different wreck two years before it was supposed to happen.”

“Agnes—”

“I have never told anyone that.” Agnes turned, and in the darkness her face was unreadable, but her voice was clear. “I have never told anyone that the reading was wrong. That it was not the wreck it said. That the sea changed its mind, or that the timing was wrong, or that there was some mistake in the way the prophecy worked. I have kept that for fifty years because I did not know what it meant, and I still don’t.”

“What do you think it means?”

“I think the sea doesn’t explain itself.” Agnes shouldered her bag. “I think the readings are true, but true the way the tide is true—it happens, and we cannot stop it, and we cannot always understand why it happens when it does. I think my brother was supposed to survive a wreck in 1989, and instead he died in a wreck in 1987, and the sea took him early, or the sea changed its mind, or the sea has reasons that don’t translate into our kind of sense.”

Mara stood in the dark and felt the cove waiting around her, the water still and patient, the place where the tide turned and the sea held what it had claimed and did not explain.

“What do you think the reading meant for Eilidh Gunn?”

Agnes was quiet for a long time. Then she said: “I think it means what it says. A wreck in three weeks. A sole survivor. A name that has been marked by the sea.” She paused. “Whether the wreck is the one the reading meant, or whether the sea changes its mind, or whether something else happens that none of us can predict—that, I cannot tell you. No one can. The readings are true, but they are not complete. They show us the shape of what is coming, not the whole of it.”

Mara wanted to ask more. She wanted to know if Agnes had ever heard of a second bottle, of a correction, of someone receiving a different reading that changed the terms of the first. But the question felt too heavy to lift, and the cove was too dark, and the water was too still, and Agnes was already walking away, already climbing the path that led back to the island and its houses and its people who did not know yet what was coming for them.

“Agnes.”

She stopped.

“What are you willing to do that Agnes was not?”

The old woman stood on the path above her, half-visible in the darkness, the bag over her shoulder, the water still lapping at her boots.

“I don’t know yet,” Mara said. “But I need to learn.”

Agnes was silent for a long time. Then she said: “The rituals require presence, attention, a willingness to stand at the edge and mean it. If you have that, I can teach you the rest. But Mara—”

“Yes.”

“Whatever you do, the sea will take what it wants. That is not something anyone can stop. The only question is what you are willing to give up to try.”

Mara looked at the cove. At the water that waited. At the place where Ellen Coulter had drowned, and the stones with their names, and the wood that sank and kept coming back, and the dark that had no end that she could see.

“I’ll come back,” she said. “Tomorrow. After dark.”

Agnes nodded. She turned and climbed, and her footsteps faded on the path, and Mara was alone with the cove and the waiting sea and the name that would not fade from her hand.

Three weeks. She had three weeks, and a cove, and a woman who knew the rituals, and a reading that could not be unread, and now she had something else too—a question, the same one Agnes had carried for fifty years. What was she willing to give up to try?

She did not know yet. But she knew she could not do nothing.

She turned and began the climb back up to the path, away from the water, back toward the island and its houses and its people who did not know. The dark was complete now, and she walked by memory and by feel, her hands finding the stones that she had known since she was a girl, since before she kept the light, since before she knew what the sea could take.

Behind her, the cove was still. The water did not fight. It waited.

She walked into the dark, and the island held her, and the question waited with the sea, and the three weeks began to count down.
