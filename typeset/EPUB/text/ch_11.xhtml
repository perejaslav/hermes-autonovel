Chapter 11: The Call

The fluorescent lights in the records office hummed at a frequency Eilidh had learned to stop hearing. Three years behind a desk had taught her body things her mind hadn’t authorized: how to sit without shifting weight, how to scan a document without reading it, how to let her eyes move across a page while her thoughts were somewhere else entirely.

She was good at it. That was what she’d been told in her performance review, and what she’d told herself in the mirror every morning since. Good at it. The words had become a kind of armor, worn smooth by repetition.

The file in front of her was a property dispute from 1987, buried in a chain of deeds that went back further than anyone cared to trace. She’d been working on it for three days. The solicitor wanted resolution by Friday. Eilidh intended to give it to him by Wednesday, because that was what she did.

Her phone buzzed on the desk. Her father’s name appeared on the screen.

She watched it buzz. Watched it stop. Watched the screen go dark.

He would call again. He always called again.

The first time, two weeks after her mother died, she’d answered. She’d been sitting in her own flat with the windows closed against the Edinburgh rain, and the phone had rung and she’d picked it up without thinking, and he’d been crying. Not the kind of crying you could hear. The kind that was just below the surface, the kind that changed the shape of words without changing the words themselves.

I know you’re busy, he’d said. I know you have your life. I just wanted to hear your voice.

She’d stayed on the line for forty-seven minutes. She’d hated every second of it.

The second time, a month later, she’d let it ring. He’d left a voicemail about the ferry schedule. She’d deleted it without listening.

The third time, she’d answered on the first ring just to prove to herself that she could. He’d asked about her job. She’d asked about the Inn. Neither of them answered. They’d hung up after twelve minutes, and she’d spent the rest of the evening unable to remember what they’d talked about.

Since then, she’d learned the rhythm. He called on Sundays. She let it go to voicemail. She listened to the voicemail, sometimes, if it had been a bad week. She never called back.

But he hadn’t called on Sunday.

She looked at her phone. Wednesday. Three days early.

Her desk phone rang.

“Records office.”

“Is that you, Eilidh?”

Her supervisor, McAldery. He was leaning over the partition that separated their desks, his face arranged in the particular way it took when he wanted something.

“Sheila wants the file on Henderson before lunch.”

“Which Henderson? There are four.”

“The 1987 one.”

“It’ll be done.”

He nodded and disappeared. Eilidh returned to the screen. The property dispute was almost resolved, buried now in a chain of easements that made her eyes ache. She found the gap in the documentation that the solicitor needed, flagged it, saved the file, and moved on.

Her personal phone sat on the desk, silent.

She worked through Henderson, then two requests from the tax office, then a filing error that someone else had made and she was expected to fix. The morning passed the way mornings passed: in increments measured by screen glare and the particular quality of the light through the windows.

At eleven-thirty, her personal phone rang.

She didn’t recognize the number.

“Hello?”

“Eilidh Gunn?”

“Yes.”

“My name is Mara Callaghan. I keep the lighthouse on Torran.”

Eilidh sat very still. The fluorescent lights hummed. Someone in the next office was laughing at something, the sound muffled by walls.

“I don’t—” she started.

“Your father called me. He said you weren’t answering his calls.”

“My father’s calls are about ferry schedules.”

“Yes. He mentioned.”

The pause that followed was long enough to mean something. Eilidh found herself holding the phone a little tighter.

“What did he tell you?” Eilidh asked.

“He told me what he knew. Which isn’t much.”

“Then why are you calling?”

“Because I found something. A message. It came in a bottle, the way these things come, and it names—”

She stopped.

“It names what?”

“You.”

Eilidh laughed. She couldn’t help it. The sound came out before she could catch it, sharp and wrong.

“A message in a bottle,” she said. “You’re calling me about a message in a bottle.”

“I know how it sounds.”

“You know how it sounds.”

“I’m not asking you to believe me.”

“Then what are you asking?”

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the building, a door closed.

“I’m asking you to listen,” Mara said.

Eilidh should have hung up. She should have said I’m busy or I have to go or simply ended the call without ceremony. She’d done it before, with survey takers, with charity callers, with her father on his worst nights.

But she didn’t.

She listened.

Mara told her about the bottle. Green glass, caught between rocks below the high-water mark. The message appearing in her hand, written in a hand that was not her own. The words that named a shipwreck three weeks away. The words that named Eilidh as the only survivor.

“And when I say survivor,” Mara said, “I mean what the readings mean. It means you’re marked. It means the sea has already claimed you, and if you go to the water, if you’re anywhere near where the ship goes down—”

“It kills me.”

“It takes you.”

“What’s the difference?”

Mara didn’t answer.

Eilidh looked at her screen. The cursor blinked in the middle of a sentence she didn’t remember starting. She deleted it.

“You found a bottle,” she said. “And it had a message. And now you’re calling me to tell me I’m going to die.”

“I’m calling you to tell you there’s a choice.”

“There isn’t. There’s a message in a bottle. Either you believe messages in bottles or you don’t, and I don’t, so—”

“You’ve been dreaming about the harbor.”

Eilidh stopped.

“What?”

“Your father said. He said you haven’t been sleeping. He said you’ve been dreaming about boats coming in that shouldn’t float.”

She hung up.

She sat at her desk and stared at the screen until McAldery asked if she was all right, and she said yes, and he went back to his own desk, and she kept staring.

Boats that shouldn’t float.

She’d dreamed about them three times in the past week. The harbor at Torran, black water, boats rising and falling on a swell that wasn’t there. She couldn’t see the boats clearly, only their shapes, only the way they moved wrong, the way they seemed to wait for something.

She hadn’t told anyone. She hadn’t even let herself think about it, not really. She’d told herself it was stress, that her father had been calling too much, that the island was finding its way into her sleep because she wasn’t letting it in while she was awake.

But she hadn’t told anyone.

She picked up her phone. The call history showed the number Mara had called from. A local number, island exchange.

She put the phone down.

At lunch, she walked to the coffee shop on the corner and bought a sandwich she didn’t eat. She sat by the window and watched the street and tried to think about something else.

The property dispute. The Henderson file. The filing error she’d fixed.

She thought about boats that shouldn’t float.

At one o’clock, she went back to her desk. She worked for an hour, maybe two, the time dissolving into the glow of the screen. Her phone sat silent beside the keyboard. She didn’t look at it.

At three, it rang.

She looked.

Her father’s number.

She answered.

“Dad.”

“Eilidh. Thank God.”

“I was busy.”

“Did she call you? Mara Callaghan? She said she would.”

“Yes.”

“And?”

“And what?”

“What did she say?”

Eilidh closed her eyes. The fluorescent lights pressed down on her eyelids, red and unwavering.

“She said there’s a message. In a bottle. She said it says I’m going to drown.”

The silence on the line was different from Mara’s silence. It was heavier. It was the silence of a man trying to figure out what to say and failing.

“That’s not—” he started.

“I know.”

“You know it’s not—”

“I know.”

“So you’re coming home. You’re coming home and we’ll figure this out.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“What else is there?”

Eilidh opened her eyes. The office was still there, the same screens and partitions, the same hum of lights and the distant sound of someone else’s printer. The world hadn’t changed. Nothing had changed.

“I have to go,” she said.

“Eilidh—”

“I have to go, Dad. I have work.”

“Your work can wait.”

“No. It really can’t.”

She ended the call.

She stayed at her desk until six. She finished the Henderson file, filed the tax requests, wrote an email to a solicitor she didn’t know about a deadline she didn’t care about. The words on her screen were legible and correct and said nothing.

When she left the office, the Edinburgh evening was already dark. The rain had stopped sometime in the afternoon, and the streets were wet, and the lights of the city reflected in ways that made everything look temporary.

She walked home. Her flat was on the third floor of a building that had been built for people who worked in offices and slept alone. She’d been there for four years. The furniture was IKEA, the books were from the charity shop, the view was of another building’s wall.

She made tea. She drank it standing at the window, looking at nothing.

Her phone sat on the counter.

She didn’t pick it up.

At ten, she got ready for bed. She set her alarm. She brushed her teeth. She stood in the doorway of her bedroom and looked at the flat and felt the weight of it, the way it pressed down on her chest, the way it asked nothing of her except that she stay.

She thought about boats that shouldn’t float.

She thought about her father’s voice on the phone, the shape of the words that meant he was afraid, the way he couldn’t say it directly.

She thought about Mara Callaghan, the lighthouse keeper she’d met twice as a child, a woman with grey hair and calloused hands who’d stood on the shore and watched Eilidh wade in the shallows and said nothing, only watched.

She picked up her phone.

The number was still in her call history. She stared at it for a long time.

It would take one call to end this. She could call Mara back, ask her what she meant about the choice, ask her what the readings had actually done for anyone before. She could find out if this was real or if her father had finally lost his grip on reason entirely.

Or she could not call. She could go to sleep and wake up tomorrow and go to work and file her files and answer her phone when her father called and ignore it when he didn’t. She could stay here, in this flat, in this life, in the distance she’d built between herself and the island.

She could stay.

She put the phone down.

She turned off the light.

In the dark, in the bed that was too large for one person, in the flat that was too quiet for anyone, Eilidh closed her eyes and waited for sleep.

The harbor came. Black water. Boats that shouldn’t float.

She watched them from the shore, and they watched her back, and the tide turned the way it always turned, and she did not dream anything else.

She woke at three in the morning and lay in the dark and listened to the city breathe.

She would think about it tomorrow. She would think about it when she was at her desk, when she was drinking her coffee, when she was walking home through streets that asked nothing of her. She would think about it, and then she would decide.

She did not mean to think about it.

She knew she would.

In the morning, the rain came back. Eilidh walked to work with her umbrella and her bag and her face that said nothing. The office was warm and the screens glowed and the Henderson file was waiting on her desk.

She sat down. She opened the file.

Her phone sat beside the keyboard, silent, patient.

She looked at it.

She didn’t pick it up.

Not yet.
