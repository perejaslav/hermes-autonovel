Chapter 10: The Skeptic

The door closed behind Mara and Finlay stood behind the bar with his hands flat on the wood. The room was empty. The chairs were up on the tables for the night and the fire had burned down to embers and the only sound was the sea talking to itself outside, the way it always did, the way it had since before anyone on this island was born.

He poured himself a whiskey. He set it down in front of him and looked at it. He did not drink.

Bottles. Old wives’ tales. A message in a bottle like something from a story his mother used to tell when he was small, about children who went to the shore and found things that should not have been found. She had told them to frighten him, he thought. To keep him close. It had worked. For years he had been afraid of the water, afraid of what it might give back, and then he had grown up and stopped being afraid and started working on the boats instead.

He picked up the glass. He put it down.

One survivor. Eilidh Gunn.

The name sat in his chest like a stone. He tried to move it. He could not.

She was in Edinburgh. She had a job and a flat and a life that did not include him, not really, not anymore. She had left because the island was too small and he was too much and there was nothing for her here. That was what she said. That was what she had always said, even when she was still here, even when she was a girl who followed him down to the harbor and asked questions about the boats and the weather and the things that mattered to him. She had stopped asking. She had stopped coming. He had not known how to stop her.

He should call her. That was what Mara wanted. Mara wanted him to call his daughter and warn her and that was what you did when someone told you something terrible—you made a phone call and you told the person you loved and then you figured out what came next. But what came next when what came next was a message in a bottle? What came next when there was nothing to do, nowhere to go, no problem to solve with the right parts and the right timing and the right sequence of actions that a man could perform and feel useful?

He could call her. She would not answer. She never answered, not when he called, not unless it was something important, not unless it was the kind of call that meant something had happened and she had to come home. He had used that. He had called her when her mother was dying and he had called her when the funeral was arranged and he had called her after, in those first weeks, and she had come home for the funeral and she had not come home since, not really, not in the way that mattered. She had come home for the funeral because she owed her mother that much. She did not owe him anything.

He picked up the phone. He dialed. He listened to it ring.

It went to voicemail. Her voice, recorded years ago, years and years ago, before her mother got sick, before everything changed, telling him to leave a message and she would call back. He had heard that message a hundred times. He had heard it so often it did not sound like her anymore. It sounded like a stranger wearing her voice.

He hung up. He did not leave a message.

What would he say? Your father’s friend found a bottle in the water and it said you’re going to drown? Mara had said there was a wreck coming. Mara had said there was a survivor, and the survivor was named, and the name was his daughter’s. His daughter who was thirty-two years old and lived in a city on the mainland and had not set foot on this island in two years except for the occasional holiday she did not want to take. His daughter who had her own life. His daughter who had left because the island was too small and he was too much and there was nothing for her here.

He had not argued. That was the thing. When she told him she was going, when she told him she had a job lined up and a flat rented and a life waiting, he had not argued. He had stood in this room and watched her pack the last of her things and he had said nothing because what was there to say? She was right. The island was too small. He was too much. There was nothing for her here. He had known this for years. He had known it and he had done nothing about it, which was what he did, which was what he had always done—he waited and he watched and he let things happen because he did not know how to make them happen differently.

His wife had hidden her illness for a year.

He had not seen it. She had been tired. She had been quiet. She had been distant in a way he had attributed to age, to menopause, to the slow erosion of a life lived on a rock in the sea where nothing ever changed and nothing ever would. He had attributed it to anything except what it was. He had not seen the weight loss until it was undeniable. He had not seen the appointments on the kitchen counter, the letters from the hospital, the things she hid in the drawer next to the sink where he never looked. She had hidden it from him for a year and he had not seen it because he had not been looking. He had been tending the bar and fixing the boats and arguing about ferry schedules and fighting the battles he knew how to fight, and his wife had been dying in the next room and he had not seen it.

She had told him at the end. That was what the doctors said, what the nurses said, what everyone said afterward—she had known for a year and she had not told anyone and there was no way to know why, no way to understand what had made her keep it silent until there was nothing left to say. He had asked her. In the hospital, in those last days, he had asked her why she had not told him and she had looked at him with eyes that were already somewhere else and she had said nothing. She had said nothing and he had never known and he had never stopped wondering.

Eilidh blamed him. That was what he understood, what he had understood since the funeral, since the silence that filled the space where his daughter used to talk to him. Eilidh blamed him for not seeing. Eilidh blamed him for the year his wife spent suffering alone. Eilidh blamed him for being the kind of man who stood in the middle of his life and watched it fall apart and did nothing until there was nothing left to do.

She was wrong. That was what he told himself. She was wrong and he had not known and there was no way anyone could have known and if she wanted to blame him for that then she was blaming him for being human and that was not fair and that was not right and he had done the best he could with what he had.

He had done the best he could with what he had.

He poured the whiskey down the drain. He did not know why. It seemed like the thing to do. It seemed like a gesture toward something he could not name—a promise, maybe, or a renunciation. He was not a man who drank alone. He was not a man who needed things. He was not a man who stood behind his own bar and poured drinks he did not drink and called daughters who did not answer and believed in messages that came in bottles from nowhere.

Mara had lit the signal fires in 1998. He remembered. He remembered the night the freighter went down two miles off the coast and the whole island came down to the harbor to watch the flares go up, to watch the light spin above the water like something holy, like something that might save someone if it was bright enough and turned the right way. The mainland rescue came too late. Eleven men. Eleven bodies that never came back. Mara had stood in the tower and turned that light and she had done everything right and it had not mattered. He knew this. He knew the story. He had lived it, same as everyone on the island had lived it, same as everyone on the island had been living it ever since, in the quiet moments, in the spaces between things.

The readings were the same. That was what he told himself. The readings were the same—things people found in bottles, things people whispered about, things that might come true or might not but either way there was nothing to be done. The readings were stories. The readings were old wives’ tales. The readings were the kind of thing that happened to other people, people who believed in such things, people who had nothing else to hold onto.

He did not believe in such things.

He had a bar to run and a ferry league to chair and a daughter who had left and a wife who was dead and a life that was defined by what he could fix and what he could fight and what he could make happen through effort and determination and the refusal to accept that some things were out of his control. He had built that life on purpose. He had built it because the alternative was this—standing in an empty room in the dark, listening to the sea, believing in nothing.

The phone rang.

He stared at it. The sound was wrong—it cut through the silence like something sharp, like something that did not belong. The phone did not ring at night. The phone rang in the day, during the hours when the Inn was open and people came and went and the world was normal. At night the phone did not ring because everyone on the island knew where everyone else was and there was no reason to call.

It rang again.

He picked it up. He said nothing.

“Da?”

It was Eilidh. Her voice, rough with sleep—she had been asleep, it was nearly midnight and she had been asleep, she was in Edinburgh in her flat and she was asleep and he had woken her with this call he had not meant to make, this call that had not been a call, this call that was just the phone ringing in his hand because he had touched it without meaning to.

“I didn’t mean to wake you.”

“You didn’t. I was—” A pause. A breath. “What time is it?”

“After eleven.”

“Jesus, Dad. What’s wrong?”

He opened his mouth. He did not know what to say. The words were there, they were right there, they were exactly what Mara had told him to do—tell her, warn her, make her understand—but he could not make them come out. He could not say a woman found a bottle in the water and it said you’re going to die to his daughter at midnight, to his daughter who had not spoken to him in months, to his daughter who had left because he was too much and the island was too small and there was nothing here for her.

“Nothing’s wrong. I just wanted to hear your voice.”

The words came out and they were wrong, they were all wrong, they were the kind of thing he never said, the kind of thing he would never have said if he had thought about it first. He did not say things like that. He did not call his daughter in the middle of the night to say he wanted to hear her voice. He called her when there was something to discuss, when there was a problem to solve, when there was a reason.

“What?” Her voice was sharp now. She was awake now. She was awake and she was suspicious, she was already building the wall she built every time he called, the wall that kept him out, the wall that kept her safe from whatever he might want, whatever he might ask, whatever weight he might try to put on her.

“Nothing. I was just thinking about you.”

“Dad. What’s going on?”

He could feel it happening—the thing he had always feared, the thing that happened every time he tried to reach her and failed, the thing that turned every conversation into an argument and every call into a wall. He could feel himself trying to control it and he could feel himself failing and he could feel the distance opening between them, the distance that was already there, the distance that had always been there since she was a girl and he was a man who did not know how to say the things that mattered.

“Mara Callaghan came by tonight.”

“Who?”

“The lighthouse keeper. She had something she wanted to tell me.”

“What?”

He closed his eyes. “A reading. She found a bottle. There was a message.”

Silence. He could hear her breathing. He could hear the sound of the city in the background, the traffic, the distant sirens, the noise of a place that was not here, that had never been here, that he had never seen except in pictures and in his daughter’s descriptions, in the places she had chosen to go instead of the place she was born.

“Dad. What are you talking about?”

“I don’t know. I don’t know what I’m talking about.”

“You’re talking about the bottle thing. The reading thing. You believe in that?”

“I don’t know what I believe.”

He heard her laugh. It was the laugh he had heard so many times, the laugh she used when she did not know what else to do, when the conversation had gone somewhere she did not want it to go and the only response was to make it smaller, to make it a joke, to make it something that did not matter.

“Dad. It’s midnight. You called me at midnight to tell me about a message in a bottle.”

“I know.”

“Are you okay?”

The question stopped him. She had not asked that in a long time. She had not asked if he was okay in years, not since the funeral, not since she had looked at him across the churchyard and seen something she could not forgive. She had not asked because she did not want to know. She had not asked because knowing would mean doing something and she had already done everything she could do—she had left, she had built her life, she had kept the distance that was the only thing standing between her and whatever it was she could not face about this place, about him, about her mother’s death.

“I’m fine,” he said.

“You don’t sound fine.”

“I’m fine. I just—Mara wanted me to call you. To tell you. She said the reading named you.”

Silence again. But different this time. He could hear something in the silence, something he could not name, something that was not the wall she had been building but was something else, something he had not heard from her in years.

“What do you mean, it named me?”

“It said there was going to be a wreck. A ship coming in. And there would be one survivor, and the survivor was going to be you.”

Another pause. He could hear her moving, heard the sound of a window opening, the noise of the city getting louder, then softer, then gone—she had gone outside, she was on the street or on a balcony or somewhere that was not the flat anymore, somewhere that was not the place where she had built her life away from him.

“Dad. That’s insane.”

“I know.”

“You know it’s insane. You know how it sounds.”

“Yes.”

“So why are you calling me at midnight to tell me about it?”

He did not have an answer. He stood in the empty bar with the phone in his hand and he did not have an answer because there was no good answer, there was no answer that made sense, there was only the feeling that had been sitting in his chest since Mara left, the feeling that would not move, the feeling that he had been trying to pour down the drain with the whiskey and could not.

“Because I can’t stop thinking about it,” he said.

Another silence. He heard her breathe out, heard the sound of the city again, heard her trying to decide something, trying to figure out what to do with the thing he had told her, the thing that was insane, the thing that she did not believe but could not quite put down.

“I’m not coming home,” she said.

“I know.”

“Whatever she said, whatever you think is going to happen—I’m not coming home. I have a job. I have a life.”

“I know.”

“So why are you telling me this?”

He looked at the bar, at the glasses he had washed and put away, at the chairs on the tables, at the fire that had burned down to nothing. He looked at the room he had built his life in and he did not know how to answer her.

“Because Mara said I should,” he said. “And because I can’t stop thinking about it.”

The silence stretched. He could hear her breathing. He could hear the sea outside, the same sea, the same sound, the same water that had taken his wife’s face and held it, that had taken eleven men in 1998 and given nothing back, that was waiting now for something he could not see.

“Dad.”

“Yes.”

“I had a dream last night. About the harbor. About boats coming in that shouldn’t float.”

He closed his eyes.

“I don’t know why I’m telling you that,” she said. “I don’t believe in that stuff. I don’t believe in the readings and I don’t believe in the bottles and I don’t believe in whatever you think is happening here. But I had the dream and I woke up and I couldn’t go back to sleep and I’ve been sitting in the dark ever since waiting for something I don’t know how to name.”

He did not say anything. He could not.

“So I guess what I’m saying is—I don’t know. I don’t know what’s happening. I don’t know if that means anything. I just needed you to know that.”

“Eilidh.”

“What?”

“Come home.”

The words came out before he could stop them. They were wrong. He knew they were wrong. He had spent years trying to make her come home and he had failed and he had accepted it, he had accepted that she would not come back, that the island was too small and he was too much and there was nothing for her here. But now he said it anyway, now he said it at midnight with the phone in his hand and the sea outside and the feeling in his chest that would not move.

“I’m not coming home, Dad.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you asking?”

He opened his eyes. He looked at the room. He looked at the dark water visible through the window, the harbor lights reflecting on the surface, the boats tied to their moorings, the same boats that had always been there, that would always be there, that were waiting for something he could not control.

“Because I can’t stop thinking about it,” he said again. “And because if something happens—if there’s something I could have done and didn’t—”

He did not finish. He did not know how to finish.

The silence stretched. Then she said, “I have to go.”

“I know.”

“Dad. I’m sorry. I just—”

“I know.”

He could hear her crying. He could hear it in the way she breathed, in the sound that was just below her voice, in the thing she was trying to hide from him the way her mother had hidden her illness, the way everyone on this island hid the things that mattered until there was nothing left to save.

“I love you,” he said.

“I know. I have to go.”

The line went dead.

He stood in the empty bar and he held the phone and he listened to nothing, to the silence that was left, to the sound of the sea and the wind and the night that did not care what he believed or what he felt or what he was going to do next.

He could not stop thinking about it.

He set the phone down. He walked to the window. The harbor was dark, the boats rocking on their moorings, the water black and still and waiting. Somewhere out there, beyond the horizon, a ship was coming. A wreck was coming. The reading said so. The reading named his daughter and the sea did not care what he believed and the three weeks had begun to count down.

He pressed his hand against the glass. The cold was sharp, real, undeniable.

He had three weeks.

He had a daughter who would not come home and a woman who believed in bottles and a feeling in his chest that would not move and a choice he did not know how to make.

He had what the sea had not taken yet.

He did not know if it would be enough.
