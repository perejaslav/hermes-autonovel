Chapter 15: The Muirhead

The morning came gray and dreich, the kind of light that made the walls sweat and the linen smell of damp no matter how long it hung by the fire. Mara had been awake since four, sitting in the chair by the window with her hands in her lap, watching the harbor lights move on the water. She had not slept well. She was not sure anyone on the island had slept well since the reading appeared. The air itself felt different, charged with something that had no name but which every person on Torran would have recognized if asked.

The bottle sat on the table where she had left it. She had not touched it since the night before. It sat in its hollow of salt-stained cloth like a thing waiting to be fed.

She made tea. She lit the peat fire. She stood at the window and watched the first fishing boat leave the harbor, its lights small and steady against the gray. The man at the helm was Angus Reid, who had been fishing these waters since before Mara came to the cottage, and who had never once asked her about the reading because Angus understood that some questions were better left unasked. She respected him for that. She respected most of the island for their restraint. They had not come to her door. They had not sent messages. They had simply watched, and waited, and let her have the silence she needed.

She had not decided what to do with the silence.

The knock came at half eight. Three sharp raps against the cottage door, the kind of knock that announced itself before it arrived. Mara knew the sound. She had known it for forty years, the sound of Muirhead authority making itself known.

She did not answer immediately. She counted to ten, the way she had learned to count when she first came to the tower, those first nights when the dark pressed against the windows and she had to make herself believe that the dark would lift. One. Two. Three. She crossed the room. She opened the door.

Catriona Muirhead stood on the threshold, her coat buttoned to the throat despite the mildness of the morning, her gray hair pulled back from a face that had not softened since Mara first knew her. Behind her stood Moira, Catriona’s daughter, thirty-five years old and still waiting for her mother to tell her she could speak. Moira carried nothing. Mara noted this. When the Muirheads came visiting with requests, they never brought anything.

“Mara,” Catriona said. “You’ll have heard I’m wanting a word.”

“I’d heard,” Mara said. She did not step aside. She did not invite them in. She stood in the doorway and made them speak to her there, on the stone step where she had authority and they did not.

Catriona’s eyes went past her, into the cottage, cataloguing. The fire in the hearth. The table. The bottle on the table.

“I’ve heard you found something,” Catriona said.

“You’ve heard.”

“The Daughters were talking. They’re always talking. I thought I’d come hear it from the source.”

Mara said nothing. She had learned, over thirty years, that some silences were more honest than words.

“The Preservation Society met last night,” Catriona continued. “We talked about the lighthouse. About the restoration. About what it would mean for the island to have a proper heritage site again. A living piece of history, not just the empty tower and the locked door.”

“I’ve read the brochures,” Mara said.

“You’ve read them.” Catriona’s voice flattened. “Then you know we’ve been trying to bring people back to the old ways. Not the sentimental old ways. The real ones. The rituals. The observances. The things that made this island what it is.”

“Dreich work,” Mara said. “Cold stone and longer hymns.”

Catriona ignored this. “The reading changes things. The reading proves the old ways have power.”

Mara felt her jaw tighten. The phrase came out of Catriona’s mouth like a coin she had been saving, polished and ready to spend. The old ways have power. Catriona believed this the way some people believed in God—not as a question but as a fact that required no examination. And because she believed it, she would try to use it.

“The reading is what it is,” Mara said. “A message in a bottle. It doesn’t prove anything except that the tide was high enough to carry glass into the rocks.”

“And the message itself?”

“I’ve told Finlay what it says. That’s where the telling ends.”

“Finlay.” Catriona’s tone made the name sound like a small thing, a thing that did not deserve to be considered. “Finlay thinks the sea is a logistics problem. He thinks if we just had better weather monitoring, fewer storms, more ferries, the island would be fine. He doesn’t understand what the sea actually is.”

“What does the sea actually be, then?”

Catriona leaned forward, and for a moment the authority slipped and something older showed through, something that remembered three sons drowning in a wreck that happened before she was born but that had shaped every decision she had made since. “The sea takes,” she said. “And the sea gives back. And the readings are how the sea tells us which is which. When a reading appears, the island has to respond. The community has to decide. That’s how it’s always been.”

“The community decides.” Mara tasted the words. They were not the worst words, but they were not good ones either. “And who speaks for the community?”

“I do.” Catriona straightened, the authority sliding back into place like armor. “The Muirheads have always spoken for Torran.”

“Finlay runs the ferry that brings your tourists. The weekenders pay for the privilege of sleeping in the old post office. The cooperative women’s weaving keeps the guesthouse in business. And you speak for them, do you?”

The silence that followed was not comfortable. Catriona’s face did not change—Mara had to give her credit for that, the woman had been practicing stillness since before Moira was born—but something behind her eyes shifted, recalculated.

“You’re making this harder than it needs to be.”

“I’m making it what it is.”

“The Preservation Society wants to bring the girl to the cairn. Let the community decide what to do with the reading. Let everyone see that the old ways still speak.”

Mara shook her head. “No.”

“The reading—”

“Is not for the Muirheads. Is not for the Preservation Society. Is not for the community. It’s mine. I found it. I read it. And what I do with the knowing is my business, not yours.”

Catriona turned to Moira, as if for confirmation, and for the first time Mara noticed something she had not expected: Moira was shaking her head, very slightly, a motion so small that only someone watching closely would have seen it. Moira did not agree. Moira had not agreed to come here, and she did not agree now. But Moira was Muirhead, and Muirheads did not speak unless their mother told them to, and so she stood silent and let her dissent live only in the smallest motion of her body.

Catriona turned back. “The cottage is on Muirhead land.”

Mara felt the words land like a slap, not for their truth but for their intent. Catriona was done asking. She was telling now, the way Muirheads had been telling since the first of them set foot on Torran.

“The deed—”

“Deeds can be examined. Land can be contested. The old surveys are incomplete, as I’m sure you know. Your father’s signature on a document forty years old doesn’t trump a family’s claim that’s been here since before the lighthouse was built.”

“My father’s deed predates your family’s involvement with this land by forty years.”

“Which will be for a tribunal to decide, if it comes to that.” Catriona’s voice was calm. She had practiced this calm, honed it over decades of getting what she wanted by the force of her certainty. “I’m not here to threaten you, Mara. I’m here to suggest that we might all be better served by cooperation. The Preservation Society has resources. We have connections. We have people who believe, truly believe, in what the old ways can do. If you let us help—if you bring the reading to the cairn, let the community see, let the Daughters do what the Daughters do—we can make something real out of what’s been forgotten.”

“What’s been forgotten stays forgotten.”

“That’s not your call.”

“It’s my call about this.” Mara gestured toward the bottle, the cloth, the whole small apparatus of the reading. “You don’t get to decide what happens to it. You don’t get to bring my life to your tribunal and call it community business. You don’t get to stand in my doorway and tell me what the sea means.”

“Every Muirhead who came before me—”

“Every Muirhead who came before you is dead. And I’m the one who’s still here, and I’m telling you no.”

Catriona’s mouth thinned. She had not expected this—she did not expect resistance, had not expected it for so long that she had forgotten how to respond when it came. She stood on the threshold with her authority in tatters and her certainty shaken, and for a moment Mara saw her clearly: a woman who had built her entire life on the assumption that she could control things, and who was discovering, slowly, that she could not.

“The cairn is where the island goes when it needs to remember itself,” Catriona said. Her voice was lower now, stripped of its public force. “That’s where we decide things. That’s where it’s always been decided.”

“Then go there and decide. Decide about your restoration. Decide about your heritage site. But don’t decide about me. Don’t decide about this.”

“The deed.”

“Go ahead.” Mara did not know if she was bluffing. She did not know if the deed would hold, if the tribunals would side with forty years of her father’s careful documentation against however many generations of Muirhead assertion. But she knew that she would rather lose the cottage than let Catriona walk away believing she had won something, that she had claimed the reading and bent it to her purpose. “Contest it. Challenge it. Take it to whatever tribunal your money can buy you. But know that the moment you do, the whole island will know what the Muirheads really want. And they’ll know why.”

Catriona stared at her. The gray morning light fell on them both, and in that light Catriona looked older than her years, worn in a way that her practiced stillness had hidden before. She had come here to take something. She was leaving without it.

“You’ll regret this,” she said. It was not a threat. It was a prediction, offered in the same voice she used for the Preservation Society’s brochures, for the heritage applications, for all the careful documents that built her legacy one signature at a time. “When the reading comes to the cairn, and everyone sees what it means, you’ll regret not being part of it.”

“I’ve regretted things before.” Mara stepped back into the cottage. She did not slam the door. She closed it quietly, the way she closed the tower door every night for twenty-seven years, with the knowledge that what lay on the other side was not hers to control. “I survived them.”

Through the window she watched them go. Catriona walked ahead, her back straight, her pace unyielding. Moira followed three steps behind, her hands in her pockets, her head down. They did not look at each other. They did not look back.

Mara stood at the window for a long time. The harbor lights came on as the morning darkened, little points of yellow against the gray. The boats rocked at their moorings. Somewhere on the island, the Daughters were talking. Somewhere, Catriona was planning her next move. The cairn waited. The reading waited. The sea waited.

She thought about Eilidh, asleep in her Edinburgh flat, dreaming dreams she did not understand. She thought about Finlay, at the Inn, making coffee and pretending that the world was a problem with a practical solution. She thought about the lighthouse, its beam cutting through the dark, marking the path that ships were supposed to follow.

She turned from the window. She picked up the bottle. She held it in her hands, feeling its weight, its cold glass certainty, the message inside that had already changed everything.

Not for them, she thought. Not for the Muirheads. Not for the community. Not for anyone who wants to use it.

She set the bottle down. She went to the fire. She fed another peat brick into the flames and watched it catch, watched the light climb and twist and settle into the slow burn that would keep her warm through the rest of the morning.

Catriona wanted to control the reading. She wanted to bring it to the cairn and make it speak for her, proof that the old ways still had power, evidence that the Muirhead vision of restoration was more than nostalgia. She wanted to use Eilidh, and the message, and the whole apparatus of what the sea had given, and she wanted to make it hers.

Mara understood this. She had spent thirty years watching the sea take things, and she had learned that the taking was never random. The sea claimed what it claimed, and it did not let go, and no amount of Muirhead assertion or Preservation Society resolution was going to change what the reading meant or who it named.

But Catriona would try. Catriona would bring it to the cairn whether Mara agreed or not. She would stand in the old place and speak the old words and call the community together, and she would make Eilidh’s name into a demonstration, a proof, a thing that served her purpose instead of the survivor’s survival.

And Eilidh did not know. Eilidh was asleep in Edinburgh, and tomorrow she would board a boat, and the boat would bring her home, and she would walk into a conflict she had not chosen and could not escape.

Mara sat by the fire and watched the peat burn.

She had told Catriona no. She had meant it. But meaning it was not the same as stopping her, and Catriona had resources that Mara did not, and the Muirheads had been claiming things on this island since before the lighthouse was built.

She would have to move faster than she had planned. She would have to find Eilidh before Catriona did. She would have to tell her the truth—or enough of the truth that Eilidh would understand what was coming—and she would have to do it soon, before the cairn and before the community and before Catriona turned the reading into a weapon.

She stood. She crossed to the table. She picked up the bottle one more time, holding it up to the firelight, watching the glass catch the glow.

The old ways have power, Catriona had said.

Mara looked at the bottle, at the message inside that she had already read and could not unread.

Yes, she thought. But not the way you think.

She set the bottle down. She put on her coat. She did not take the bottle with her—she left it on the table, in its hollow of cloth, waiting—but she did take her father’s ledger from the shelf where it had sat for fifteen years, the record of every reading he had ever seen, every prediction he had ever transcribed, every small piece of the truth the sea had given him and that he had kept in ink and paper instead of letting it consume him the way it consumed the others.

She opened the ledger. She found the page she wanted. She read the entry one more time, the same words she had read a hundred times as a girl, the same words she had tried to forget and could not.

When the reading names one, the sea does not forget.

She closed the ledger. She put on her boots. She walked to the harbor, to find the boat that would bring Eilidh home, to reach her before Catriona did, to give her the warning she needed in time for the warning to matter.

The lighthouse stood above the harbor, its beam resting between sweeps, patient and indifferent and eternal.

The boats rocked at their moorings.

The water waited.
