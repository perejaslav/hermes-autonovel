Chapter 7: The Daughters

The vestry smelled of cold stone and dried seaweed. Mara had forgotten that. She had not come to the Sunday gatherings in years—not since she stopped keeping the light, maybe not before that—and the smell was a door she had not meant to open. She stood in the doorway and let it open anyway.

The room was small, the walls two feet of granite dressed in whitewash that had gone grey in patches. A table ran the length of one side, covered in cups and a plate of oat cakes someone had brought. The chairs were mismatched, borrowed from the school when the school had chairs to lend. Five women were already there when she came in. She knew their faces. She knew their names. She had delivered three of their children and held two of their husbands while the breathing stopped. They looked up when she entered and the look was the same look the island gave everything that came back from the dead: acknowledgment, caution, a willingness to let the matter rest.

“Mara.” This from Biddy Sinclair, who was seventy-three and had outlived everyone she’d expected to outlive. “Sit. There’s tea.”

Mara sat. She took a cup she did not drink from. She folded her hands around it because her hands did not know what else to do when they were not holding something useful.

The conversation moved around her the way water moves around a stone. Biddy was talking about her nephew in Glasgow, who had a boy now, who was walking already at ten months, who would be brought to the island for the summer if God was good. Meg Forsyth was saying the herring were late this year, later than anyone could remember, and was that the reading making itself known early or was it just the currents. Catriona Maclean, who was not the Muirhead Catriona but was related somehow and carried herself like it mattered, said the currents were always the currents and the reading was always the reading and the two had nothing to do with each other.

No one asked Mara why she had come. This was either kindness or strategy. She had not decided which.

The Daughters of the Deep met every Sunday because someone had to remember the old things. The blessing of boats was the most visible—the sea-wort hung in every wheelhouse on the island, the salt placed in the bow of every new launch. But there was more than that. There was the naming of wrecks, the words spoken over the water when a body came back or when it didn’t. There was the burial of things the sea gave back, the rituals for objects that should not float and did anyway, the observances for the spaces between what the island was and what the sea wanted it to be. The Daughters kept these things. They were not a religious group, not exactly. They were women who had decided that someone should do this work, and they had not yet found a reason to stop.

Mara had been one of them, once. She had sat in this room and spoken the words and believed that the words mattered. She had stopped believing somewhere around 1998, the same place she had stopped believing in most things. But she remembered.

She remembered enough.

The meeting ran its usual course. News was exchanged. Complaints were aired about the ferry schedule, the weather, the price of diesel. Catriona Maclean brought a small jar of salt from the old mines and said it was better for blessing than the stuff you bought in bags, and everyone agreed even though half of them used the bagged salt and always had. Meg Forsyth asked after Biddy’s nephew’s boy again, and Biddy showed a photograph on her phone, and everyone said the right things about his eyes, his smile, his father’s stubborn chin.

An hour passed. Maybe more. The light through the small windows moved from grey to darker grey as clouds crossed the sun outside. The tea cooled in cups that were not emptied and refilled. Mara listened. She did not speak. She watched the door.

When the gathering ended, the women rose and gathered their things and spoke the quiet words they always spoke—the sea keeps what it claims—before they left. Biddy was the last to go. She paused at the door and looked back at Mara and said nothing. She had that much wisdom, or that much old habit. She closed the door behind her and Mara was alone in the vestry with the smell of seaweed and cold stone.

Agnes had not left.

She was sitting at the far end of the table, hands folded, her cup still half full of tea that would be cold now. She had not spoken during the meeting, not more than a word or two. She had listened the way she always listened—present, attentive, her eyes on whoever was speaking without ever appearing to watch. She was not a large woman. She was not a loud one. But there was a weight to her stillness that made the room feel smaller when she was in it.

Mara had come to speak to Agnes because Agnes knew things no one else knew. Because Agnes led the Daughters, and kept the rituals, and had received a reading herself and never spoken of it. Not once, in forty years, had anyone on the island heard Agnes mention it. But Mara knew. She had known for thirty years, since the morning she had climbed the tower and looked down at the harbor and seen the boat coming in with a box on its deck, and recognized the box, and understood what it meant.

She had not asked Agnes about it then. She had not asked anyone. She had climbed down from the tower and gone to her cottage and sat in the dark until the dark became morning. She had been a keeper then, and keepers did not speak of such things. They watched. They recorded. They kept the light burning for whatever came next.

She was not a keeper now.

“Agnes.”

“Mara.”

“I need to speak to you. Privately.”

Agnes looked at her for a long moment. There was no surprise in her face. There was no expression at all, not until the end, when something moved behind her eyes that Mara could not name.

“The others will be gone by now,” Agnes said. “Unless you want me to ask them back.”

“No. This is for you alone.”

Agnes nodded. She did not ask Mara to sit, because Mara was already sitting. She did not pour fresh tea, because the tea was cold and neither of them wanted it. She simply waited, her hands still folded, her weight still settled in her chair, the way a woman waits when she knows the words are coming and knows they will not be stopped.

Mara told her about the bottle. About the message that had written itself in her hand. About the words that named Eilidh Gunn and promised a shipwreck and left her standing in her kitchen at four in the morning with a glass container she should have thrown into the sea.

She did not explain what she intended to do. She did not ask for help, not yet. She simply told the facts as facts, the way she had learned to tell them during thirty years of recording weather and tide and the movements of boats she could not stop. The message was real. The reading was real. The name was Eilidh Gunn, and she had three weeks.

Agnes listened without expression.

This was the thing Mara had not understood when she was younger: the weight of hearing something and not showing that you have heard it. The discipline required to hold a thing inside your body and let it move through you without moving your face. Agnes had learned this, or she had always had it. Either way, her stillness now was complete. She did not nod. She did not frown. She did not look away or look too long. She simply received the words as they came and let them come.

When Mara finished, the silence that followed was not empty. It was full the way water is full when it has nowhere to go.

“I know what they cost.”

Agnes said this quietly. The words were not loud but they filled the room the way a bell fills a space—not with volume, but with resonance. Mara heard them and felt the cold from the stone walls move into her chest.

“I know what the readings cost,” Agnes said again. “I have known for fifty years.”

She did not elaborate. She did not need to. The words were a door she was opening, and she would open it at her own pace, or not at all.

“1973.” Agnes said the year like she was naming a wound. “I was seventeen. My brother was twenty. His name was Calum.”

She stopped. She looked at her hands, which were still folded, which had not moved.

“Calum was the best swimmer on the island. He could hold his breath longer than anyone. He could read the water the way some people read books. He was going to be a fisherman all his life, and he was already better than the men twice his age.”

Mara said nothing. She had learned, over thirty years, that some silences were more honest than words.

“There was a bottle,” Agnes said. “On the rocks below the old schoolhouse. I found it in the morning, before anyone else was awake. I opened it and the message was there, in my hand, written in my hand, but the words were not my words.”

She looked up. Her eyes were dry. Her voice was steady.

“It said he would survive a wreck. It said he would be the only one. It said the sea had already claimed him and was waiting to take him home.”

The room was very quiet. The light had gone fully grey now, the clouds having won their argument with whatever sun had tried to break through. Mara could hear her own breathing and the faint sound of the sea against the harbor walls a hundred yards away.

“I told him,” Agnes said. “The moment I knew. I went to him and I told him what the message said and I begged him to stay on the island. To not go to sea that winter. To wait, at least, until the spring.”

“And he didn’t.”

“No.”

Agnes unfolded her hands. She pressed her palms flat against the table, the way a woman does when she needs to feel something solid beneath her.

“He was twenty years old. He was the best swimmer on the island. He thought he knew what the sea could do, because he had spent his whole life swimming in it. He thought the reading was a warning he could heed—that if he was careful, if he was good, the wreck would come and he would survive it and it would prove that the sea was not something to fear.”

“But that’s not what the readings mean.”

“No.” Agnes’s voice was quiet. “The readings don’t mean what we want them to mean. They mean what they mean. The sea has already claimed who it has claimed. The wreck happens because the wreck happens. The survivor survives because the survivor survives. The reading doesn’t change any of that. It just lets us know, before, so that the knowing is its own punishment.”

“What happened to him?”

“He went to sea in November. He came back in January, in a box. The wreck was three miles off the mainland coast. He was the only one who made it to the rocks, and he held on for six hours, and the boat that should have picked him up took on water and sank, and by the time another boat came he was gone.”

She stopped. The room was very still.

“He held on for six hours,” Agnes said again. “The reading named him as the survivor. He survived the wreck. He survived the water. He survived six hours on those rocks in January. And then he died of exposure because by the time anyone could reach him, it was too late.”

Mara felt the words land in her chest like stones dropped into deep water. She felt them sink.

“I tried to warn him,” Agnes said. “I tried to stop him. I told everyone who would listen and some who wouldn’t. I went to his captain and I went to the harbor master and I went to my father, and they all said the same thing: the readings come true, but they can’t be stopped. Calum would survive, they said, because the reading said so. They told me not to worry.”

“And he did survive. For a while.”

“Yes.” Agnes folded her hands again. “He survived for a while. And then he didn’t. And I have never told anyone what the reading said, because what would be the point? I told him, and it didn’t save him. I told the others, and they didn’t believe me until it was too late, and then they believed me too much and blamed themselves for not listening sooner. The reading doesn’t give you a way out. It just gives you a name, and a date, and a weight you have to carry.”

She looked at Mara directly now.

“You came here because you want to save the girl. Eilidh Gunn. Finlay’s daughter.”

“Yes.”

“You want to know if there’s something you can do. Something the rituals can do. Something the old observances can change.”

“Yes.”

Agnes was quiet for a long time. The grey light moved across the walls. Somewhere outside, a gull called and was answered by another.

“I learned the rituals when I was young,” Agnes said. “I learned them because someone had to, because the island needed people who remembered. I have performed the blessing of boats for forty years. I have named the wrecks and buried the things the sea gave back. I have done everything the old observances require, exactly as they were written, exactly as they have always been done.”

“Have they worked?”

“They have done what they were meant to do.” Agnes’s voice was careful. “The rituals don’t stop the sea from taking what it has claimed. They never have. What they do is acknowledge. They let the living say what needs to be said. They let the dead be named instead of forgotten. They give us something to do while we wait for the inevitable, and they give us a way to carry what comes after.”

“But people have tried to change things. To intervene.”

“Yes.” Agnes looked at her hands again. “There was a woman, before my time. 1941. She received a reading that named her son. She went to the harbor and she poured salt into the water and she spoke the words of unbinding, the old words that were supposed to release what the sea had claimed.”

“Did it work?”

“Her son went to sea anyway. The wreck happened. He survived.”

“See,” Mara said. “There are things that—”

“Her son survived the wreck,” Agnes said. “He made it to shore. He walked home. And then, three days later, he walked into the sea behind his mother’s cottage and did not come back.”

Mara felt the cold again, the deep cold that had nothing to do with the stone walls.

“The reading names the survivor,” Agnes said. “The survivor survives the wreck. But the sea has claimed who it has claimed. The rituals can change the terms. They can pay a different price. But they cannot undo what was already written.”

“What price?”

“That depends on what you’re willing to give.”

Agnes stood. She moved to the small window and looked out at the grey sky and the grey water beyond the harbor.

“The woman in 1941—the unbinding cost her something. I don’t know what, exactly. The records are old, and people don’t write down what they lose. But her husband died that winter, and her other children left the island, and by the time she was gone the Muirheads had bought her land and there was nothing left of her name except a story the Daughters tell when someone asks about the unbinding.”

“And no one has tried it since?”

“People have tried. They don’t talk about it afterward. The ones who survive the attempt are the ones who are still alive, and they usually wish they weren’t.”

Mara stood. Her legs were stiff from sitting, from the cold, from the weight of what she was hearing.

“Show me,” she said.

Agnes turned. There was something in her face now, something Mara had not seen before. It was not hope. It was not pity. It was the look of a woman who had carried a stone for fifty years and was only now considering whether she was strong enough to set it down.

“You want to see what intervention looks like.”

“Yes.”

“It will not be what you expect.”

“I know.”

“No.” Agnes shook her head slowly. “You don’t. You think you know, because you’ve read the message and you know what’s coming. But you don’t know what intervention costs. You don’t know what it takes from you. You don’t know whether the price is worth paying for someone else’s life.”

“Is that a question?”

“No.” Agnes moved back to the table. She sat down again, her hands folding, her weight settling. “It’s a warning. I’ve given my warning. What you do with it is your choice.”

“Show me anyway.”

Agnes looked at her for a long moment. The silence stretched between them like a rope pulled tight.

“There’s a cove,” she said. “On the east side. Ellen’s Cove. The water there is still. The sea doesn’t fight there—it waits. That’s where we do the observances. That’s where we go when we need to speak to the sea on its own terms.”

“I know the cove.”

“I know you do. You’ve been avoiding it for thirty years.”

Mara did not answer. She did not need to.

“Tonight,” Agnes said. “After dark. Come alone. Wear what you can move in.”

“And then?”

“And then I’ll show you what I know. Whether it’s what you need—” Agnes shrugged, a small movement, almost invisible. “That’s something you’ll have to decide for yourself.”

The light had not changed. The grey was the same grey it had been since she walked in. But something had shifted in the room, some weight had moved from one place to another, and Mara felt it in her chest like a tide going out.

“Why are you helping me?”

“Because you came. Because you’re the first person in fifty years who came to me with a reading instead of running from it. Because Calum would have wanted me to try.”

She stopped.

“Because I failed him,” she said. “I told him the reading and I begged him to stay and I did not do the unbinding because I was seventeen and I was afraid and I thought there would be time. I thought if I waited, if I was patient, the reading would change on its own. I was a coward, and he died, and I have spent fifty years wondering what would have happened if I’d been braver.”

She looked at Mara directly.

“You came to me for help. I cannot give you what you want. I cannot promise that anything will save the girl, or that the rituals will do what you hope, or that the sea will listen to anything we say. What I can tell you is what I know. What I can show you is what I learned. What I can do is stand with you in the cove and speak the words that have to be spoken, if you decide that’s what you want.”

“And if I decide I want more than that?”

“Then you’ll have to pay more than that.” Agnes’s voice was quiet. “The sea always takes something. That’s the one thing I know for certain. It takes what it takes, and it keeps what it keeps, and no ritual and no reading and no amount of wanting changes that. The only question is what you’re willing to give.”

She stood again. She moved toward the door, her steps quiet on the stone floor.

“Tonight,” she said. “After dark. Ellen’s Cove.”

She paused at the threshold. She did not turn around.

“The boat comes in at six,” she said. “The one that shouldn’t float. The one with her name on it. I’ve seen it in my dreams for three months. I didn’t know what it meant until you walked through that door.”

She opened the door. The grey light flooded in, and the sound of the harbor, and the smell of salt and diesel and the sea that never stopped.

“I know what they cost,” she said again, and then she was gone, and Mara was alone in the vestry with the smell of seaweed and cold stone and the weight of fifty years.

She sat for a long time. The light did not change. The tea in her cup did not warm. Somewhere beyond the walls, the sea moved against the harbor and the boats rocked at their moorings and the world continued in the way it always did, indifferent to what was coming, indifferent to what might be done.

She thought about Eilidh Gunn, thirty-two years old, asleep in Edinburgh, dreaming about boats that shouldn’t float. She thought about Finlay, who would not believe until it was too late and then would believe too much. She thought about her own hands, which had held the bottle and written the message and could not unknow what they knew.

She thought about Calum, who survived six hours on the rocks and died anyway. She thought about the woman in 1941, who unbound her son from the sea and lost everything else. She thought about Agnes, who had carried the stone for fifty years and was still carrying it, still standing, still willing to show someone else the shape of the weight.

She thought about the cove. The still water. The words that had to be spoken.

She stood. Her legs were stiff. Her hands were cold. The room was the same room it had always been, the stone walls and the mismatched chairs and the table with its cups and plate of oat cakes no one had eaten.

She walked to the door. She opened it. The grey light was waiting.

Three weeks.

She had three weeks, and a cove, and a woman who knew the rituals, and a reading that could not be unread.

She walked out into the grey afternoon and she began.

Three hours until dark. Three hours to decide whether she would go.

She did not know yet. She did not know what the rituals could do or what they cost. She did not know whether Agnes would show her what she needed or only what Agnes was willing to give.

But she knew she could not do nothing. That much the night had given her. That much she had learned in the morning when she sat by the cold fire and felt the weight settle into her chest.

She could not unknow the name. She could not unknow what the name meant.

She walked toward the harbor and she thought about boats that shouldn’t float, and she did not stop.
