Chapter 23

The floor was cold under her cheek.

Mara opened her eyes to grey light. The cottage. She was on the floor of the cottage, and her back ached in the way that meant she’d slept wrong, the way that meant she’d slept at all, which she had not intended to do. The storm—had there been a storm? The night came back in pieces. The cove. Agnes. The naming.

The naming.

She pushed herself up. Her joints protested, her right knee especially, and she sat on the edge of the cot for a moment with her hands on her thighs, letting her breath come back to her. The cottage was cold. The fire had gone to ash sometime in the night, and the windows were crusted with salt spray where the storm had thrown itself against the cottage walls and left its mark.

Agnes was sitting at the table.

Mara hadn’t heard her come in. The old woman sat with her hands folded in her lap, her coat still on, her boots still caked with the black mud of the cove. She looked like someone who had been sitting there for hours. She looked like someone who had been waiting.

“The storm passed,” Agnes said.

Mara’s mouth was dry. She tasted copper, and sand—she must have been lying face-down in the shallows at some point, though she didn’t remember going into the water. Her boots were wet. Her sleeves were wet. The hem of her coat had soaked through and dried and soaked through again, leaving a tide line on the wool.

“How long?”

“Six hours. Maybe seven.” Agnes’s voice was hoarse. She had been talking to the sea. She had been naming. “The cove went still around three in the morning. I watched.”

“You should have woken me.”

“You needed to sleep.”

Mara stood. Her legs were unsteady, but they held. She crossed to the fire and bent to feed the kindling, then thought better of it and straightened instead, standing with her back to the cold hearth. The grey light through the windows was the light of early morning, the sky heavy with cloud that hadn’t finished deciding whether to rain again.

“Did it work?” Mara asked.

Agnes nodded. Her eyes were red-rimmed, exhausted, the eyes of someone who had spent the night in prayer or something that resembled it.

“The sea answered,” she said.

The words settled into the cottage like a stone dropped into still water. Mara felt them ripple through her chest, through the place where hope had calcified into something she no longer trusted herself to feel.

“And?”

Agnes folded her hands tighter. “The sea will let her live.”

Mara exhaled. The breath came out ragged, shaking. She pressed her palm against the mantelpiece to steady herself, feeling the cold of the stone seep into her hand, anchoring her to something solid.

“But?” she said.

“But she will lose something she loves.”

The word but hung in the air between them. Mara had known it was coming. She had known from the moment Agnes said the word price that it would not be simple, that the sea did not give without taking, that the readings had never been free. But knowing and hearing were different things. Knowing was abstract. Hearing was a fist closing around her ribs.

“What?” Mara asked. “What will she lose?”

Agnes shook her head. “It doesn’t say. The price is never exact.”

“Can’t you—”

“The ritual asked. The sea answered. What it takes, it keeps to itself until the taking is done.” Agnes’s voice was steady, the voice of someone who had accepted the terms long ago. “That’s how it works. That’s how it’s always worked.”

Mara turned away from the hearth. She walked to the window and looked out at the cove, at the grey water lying flat and calm under the overcast sky. It looked peaceful. It looked finished. As though the storm had never happened, as though the sea had simply decided to be still, and that stillness was the answer.

“What she loves,” Mara said. She was not asking Agnes to confirm. She was testing the words, feeling their weight, trying to understand what they might mean. “A person? A thing? A possibility?”

“Any of those. All of them.” Agnes rose from the table, slowly, like someone whose joints had stiffened in the cold. She crossed to stand beside Mara at the window. “The readings take what the sea decides is equivalent to what was given. A life for a life is the simple accounting. But sometimes—”

“Sometimes?”

“Sometimes the sea wants something smaller. A relationship that would have been. A future that was possible. Something that mattered to the person who was supposed to die, even if it didn’t matter to anyone else.” Agnes looked at the water, and her face was unreadable. “My grandmother told me about a reading in 1892. A boy was named as survivor. He lived. But the girl he would have married married someone else instead, because he came back changed, and she couldn’t bear it. The sea took his chance at happiness. It wasn’t a life, but it was something.”

“And there’s no way to know. No way to prepare.”

“No way to know.”

Mara watched the water. The cove was still. The boats in the harbor would be damaged—she had seen the waves from the tower window, seen them breaking over the breakwater, seen the way the moorings shuddered under the strain. The storm had been bad. The storm had been worse than bad. And somewhere on the mainland, Eilidh was waking up in a flat in Edinburgh, dreaming of nothing, knowing nothing, about to learn that her life had been weighed against something she would never see.

“She’ll have to be told,” Mara said.

“Yes.”

“She’ll have to choose.”

“Yes.”

“And if she refuses? If she says she won’t pay the price?”

Agnes was quiet for a moment. The wind rattled the window frame, and a gull cried somewhere outside, high and sharp.

“Then the price is still paid,” Agnes said. “The sea doesn’t negotiate. It doesn’t wait for consent. If she won’t give what it’s asking, it takes anyway. It takes something else, something worse, something that wasn’t hers to lose.” She turned to look at Mara directly, and her eyes were steady and sad. “That’s what the readings are. That’s what they’ve always been. Warnings that come too late to change anything. The survivor lives. That’s the one thing that’s certain. But what surviving costs—only the survivor knows.”

Mara thought of the eleven faces. She thought of them now, not as they had been when she first saw them through the rain-streaked glass of the tower, but as they had been when the rescue boat finally reached them, broken and waterlogged, their skin the color of old wax. She had learned their names afterward. She had said them aloud in the cottage, alone, in the dark, and she had never said them again. The sea had taken them, and what they had loved—their wives, their children, their futures—none of it had mattered. The sea had wanted them, and the sea had taken them, and that was the only accounting that counted.

Eilidh would not die. That was the answer. That was the thing Mara had gone into the water to ask for, and that was the thing Agnes said the sea had granted. Eilidh would live.

But she would lose something she loved.

Mara pressed her forehead against the cold glass of the window. She could feel the grain of the wood under her fingertips, the slight give of the putty, the vibration of the wind against the frame. The cottage had stood here for over a hundred years. It had survived storms that made the walls groan and storms that made the roof shake. It would survive this one too. It would stand here long after Mara was gone, and the sea would still be taking, still be giving, still be keeping its accounts in a ledger no one could read.

“I have to tell her,” Mara said.

Agnes nodded. “You do.”

“She needs to know what it will cost. She needs to know what she’ll lose.”

“She needs to know.”

“But she might refuse.” Mara turned from the window, and the cold of the room hit her like a wall. “She might say no. She might say she’d rather die.”

“Then she dies.”

The words were blunt, flat, without comfort. Agnes said them the way the sea would have said them—without malice, without pity, without any acknowledgment that the choice mattered to anyone except the person making it.

“And if she dies, the reading comes true anyway. The other survivor clause. The one where ships that should have rescued her sink. The one where ropes that should have held break.” Agnes crossed her arms, and she looked old, older than Mara had ever seen her, old in the way that the island itself was old. “The sea doesn’t forget what it’s claimed. If Eilidh runs, the wreck follows her. If she hides, the wreck finds her. If she refuses to be saved, the sea saves her anyway and takes the price from somewhere else. From someone else.”

“Who?”

Agnes shook her head. “Anyone. The reading names a survivor, but it doesn’t name who pays. That’s the part they never tell you. That’s the part that isn’t in the Registry.”

Mara thought of Finlay. Finlay, who did not believe. Finlay, who would fight the reading with every practical tool at his disposal—ferry schedules, weather reports, the weight of his own refusal to accept what he could not control. Finlay, who would try to save his daughter by keeping her away from the island, not understanding that the sea did not care about geography.

“It could be him,” Mara said. “The price could fall on Finlay.”

“Yes.”

“And if it does—”

“Then he pays. And she lives. And the debt is settled.” Agnes moved toward the door, her boots leaving small dark prints on the stone floor. “That’s the other thing the readings don’t tell you. Who will break. Who will bend. Who will pay the price you couldn’t warn them about.”

She stopped at the door and turned back. The grey light caught the lines of her face, the exhaustion in her eyes, the set of her jaw. She looked like someone who had been carrying something heavy for a very long time and had finally, finally set it down.

“You named her,” Agnes said. “You went into the water and you named her and the sea heard. That means the price is yours to offer, or hers to pay. But it’s being paid either way. The only question is whether she knows it’s coming.”

She opened the door. The wind came in, salt and cold and wet, carrying the smell of the harbor—the diesel, the fish oil, the rot of the things the storm had torn loose. Somewhere down the hill, a bell was ringing. The ferry was coming in. The island was waking up to find itself battered but still standing, still breathing, still waiting for the sea to decide what it wanted next.

“I’m going to the harbor,” Mara said.

Agnes nodded. She stepped out into the wind and did not look back, and Mara watched her go, watched the old woman walk down the path toward the village with her coat pulled tight around her and her head bowed against the weather. Then Mara turned from the door and pulled on her boots and went to find Finlay and Eilidh.

The harbor was quieter than it should have been.

Mara stood at the top of the dock and looked down at the boats. Two of them had torn free from their moorings in the night—the ropes had snapped or come undone, and they had drifted against the rocks at the harbor’s edge, scraping their hulls against the granite in the dark. One of them was listing badly, its starboard side stove in, water pooling in the well. The other had been driven up onto the stones and would need to be hauled off by hand, a job that would take six men and half a day.

She counted the boats she could see. Six total. Three working, three damaged. The same ratio as always, or close to it. The island had been dying for as long as Mara could remember, and the boats were a measure of it—not the dramatic failures, the sinkings and the drownings, but the slow attrition of things that could not be repaired, could not be replaced, could not be sustained against the weight of time and weather and the sea that took what it wanted and gave back only what it chose.

Finlay was standing at the end of the dock.

He had not seen her yet. He was looking out at the water, at the boats that had survived and the boats that had not, and his hands were in his pockets and his shoulders were hunched against the wind. He looked tired. He looked old. He looked like a man who had spent the night awake, waiting for the storm to pass, praying to gods he did not believe in that his daughter was safe on the mainland, far from the water that had taken everything else.

“Finlay.”

He turned. His face when he saw her was complicated—relief and suspicion and something that might have been anger, all tangled together in the lines around his mouth. He had not slept. She could see it in the shadows under his eyes, in the way he moved, in the slight unsteadiness of his stance.

“Mara. You’re alive.”

“Mostly.”

“The storm—” He stopped. He looked back at the harbor, at the damaged boats, at the water that was still choppy with the memory of what it had done. “The storm was bad. I’ve never seen it like that. I thought—” He stopped again, and this time the word that came out was different. “Eilidh called. Last night, when the storm started. She couldn’t sleep. She said she kept dreaming about the harbor.”

Mara felt the words land in her chest like stones.

“What did she dream?”

“I don’t know. She couldn’t say. She just said she couldn’t sleep, and she was worried, and she wanted to know if the boats were all right.” Finlay laughed, but there was no humor in it. “She wanted to know if the boats were all right. That’s my daughter. Worried about the boats.”

“Did you tell her?”

“Tell her what? That the storm was bad? That I was worried?” He shook his head. “I told her to go back to bed. I told her the boats would survive. I told her to stop worrying about things she couldn’t control.”

Mara nodded. She understood. She understood perfectly. Finlay had spent his whole life trying to protect his daughter from the things she could not control, and in doing so he had made her feel that the things she could not control were the things that mattered. He had spent his whole life fighting the sea, and in doing so he had made her believe that the sea was something that could be fought.

But the sea did not fight. The sea simply was. It took what it wanted, when it wanted, and no amount of ferry schedules or weather reports or prayers to gods of steel and diesel could change that.

“Eilidh’s coming back,” Mara said.

Finlay went still. The word still was the only way to describe it—he had been moving, breathing, existing, and then he was not. He was a statue, carved from the same granite as the island, rooted to the dock by a weight she could not see.

“What?”

“She called the ferry this morning. Before dawn. She wanted to come home.” Mara watched his face, watched the war between hope and fear play out in the lines of his forehead, the clench of his jaw. “She’s on the boat now. She’ll be here by noon.”

“The ferry doesn’t run in weather like this—”

“It’s running. The captain said the worst is over.” She did not tell him where she had learned this, or what the captain had said before the ferry同意ed to make the crossing. Some things were better left unexplained. “She’s coming home, Finlay. She’s on her way.”

He opened his mouth. He closed it. He turned away from her and looked at the water, and she could see him trying to find a reason to hope, trying to construct a version of events where his daughter’s sudden decision to return was a coincidence, a whim, a sign that she was finally ready to come home instead of a sign that she was already running from something she could not name.

Mara let him have his silence. She had given him the truth, or as much of it as she could give, and now the rest was up to him. He would have questions. He would have demands. He would want to know why Eilidh was suddenly, inexplicably coming home, and he would not be satisfied with any answer that did not make sense, that did not fit into the framework of cause and effect he had spent his whole life building around himself.

But there was no answer that would make sense. That was the thing about the readings. That was the thing about the sea. The answers were there, but they did not make sense. They simply were.

“Finlay,” she said, and her voice was quiet, the quiet she used when she was talking about the eleven, when she was talking about the things that could not be fixed. “There’s something else.”

He turned back. His face was raw, unguarded, stripped of the defenses he usually wore. He looked like a man who had been bracing for bad news all night and had finally stopped pretending it was not coming.

“Tell me.”

So she did. She told him about the bottle and the message, about the reading that named Eilidh as the sole survivor, about the wreck that was coming and the storm that was the first sign of it. She told him about Agnes and the ritual, about the naming and the asking and the price that had to be paid. She told him that the sea would let his daughter live, but that she would lose something she loved, and that no one could say what that something would be until the taking was done.

She told him all of it, and she watched his face as she spoke, watched the disbelief rise and fall, watched the anger come and go, watched the fear settle into his bones and stay there. When she finished, he was silent for a long time.

The harbor was still. The wind had died down, or perhaps she had simply stopped hearing it. The gulls wheeled overhead, crying their endless hunger, and the boats creaked at their moorings, and somewhere out on the water, the ferry was making its way toward the island, carrying Eilidh home.

“Tell me what to do,” Finlay said. His voice was hoarse. He did not look at her. “Tell me how to stop it.”

Mara shook her head. “You can’t stop it.”

“Then tell me how to change it. Tell me how to pay a different price.”

“You can’t do that either.”

“Then tell me—” He stopped. He pressed his hands against his face, and for a moment he was not Finlay the innkeeper, not Finlay the practical man, not Finlay the skeptic who did not believe in readings or rituals or the sea’s intelligence. He was just a father, afraid for his child, and Mara understood that fear because she had carried it herself for thirty years, for the eleven, for the faces she could not forget.

“Tell me what you need,” he said finally. “Tell me what to do. I don’t care about the readings. I don’t care about the sea or the price or any of it. Just tell me how to save my daughter.”

Mara looked at him. She looked at the harbor, at the boats that had survived and the boats that had not, at the water that was calm now but would not stay calm, at the island that had stood here for a hundred years and would stand here for a hundred more, weathering whatever the sea decided to throw at it.

She thought of her grandmother, who had watched the 1916 rescue and remembered it sixty years later. She thought of the lighthouse tower she could not climb, the light she no longer kept, the thirty years she had spent watching ships and counting the ones that did not make it home.

She thought of Eilidh, who was on the ferry now, who was coming home to an island that was about to tell her that her life had been weighed and measured and found worthy of survival, but only at a price.

“Wait,” Mara said. “She’s on the ferry. She’ll be here by noon. I’ll tell her everything—the message, the price, what the sea has asked. And then she chooses.”

“And if she chooses wrong?”

Mara did not answer. There was no answer. The sea did not deal in wrong choices or right choices. The sea dealt in tides and storms and the slow, inexorable weight of what it wanted. Eilidh would choose, and whatever she chose, the sea would take what it was owed.

But at least she would know. At least she would have the chance to decide what she was willing to lose, rather than having it taken without her consent.

That was the only thing Mara could give her. That was the only thing any of them could give.

The choice.

“Go to the harbor master’s office,” she said. “Make sure the ferry can dock. The moorings are damaged—I don’t know if it can tie up safely.”

Finlay stared at her. “You’re changing the subject.”

“I’m giving you something to do.” She turned from the dock and started walking, toward the path that led up the hill, toward the cottage, toward the place where she would wait until the ferry arrived. “You can’t stop what’s coming. But you can make sure the harbor is ready. You can make sure the boats are secured. You can make sure that when she steps off that ferry, there’s a place for her to stand.”

She did not wait for his answer. She walked up the path, her boots slipping on the wet grass, her coat catching the wind. Behind her, Finlay stood at the edge of the dock, watching her go, and she could feel his eyes on her back like a weight.

The harbor was still. The boats were damaged. The ferry was coming.

And somewhere on the water, Eilidh was dreaming of harbors she did not understand, counting boats she could not see, moving toward a home that was about to tell her the price of staying alive.

Mara climbed the hill alone.
