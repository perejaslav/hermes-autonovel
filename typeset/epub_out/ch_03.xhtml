Chapter 3

The morning came the way mornings did on Torran, which was to say it came reluctantly, dragging its grey skirts across the harbor and leaving them there. Mara watched it arrive from the cottage window. She had not slept. The bottle sat on the table where she had left it, and she had looked at it so many times in the dark that its shape was burned into the backs of her eyes—a medicine bottle, green glass, the kind that used to hold things before medicine came in plastic.

She had stopped looking at it an hour before dawn. She had looked at the ceiling instead. She had counted the beams. She had counted the cracks in the plaster. She had not counted the faces, which was a small victory, though the faces had not gone away.

The fire was cold. She did not light it.

She put on her boots. She put on her coat. She walked out of the cottage and down the path to the harbor road, and the mist was still hanging in the air like something that had not decided what it wanted to be yet, and the rocks below the lighthouse were slick with spray, and the world was the world, which meant it was doing what it always did, which was continue.

She did not go to the shore. The shore was where the bottle had been, and she was not ready to stand in that place again. Instead she turned left, toward the village, toward the Inn, toward Finlay, who would be behind the bar by now, setting out the breakfast things, and who would ask her how she was, and she would say she was well, and he would say the weather was going to hold, and neither of them would say what either of them meant.

This was the shape of her mornings. She had learned to live inside it.

Aul迆 was three streets and a harbor. The street that ran along the water had the Inn on one end and the closed post office on the other, and in between there was the cooperative building where the women worked the looms and the harbor master’s office that had been empty since 2011, and the whole thing was maybe two hundred yards long if you walked it slowly, which everyone did, because there was no reason to walk it fast.

Mara walked it slowly now. Her boots found the familiar unevenness of the cobblestones. The stones were old, older than her, older than her father who had come from the mainland as a signalman and married local and raised her to know the difference between a keeper’s work and a keeper’s life. She knew the difference. She had spent thirty years in the tower and fifteen years in the cottage, and the sum of it was that there was no difference, that the sea followed you home.

She stopped at the corner where the harbor master used to sit. The window was dark. A gull stood on the sill, looking in. It did not move when she passed.

Three boats in the harbor this morning. She had counted them from the path. The Morrison boat, the MacBride boat, the Muirhead boat that wasn’t really a Muirhead boat anymore because Cormac had sold it to pay his father’s debts and now it flew colors Mara didn’t recognize, some merchant flag from the mainland that meant nothing except that the boat had changed hands and the island had let it.

Three boats. She had counted three boats every morning for fifteen years. She knew their names. She knew which ones went out in what weather. She knew which ones came back.

She had counted them this morning, and the count was wrong.

She did not know how the count was wrong. The boats were the same. The moorings were the same. The water was the same grey-green it always was before the tide turned. But something in the arrangement had shifted, some weight or angle she could not name, and Mara stood at the corner and looked at the boats and felt the wrongness like a splinter under a nail.

She looked away. She walked to the Inn.

The door was open. The smell of it hit her first—peat smoke and bacon grease, coffee from the mainland that Finlay ordered in bulk and complained about every time, tea that was too strong because Finlay believed tea should be tea and not some herbal nonsense the tourists brought. The interior was small and low-ceilinged and had been the same since before she was born. Four tables. A long bar. Windows that looked out on the harbor. The kind of place that did not need to be anything other than what it was.

Finlay was behind the bar. He was not a large man, but he had the kind of presence that filled a room—not through noise but through attention. He had been running the Inn since his wife died in 2016, and before that he had been running it with her, and before that his father had run it, and the line went back far enough that no one remembered a time when the Inn had been anything other than Finlay’s, or Finlay’s father’s, or some name that had become Finlay’s father’s name.

“Mara.” He looked up as she came in. “You’re early.”

“I’m here,” she said.

“Sit.”

She sat at the bar. He poured tea without asking, which was how he had always done it, and she wrapped her hands around the cup and felt the heat of it through the ceramic. The cup was chipped on one side. She knew the chip. She had drunk from this cup a hundred times.

“Weather’s going to hold,” Finlay said. He said it the way he always said it, which was not as a prediction but as a hope, and Mara understood that he needed the weather to hold because the ferry was coming tomorrow and if the weather turned the ferry wouldn’t come and if the ferry didn’t come the island would live on canned goods for another week, which was not a crisis but was an inconvenience that Finlay took personally.

“I saw the boats,” Mara said.

“Three of them.”

“Three.”

“What’s the matter with you this morning?”

She looked at him. He was older than she was, though not by much, and he had the kind of face that had settled into itself over the years—not soft, but certain. He knew who he was. He knew what he did. He woke up every morning and he opened the Inn and he counted the boats and he waited for the ferry and he talked to his daughter on the phone when she would talk to him, which was not as often as he would have liked.

Finlay’s daughter. Eilidh. Thirty-two years old, living in Edinburgh, working in something bureaucratic that Mara had never asked about. She came home for holidays she treated as obligations. She stood in the harbor and looked at the water with an expression Mara recognized, which was the expression of someone who was leaving but hadn’t left yet.

Finlay knew his daughter was marked. He did not know what that meant.

“Eilidh called last week,” Finlay said. He said it the way he always said things about Eilidh—casually, as though the subject did not cost him anything, as though he had not spent the last five years watching her pull further and further away. “She’s thinking about coming up for the spring.”

“Good,” Mara said.

“I could use the help. The season’s starting. Those tourists want their eggs and their towels and their little boxes of tartan soap, and I’m one man.”

“You’re one man who won’t hire anyone from the Muirhead side.”

“Muirhead side wants a percentage. I don’t give percentages.” He wiped down the bar. The rag moved in circles, methodical, patient. “Besides. Eilidh needs to come home sometime. She’s been gone three years now. Three years and she still sounds like she’s calling from a foreign country.”

“She’s not.”

“She is. Edinburgh’s foreign. All those streets and all those people and not one of them knows what tide’s coming in.” He shook his head. “I don’t understand it. I never have.”

Mara said nothing. She drank her tea. The harbor was visible through the window, and the boats were there, and the water was doing what it always did, which was wait.

“You’ll talk to her,” Finlay said. “When she comes. You’ll talk to her like you always do. She likes you.”

“She barely knows me.”

“She knows you. She watched you walk the shore every morning when she was a girl. She asked me once why you did that. I told her you were counting boats. She said it looked like you were counting ghosts.”

The word landed in the room like a stone in still water. Mara felt it move through her.

“She was a strange child,” Finlay said. “She saw things.”

“Children see things.”

“They stop, though. Most of them stop.”

Most of them. Not all of them. Mara thought of the bottle in her cottage. She thought of the message that had appeared in her hand, in her handwriting, in words that were not her words. She thought of the name that was written there, the name that the sea had put in a bottle and sent to her shore, the name that meant Eilidh, that meant Finlay’s daughter, that meant someone who was going to die unless someone did something, unless someone told her, unless someone broke the silence that Mara had spent fifteen years learning to keep.

The words rose in her throat. They pressed against the back of her teeth. She could feel them there, solid and real, the same words she had read in the dark last night, the words that would not let her sleep, the words that meant everything and nothing depending on whether she said them.

Finlay was looking at her. He had stopped wiping the bar. He was watching her with an expression she did not recognize, or did not want to recognize—something between concern and calculation, the look of a man who was used to managing things and did not know what to do when a thing would not be managed.

“Mara.”

“Yes.”

“You’ve got that look.”

“What look.”

“The one you had in ’98. After the wreck. Like you’d seen something the rest of us hadn’t.” He leaned forward. “What is it?”

She opened her mouth.

She almost said it.

The words were there, right there, the whole terrible sentence of it, and all she had to do was open her mouth and let them out and Finlay would know, Finlay would understand, Finlay would have to believe because it was his daughter, because it was Eilidh, because no father could hear that his child was marked and do nothing, and she opened her mouth and she almost said it and then she closed it again.

She could not.

The silence held. The tea cooled in her hands. The harbor waited outside the window, patient and green and endless, and the boats did not move, and Finlay watched her, and she looked at the chipped cup and the worn bar and the familiar room, and she said nothing.

“Nothing,” she said. “I’m tired. I didn’t sleep well.”

“You should see Dr. Macallister. He’s on the ferry tomorrow.”

“I’m fine.”

“You’re not fine. You’re sitting in my Inn looking like you’ve seen a ghost.”

“I told you. The boats.”

“The boats are fine. Three of them, same as always.”

Same as always. Finlay said it like it was true, like the boats had always been three and always would be three, like nothing ever changed except the weather and the tourists and the tide. He did not see what she saw. He did not see the wrongness in the count, the shift in the weight, the way the harbor looked at her like it was waiting for her to notice something she had not yet noticed.

She stood. She left money on the bar. She did not drink the tea.

“Mara.”

She stopped at the door.

“When Eilidh comes,” he said, “you’ll talk to her.”

“Yes.”

“Tell her the island missed her. That’s all. Just tell her that.”

She nodded. She walked out. The door closed behind her and the mist was still there, hanging in the air, and the harbor was there, and the boats were there, and Finlay was inside with his rag and his hope and his daughter who was going to die unless someone did something, unless someone told her, unless someone broke the silence.

Mara walked to the water’s edge.

The stones were slippery with spray. The seaweed came up black and heavy between the rocks. The water was green today, not grey, a green that looked like something alive, like something that was thinking, and Mara stood at the edge of it and felt the cold come through her boots and she did not step back.

She almost said it. She almost told him.

But she had spent fifteen years learning not to speak, and the learning was deep, and the silence was home, and the words stayed where they were, lodged in her chest like the splinter they were, like the splinter that would work its way out or kill her trying.

The harbor was quiet. The boats moved gently on their moorings. A gull screamed overhead, the same gull that had been on the harbor master’s sill, or its twin, or its ghost. The world was doing what it always did, which was continue, which was wait.

Mara stood at the water’s edge and she watched the green water and she did not count the boats because the count was wrong and she did not know how to fix it and she did not know how to tell anyone and the morning was almost gone and the mist was lifting and the harbor would wake soon, and Finlay would open the Inn again, and the boats would go out or stay in, and the island would do what islands did.

She thought about Eilidh in Edinburgh, in a flat, in a life, in a future that the message had already seen. She thought about the way Finlay held his shoulders, the way he set his jaw, the way he talked about his daughter like she was already lost. She thought about the name in the bottle and the silence in her throat and the fifteen years she had spent learning not to act, and she wondered if she could carry this alone, and she knew the answer, and the answer was no, and she stood at the water’s edge anyway, and she did not move, and she did not speak.

The water was green and patient. The boats waited. The island held its breath.

Mara let hers out. She walked back toward the cottage. The mist was lifting. The harbor was waking. Somewhere on the mainland, Eilidh Gunn was waking too, in a flat, in a life, in a future that did not know what was coming.

The tide was coming in. The mist was lifting. And Mara walked home with the silence pressing against her teeth, and she did not throw the bottle into the sea, and she did not bury it in the garden, and she did not tell Finlay what she knew, and she did not know if she ever would, and the morning was almost gone and the chapter was ending and the question was still the same question it had always been, which was not whether the sea would take what it had claimed but whether anyone would try to stop it.

She climbed the path to the cottage. She opened the door. The bottle was on the table where she had left it.

She sat in her chair.

She did not touch the bottle.

She waited for the day to end, and the night to come, and the faces to arrive, and the silence to do what silence always did, which was hold her, and she did not know if she could carry this alone but she did not know what else to do, so she sat in the dark and she held her hands in her lap and she waited.

The fire was cold. The cottage was quiet. And the sea went on doing what the sea always did.
