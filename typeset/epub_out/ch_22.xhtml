Chapter 22

The storm was a ceiling above them. Mara could see the way the clouds pressed down on Ellen’s Cove, close enough to touch, and she could feel the weight of all that water waiting to fall. But the cove itself was still. The water lay flat as glass. Not a ripple. Not a breath of wind. The granite walls of the cove held the air in place, and the air held its silence, and Mara stood at the water’s edge with her boots soaked through and her hands shaking for reasons she did not examine.

Agnes was already there. She had been there since before Mara arrived, kneeling at the place where the stone sloped down into the shallows. She did not turn when Mara came down the path. She did not need to.

“You came,” Agnes said. Not a question.

“You knew I would.”

“I hoped.”

Mara moved to stand beside her. The water reached her ankles and went no further. In the harbor at Aul迆, the tide would be halfway through its rise by now. Here it had stopped. She did not know what that meant, but she felt it—the stillness was not natural. The sea was listening.

Agnes had laid out her things on a flat rock: a wooden bowl cracked with age, a bundle of dried Heather tied with twine, a small tin box that Mara recognized from the Daughters’ observances. A lock of hair wrapped in muslin. A coin, old and worn smooth. A word, perhaps. Or a name.

“I need you to watch,” Agnes said. “And when I tell you, I need you to speak. Can you do that?”

“I don’t know.”

“Honest.” Agnes smiled, briefly. “That’s what I need. Not certainty. Honesty.”

She lifted the wooden bowl and held it over the water. The crack ran along one side, dark against the pale wood. Mara had seen this bowl before, at blessings and burials, passed down through the Daughters for as long as anyone could remember. It was older than the lighthouse. Older than the Muirheads. Older than the island had a name.

Agnes spoke, and the words were not English.

Mara had grown up hearing Gaelic. Her grandmother had spoken it when she was tired or angry or deep in memory, the words falling out of her like stones dropping into water. But this was not the Gaelic of conversation. This was something older. The sounds moved like the tide—not forward but in and out, pulling back before they pushed forward, each word a wave that withdrew and returned changed.

She did not understand the words. She understood the weight of them.

Agnes lifted a pinch of salt from the bowl and let it fall. The grains caught the gray light and vanished into the water, and for a moment nothing happened. Then the surface of the cove shivered. Not a ripple—a shudder, like a body trying not to shiver and failing.

The salt sank.

Agnes lifted the bundle of heather and held it over the flame of a taper she had lit from somewhere—it burned steady and small, giving no smoke, giving no warmth. The dried flowers caught and blackened and fell, embers dropping into the water with a sound like whispered secrets. The smell of burning heather rose and mixed with the salt air, and Mara breathed it in and tasted something she could not name. Childhood, maybe. Or grief. Or the place where those two things became the same.

“Ellen Coulter,” Agnes said. Her voice had changed. The measured cadence was still there, but underneath it Mara heard the years—the forty she had spent performing these rituals, the faces she had watched the sea take, the names she had spoken into water and wind and the silence that followed. “Daughter of the engineer who built the light. She drowned here in 1901. She was twenty-three years old.”

Mara knew this. Everyone on Torran knew this. Ellen’s Cove was named for her, and the name was a warning as much as a memorial. The Muirheads had tried to change it once, in the 1960s. The island had refused.

But hearing Agnes say it here, in this stillness, with the storm pressing down and the water not moving—

Mara felt the weight of it settle into her bones.

Agnes performed the naming. She spoke the names of every wreck that had come through these waters, and the names were not in any order Mara recognized—some were old, predating her grandmother’s grandmother, and some were close enough that she could have pointed to the rocks where the ships had broken. The Margaret Anne, 1892. The Dundee Pride, 1909. The Archibald, which had no business being this far north and came anyway, and the sea had taken it in a single night. The cargo ship from 1998. She heard the number of the dead after each name: three, seven, twelve, eleven. Her eleven were in that list. She heard their silence, and she did not look away.

The names went on. The cove did not move.

Then Agnes performed the burial.

She cut a lock of hair from her own head—Mara saw the silver strands fall into the water, drift, sink—and she placed it in the wooden bowl and held it underwater. The coin she dropped from her fingers into the shallows, where it caught a glint of light and disappeared. And the word, the last thing in the tin box, she spoke aloud before she let it go.

“Return,” Agnes said. “Return what was taken.”

The water accepted each offering without resistance. The hair drifted down. The coin turned once and vanished. The word—

Mara felt the word land. She did not hear it hit anything. She felt it land the way she felt a change in pressure before a storm, the way she felt the moment a ship decided to break.

Agnes rose. Her knees were wet to the bone. She did not complain. She turned to face Mara, and her eyes were the color of the water in the cove—gray and deep and holding things that had been placed there and never retrieved.

“You must name the survivor,” she said.

Mara’s throat closed. She had known this was coming. She had known since she woke in her cottage with the message burned into her hand, since she walked up the hill to the lighthouse and stood at its base and could not climb, since she went to Finlay and watched him refuse to believe and then went to Catriona and watched her want too much. She had known. But knowing and speaking were different countries, and the border between them was a cliff she had been standing at for three days.

“Not to ask for her life,” Agnes said. “To ask for a different price.”

“What do you mean?”

“The sea has already named her. The reading named her. When the wreck comes—” Agnes paused. She looked at the water, at the still and waiting water, and her face held something Mara had never seen there before. Fear, yes. But also certainty. The certainty of someone who has stood at this edge before and did not fall. “When the wreck comes, the sea will take what it has claimed. Unless someone speaks for her. Unless someone offers something in her place.”

“Something.” Mara’s voice came out rough. “What something?”

“It doesn’t say. The price is never exact.” Agnes turned back to her. “I offered hair. A coin. A word. I offered things the sea might want—things that belonged to me, things I could give up without losing who I am. That’s what the ritual asks. Not a payment. A substitution. You offer something of yourself, and you ask the sea to look at that instead.”

“And if it accepts?”

“Then she lives. And you carry whatever was taken.”

“And if it doesn’t accept?”

Agnes did not answer. She did not need to.

Mara looked at the cove. The water lay flat and still, and the storm above it churned in its prison of clouds, and the air smelled of salt and heather ash and something else—something old, something that had been waiting a very long time. The faces of the eleven came to her, as they always did, and she did not push them away. She let them look at her. She let them wait.

“Finlay’s daughter,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Eilidh Gunn.”

Agnes nodded. She did not tell Mara to be sure. She did not ask if this was what she wanted. She knew what wanting had to do with it and what it did not.

“Speak her name into the water,” Agnes said. “And mean it.”

Mara stepped forward. The water rose to her calves, cold enough to ache, cold enough to remember. She had waded into water like this before—the morning she found the bottle, the morning she read the message, the morning her life became a thing that required choosing. She had not known, then, that she was choosing. She knew now.

She opened her mouth.

“Eilidh Gunn,” she said.

The name fell into the water. It did not echo. It did not ripple. It went down like a stone into deep water, silent and straight, and the cove received it and held it, and for a moment nothing happened.

Then the water moved.

Not a wave. Not a tide. The whole body of the cove shifted, a shudder that ran from the shallows to the深处 and back again, and Mara felt it through her boots, through her bones, through the place where fear lived and had lived for thirty years. The storm above them cracked open—not rain yet, but the sound of rain waiting, the pressure of all that water about to fall.

The cove began to fill.

Mara watched the water rise. It came in from nowhere she could see, drawn up from some deep place or some distant place or some place that had no name, and it covered the stones at the water’s edge, the flat rock where Agnes had laid her things, the place where the offerings had sunk and disappeared. It rose slowly. It rose steadily. It rose until it reached the level of Mara’s thighs, and then it stopped.

Not receded. Stopped.

Agnes stood beside her, still as the water, and she looked at the cove with an expression Mara could not read.

“It’s waiting,” Agnes said.

“For what?”

“For you to give it something.” Agnes’s voice was quiet. “You named her. The sea heard. Now it wants to know what you’re willing to pay.”

Mara looked at her hands. The message was gone from her palm—the words had faded sometime in the night, leaving only the memory of the burn. But she felt the message still. She felt the weight of the names, the survivor’s name and the dead’s names and her own name, standing at the edge of all of them.

She thought of the eleven faces. She thought of her grandmother, who had watched the 1916 rescue and remembered it sixty years later, who had told Mara the story and made her promise to remember. She thought of the lighthouse tower she could not climb, the light she no longer kept, the thirty years she had spent watching ships and counting the ones that did not make it home.

She thought of Finlay, who did not believe. She thought of Catriona, who believed too much. She thought of Eilidh, who did not know yet, who was somewhere on the mainland living a life that did not include boats or islands or the weight of old names spoken into water.

The water waited.

“What do I give?” Mara asked.

Agnes shook her head. “That’s yours to know. The ritual shows you how to ask. It can’t tell you what to offer. That has to come from you.”

From her. From the place where wanting lived and had lived for fifteen years, in the silence of the cottage, in the absence of expectation. From the place where she had locked away the faces of the eleven and the weight of the lighthouse and the light she had once carried and could not carry now.

The cove filled around her. The water was at her waist. It was cold. It was still.

She did not know what she was willing to lose.

She did not know what she had left.

The storm broke above them, and the rain came down, and the water in the cove rose with it, and Mara stood at the edge of the sea and waited for the price to be named.
