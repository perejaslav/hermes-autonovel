Chapter 24

The Inn was warm. Mara had forgotten what warm felt like when it wasn’t the cottage and its peat fire, when it was a room full of bodies and heat from the kitchen and the particular closeness that came from too many people in too small a space. She stopped in the doorway and let her eyes adjust.

Finlay sat at the table nearest the fire. Eilidh sat across from him. Between them, two cups of tea gone cold, and the space where a conversation had been—Mara could see it in the way they weren’t looking at each other, the way Eilidh’s hands were wrapped around her cup as though it were the only solid thing in the room.

Finlay saw her first. His face did something complicated, and then he was standing, and then he was crossing the room, and his hand was on her arm, and he was saying her name like it was an accusation.

“You went up the hill,” he said.

“I came back down.”

“You were supposed to stay. I told you to stay.”

“You told me to stay away from your daughter.” Mara looked past him, to where Eilidh had risen from her chair and was standing very still, watching. “I didn’t promise to obey.”

Finlay’s grip on her arm tightened, then released. He stepped back. His face was red—from the fire, from something else. “She came on the morning ferry. She came home. That’s—that’s what she does. She comes home.”

“Does she.”

“She came home.”

Eilidh spoke from across the room. Her voice was steady, which surprised Mara. Young women who had just been told they were marked for death did not usually sound steady. “He told me about the message. The reading. He told me what it says.”

“And what does it say?”

“That I drown.” Eilidh’s hands were still around her cup. She looked down at them as though they belonged to someone else. “That I’m the only one who survives, but I drown. I don’t understand that part. How do you survive drowning?”

“You don’t,” Mara said. “That’s why there’s a price.”

The room had gone quiet. The other patrons—the three fishermen at the back table, the old woman by the window who was always there, the weekenders who had wandered in for lunch and now sat frozen with their forks halfway to their mouths—all of them were listening, and none of them were pretending not to. This was the island. This was what happened when you spoke in public. You spoke to everyone.

Finlay turned to Eilidh. “She doesn’t know what she’s talking about. Readings are—there’s no proof, there’s never been any proof, it’s superstition and old women and—”

“Finlay.”

“Don’t tell me to be quiet.”

“I’m not telling you to be quiet. I’m telling you to listen.” Mara moved past him, toward the fire, toward the table where Eilidh stood. “Your daughter came home. She got on a boat and she came back to an island that was about to tell her that her life had been weighed and measured and found worthy of survival, but only at a price.”

“That’s not—”

“The sea will let her live,” Mara said. She was close enough now to see the fine lines around Eilidh’s eyes, the exhaustion that sat in her like a stone. This was Finlay’s daughter. This was the child he had watched grow, the child he had lost to Edinburgh and a life that was not this one, the child he had clung to after her mother died. “But she will lose something she loves. That’s the bargain. That’s what the sea has asked.”

Finlay made a sound. It was not a word. It was the sound of something tearing.

“No.” He was shaking his head. “No. Not again. We don’t make bargains with the sea. We don’t—we don’t negotiate with it, we don’t—we don’t give it things, we just—”

“Finlay.”

“Someone drowned in 1987. Did you know that? A man from the Cairn family went swimming in Ellen’s Cove and didn’t come back. And afterward they said the sea had taken him because he’d made a bargain, fifty years before, when he was young and stupid and thought he could get something for nothing. They said the price had finally come due.” His voice was rising. The fishermen in the back were staring. The weekenders had put down their forks entirely. “That man was my grandfather. He drowned because his father made a deal, and now you’re telling me my daughter—”

“Your grandfather made a bargain to save someone,” Mara said. “Someone he loved. He paid the price when it came, and the sea let him live until it didn’t. That’s how it works. The debt doesn’t come due until it comes due.”

“That’s not—that’s not a reason. That’s not a reason to—”

“What would I lose?”

Eilidh’s voice cut through Finlay’s. She had not moved from her position by the table, had not raised her voice, had not done anything but ask a question, but the room went quiet for her the way it always went quiet for women who had learned to make themselves heard in places that did not want to hear them.

Mara turned to face her. “I don’t know. That’s how it works. The reading tells you that you will lose something. It doesn’t tell you what. It doesn’t give you a list. It just—takes.”

“So I could lose anything.”

“Yes.”

“My car. My job. My—”

“Anything you love.”

Eilidh was quiet for a moment. She looked at her father. Mara watched the conversation pass between them without words—years of silence, years of leaving, years of grief neither of them had known how to carry. Finlay’s face was raw. His hands were clenched at his sides.

“I’d rather lose something than lose everything,” Eilidh said.

The words landed in the room like a stone in still water. Mara felt the ripples move outward—Finlay flinching, the fishermen turning away, the old woman by the window closing her eyes as though in prayer or in memory.

“Eilidh.” Finlay’s voice broke on her name. “Eilidh, you don’t have to—you don’t have to decide now, you don’t have to decide at all, we can wait, we can think, we can—”

“What would thinking change?”

“I don’t know. Something. Anything.” He was moving toward her, reaching for her, the same gesture he had made with Mara at the door—grabbing hold as though physical contact could stop what was coming. “There has to be something. Another way. A different price.”

“There isn’t.”

“How do you know? You’re not—you’re not an expert, you’re just a woman who found a bottle, you’re just—”

“I’m the woman who has watched the sea take things for thirty years.” Mara’s voice was quiet. It did not need to be loud. The room was listening. “I’m the woman who lit the signal fires when the Mairead went down in ’98. I’m the woman who counted the boats in the harbor every morning and knew which ones wouldn’t come back. I’m the woman who knows what the readings cost because I have seen the people who paid them, and I am telling you now: there is no other price. There is no bargaining with the sea. There is only what it asks, and what you give it, and whether you can live with the difference.”

Finlay’s hand was on Eilidh’s shoulder. He was holding her like she was something that might fall, or something that might fly away. His face was wet. Mara had not seen him cry since the funeral.

“Then I’ll pay it,” Eilidh said.

“Eilidh—”

“I’ll pay it.” She stepped back from her father’s touch, and the motion was not rejection but necessity—she needed the space, needed the room to stand in, needed to be her own person in this moment and not his child. “I’ll get on the ferry. I’ll take my chances. And if the sea wants something in return, I’ll give it.”

“You can’t just—”

“Dad.” She looked at him. “I came home. I got on the boat and I came back, and I don’t know why, I don’t know what made me do it, but I did it. And now I’m here, and the message says I survive, and all I have to do is pay a price I don’t know yet.” She took a breath. Her voice was steady. Mara wondered how she had learned to sound so steady, this young woman who had grown up on an island and fled to the city and come back to find her life measured out in tide and storm. “I’d rather lose something than lose everything.”

Finlay said nothing. His hands fell to his sides. He stood there, in the warm room that smelled of cooking and bodies and the sea that had made them all, and he looked at his daughter with the face of a man who had finally understood that he could not hold on.

Mara went to the window. Outside, the harbor was still. The clouds had thinned. The wind had dropped. For the first time in days, the weather held.

“Ferry comes at noon,” she said. “Weather window opens in an hour. If she’s going to go, she goes now.”

Finlay made a sound. It might have been a word. It might have been her name.

Eilidh was already moving. She crossed to her father and she put her hand on his arm, briefly, and she said something Mara could not hear, and then she was walking toward the door, toward the harbor, toward the water that had been waiting for her all along.

Mara watched her go. She watched Finlay stand frozen by the fire, watched the fishermen exchange glances, watched the weekenders look at their half-eaten lunch with expressions of people who had stumbled into something they did not understand and would not be able to explain.

Outside, the sky was clearing. The storm was over. The sea was waiting.

And somewhere on the water, the ferry was coming, and on it was a woman who had chosen to pay a price she did not know, and the only question left was the one that had no answer: what would she lose?

Mara followed her out the door.
