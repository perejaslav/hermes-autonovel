Chapter 19

The weekenders’ daughter was building a castle at the waterline. Eilidh watched from the harbor wall, her hands in her pockets, her back to the inn. The girl was maybe eight, maybe nine—young enough to believe that sand could hold against the sea. Her mother called to her from the rocks where she was sketching. Her father sat on the wall farther up, reading something on his phone, shielding the screen from the glare. They had come for two weeks. They would leave with photographs and memories of quaintness and a vague sense that they had seen something authentic.

They didn’t know about the reading. They didn’t know about any of it. They were just here.

The girl shoved another handful of sand into place. The tide was coming in, slow, inevitable. She would have maybe twenty minutes before the water reached her walls.

Eilidh had counted the boats that morning. Six in the water, two on the hard. The Muirhead boat was in the outer mooring, where it had been all week—theirs or someone else’s, she didn’t know. The ferry was due in three hours. She had looked at the schedule on the way off the mainland boat, noted the times, calculated the connections. Edinburgh to the coast. Coast to the islands. Three days she could be gone if she left now, before Finlay knew she was awake, before anyone saw her on the street and thought to ask questions.

The girl stamped on her castle. Patting, shaping. She believed.

Eilidh turned and walked to the inn.

Her father was behind the bar, drying glasses with a cloth that had been white once. The morning trade was light—a pair of the ferry crew finishing breakfast, the weekenders drifting in for coffee. Finlay looked up when she came in. His face did something she had learned to read as concern, which he tried to disguise as casualness.

“You’re up early.”

“Slept badly.”

“The ferry ride?”

“Bad dreams.”

He set the glass down. Wiped a spot that wasn’t there. “I called you.”

“I know.”

“You came.”

“I came.”

They had this conversation every time, though not always in words. She was here because he called. She was here because of the dreams. She was here because she had swum in water that smelled like the harbor at night, water that shouldn’t have been in her apartment, water that meant she was already back before she had decided to come.

The weekenders came through the door. The man nodded at Eilidh, at Finlay, at the room in general. His wife followed with the sketches she’d made on the rocks—Ellen something, from somewhere, she had introduced herself but Eilidh had already forgotten. They wanted coffee. They wanted to sit by the window. They wanted to watch the harbor and feel the feeling they came here to feel.

Finlay poured their coffee. Eilidh watched the harbor through the salt-streaked glass.

“It’s so peaceful here,” the wife said, not to anyone in particular. “No phones. No rush. Just the sea.”

Eilidh almost laughed. The weekender looked at her, curious.

“Sorry,” Eilidh said. “Just thinking about something.”

The wife smiled. She had the kind of face that had never been tested by anything harder than a delayed flight. She had come to get away from the noise. She had succeeded. She would go home with clearer skin and a deeper understanding of her own privilege, and she would tell people it was spiritual, what the island had done for her. She would mean it.

“Just the sea,” Eilidh said. “Yeah.”

She thought about the boats. She thought about the moorings. She thought about the channel between the harbor and the open water—maybe half a mile of deep water, tide running north, current pulling toward the shipping lanes where the big boats went through without stopping. She thought about swimming. She thought about the dinghy tied up at the end of the pier, its painter knotted in a loop she could undo in thirty seconds if she was quick, if she was quiet, if Finlay was busy with the weekenders and no one saw her cast off and drift.

She could be in the water before anyone noticed.

She could be past the harbor mouth before the ferry arrived.

She could disappear.

But the sea was cold this time of year. Cold enough to steal your breath, your strength, your sense of which way was up. She had swum as a girl, in the cove behind the old packing station, in water that was shallow enough to stand in and warm enough not to kill you. She wasn’t a strong swimmer. She knew what the harbor could do.

She knew what it had done.

Her mother had swum. Finlay had told her that once, early in their marriage—Mhairi had swum every summer, braved the cold water like it was nothing, came out shaking and laughing and alive. But that was years before Eilidh was born. By the time Eilidh knew her mother, Mhairi didn’t swim anymore. Didn’t go near the water unless she had to. Eilidh had asked once why, and her mother had looked at her with an expression she hadn’t understood then and understood now—a look that said the answer was too heavy to carry and too true to speak.

Her mother had stayed. That was the thing. Through the winters and the storms and the slow dying of the island, through the automation and the departures and the years when it felt like everyone Eilidh knew had left, her mother had stayed. She had said it was because of the house, the community, the history. But Eilidh knew now that it wasn’t any of those things. Her mother had stayed because leaving would have meant admitting that the sea had won, that the island had taken enough, that there was nothing left worth keeping.

She had stayed, and she had died anyway.

Eilidh pushed back from the table. The weekenders looked up, then away. Her father was watching her from behind the bar.

“I’m going out,” she said.

“Out where?”

“The harbor. Walking.”

“Alone?”

“I’m not going to drown, Dad.”

He flinched. She hadn’t meant it as a cruel thing, but she said it and felt the weight of it and knew that her mother was in the room with them, in the silence between his breath and hers. Her mother, who had hidden her illness for a year. Her mother, who had bled into the hospital on the mainland while Eilidh was in Edinburgh failing to answer her phone.

Finlay wiped the bar with the cloth. His hands were steady. His eyes were not.

“I need to go to Mara’s,” Eilidh said.

The cloth stopped.

“What?”

“Mara Callaghan. At the cottage. I need to talk to her.”

Finlay set the cloth down. He looked at the weekenders, then at Eilidh, then at the harbor through the window. He was thinking, she could see it—the calculations running behind his face, the attempt to manage what could not be managed.

“She found the reading,” he said.

“So I heard.”

“Eilidh.”

“What?”

He came around the bar. He was wearing the apron he wore for the breakfast shift, white with the inn’s name embroidered at the chest, a gift from his wife years ago. He looked like a man who had rehearsed what he was going to say and was now discovering that the words didn’t work.

“What do you think you’re going to learn from her?”

“Everything.”

“There is no everything. There’s what she thinks she knows and what the rest of us have to live with.”

“Then I’ll learn what she thinks she knows. And I’ll decide for myself.”

He stepped closer. Lowered his voice. “You don’t have to decide anything. That’s the point. You can leave. The reading doesn’t—”

“The reading named me.” Her voice was flat. The weekenders had stopped talking. She didn’t care. “That’s what you said. That’s what the message said. So don’t tell me I don’t have to decide. Don’t tell me it’s optional. Because it’s not.”

Finlay’s jaw worked. He was controlling himself, she could see it—the effort it cost him not to say something he would regret, not to grab her arm and lock her in a room until she came to her senses. He had done that once, years ago, when she was seventeen and had been out all night with the Muirhead boy. He had waited up with the door locked and a face like stone, and she had screamed at him until her throat hurt, and then she had sat in her room and listened to him pace below her, and in the morning he had made her breakfast and said nothing.

He was doing the same now. Controlling himself. Letting her have the space he thought she needed.

But he couldn’t control this. He couldn’t control what the message had said, or what the sea had marked, or what was coming whether she faced it or fled.

“I’m going,” she said.

She walked out.

The path to the cottage was gravel and old stone, worn smooth by generations of feet. Eilidh had walked it a hundred times as a child, when Mara was still the keeper, when the light was still lit, when the tower was a thing of wonder instead of a thing of loss. She had climbed it once, with her mother holding her hand. She had stood at the top and looked out at the water and felt small in a way that was good, that made her feel like part of something larger than herself.

She didn’t feel that now. She felt the size of the water, its indifference, its willingness to take whatever it was offered.

The cottage was gray stone, windows dark, chimney cold. Mara would be inside. Mara would be waiting. Mara had been waiting since the message appeared, since the words formed in her hand and named a daughter who had tried to forget where she came from.

Eilidh stopped at the gate.

The lighthouse stood behind the cottage, sixty-two feet of stone and iron, dark in the morning gray. The light was burning—she could see the faint glow through the housing, the mechanism that turned the lens, the system that someone else now maintained. Mara had climbed those stairs every night for twenty-seven years. She had climbed them alone, in the dark, in the cold, in the wind that screamed through the gaps in the door. She had lit the light and watched the sea and known what she was for.

Now she was retired. Now she was alone. Now she was waiting for a girl to come ask her the question that had no good answer.

Eilidh’s hands were shaking. She shoved them deeper into her pockets. She breathed in, out, in, out. She thought about the ferry schedule and the boats and the swimmer’s stroke that would carry her past the harbor mouth if she was fast enough, strong enough, cold enough to survive.

She was not fast enough. She was not strong enough. She was not cold enough.

Or maybe she was all of those things, and it didn’t matter, because the reading had said what it had said and the sea had marked who it had marked, and running would only change the location of the ending.

She walked up to the door.

She knocked.

The door opened. Mara stood in the doorway. She looked old. She looked tired. She looked like someone who had been waiting for exactly this moment.

“You’re here,” Mara said.

“Tell me everything.”

Mara stepped back. The door opened wider. The room beyond was dim, firelight and salt smell, the same as it had always been.

Eilidh stepped inside.

The door closed behind her.

The lighthouse beam swept across the cove, past the harbor, past the boats, and somewhere in the dark water, the tide turned.
