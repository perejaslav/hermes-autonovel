Chapter 21

The storm was here.

Mara heard it before she saw it—the way the wind shifted, the way the air pressure changed against her skin, the way the birds went silent on the headland. She had watched storms build on the horizon for forty years. She knew the signs the way she knew her own heartbeat. This one was not building. It had built. It was here.

She was in the cottage when it arrived, standing at the window with Finlay’s words still heavy in her mind: Something is moving. Past the channel. Coming in from the west. Agnes had gone out into it twenty minutes ago, pulling on her old oilskin, the one she had worn for forty years of ferry runs and blessing ceremonies and trips to Ellen’s Cove. Her face was grave.

Now the rain came. Not drizzle—driving horizontal sheets that hammered the windows and turned the world outside into a grey wall of noise. The wind screamed over the headland, finding the gaps in the stone, and the cottage walls shuddered the way they always did in storms. Mara did not move from the window. She watched the harbor through the blur of water running down the glass. The boats were gone from view. The water was gone from view. Everything was grey and violent and beyond her.

Except the light.

The lighthouse mechanism hummed. She heard it through the walls, felt it in the floor, the same low vibration that had been her companion for twenty-seven years. The beam swept past the window, cutting through the rain, and she watched it go. Someone else’s light now. The company maintained it for the cargo ships that came through the channel, the ships that didn’t stop, didn’t anchor, didn’t know the names of the people who watched them pass. Mara had given thirty years to that light. Now it gave nothing back. It just turned, over and over, indifferent as the sea.

A ship was coming. She knew this the way she knew the storm: Finlay had said it, Agnes had felt it, her own body read the pressure in the same way her grandmother had taught her to read it. The Daughters felt it. The current shifted an hour ago. Something is moving. The Daughters were not wrong. They rarely were.

Mara put on her boots. She did not think about it. She had spent three weeks thinking, and thinking had led her nowhere. She knew what the reading meant. She knew what it meant that the message named a survivor. She knew what it meant that Eilidh Gunn was marked, that the sea had already chosen her, that no amount of thinking would change what was coming. She had learned this lesson before. She had learned it in blood and salt water and the faces of eleven men she could not save.

She had spent three weeks watching the harbor for signs of her. There had been no signs. The ferry had run twice, in the lulls between weather systems, and each time Mara had stood at the harbor road and watched the passengers come and go. No Eilidh. No one who looked like Finlay’s daughter, though she had never met the woman, had only seen her once or twice as a child before the girl grew up and left and became a name that Finlay spoke with his teeth closed. Finlay did not say Eilidh’s name. He said she, and he said my daughter, and he said the girl, and he said everything but the word that mattered. Mara had learned to hear what people didn’t say. On this island, that was the only way to survive.

She climbed down the granite steps to the rocks below. The tide was high, driven in by the storm, and the waves were crashing against the cliff face twenty feet up, spray exploding against stone, the whole headland shaking with the impact. Mara had seen this before. She had seen it a hundred times. She climbed down anyway, her boots finding the worn grooves where her own feet had carved the path over forty years. Her father had taught her this descent when she was eight years old. He had held her hand the first time. The second time he had stood at the top and watched. The third time he had let her go alone, and she had never slipped, never fallen, never doubted the stone.

Now she walked to the edge of the flat rock where she had always stopped, the place where the spray was constant and the roar of the sea filled everything, where a person could stand and watch and feel the whole weight of the ocean pressing against them. The waves were enormous. The spray soaked her coat and ran down her face and she did not wipe it away. She stood there, in the storm, in the dark, and she watched.

The lighthouse beam swept past. She watched it go. She had not turned that light on in years. The company kept it running, kept the mechanism turning, kept the light burning in a tower that was no longer hers. She thought about this sometimes. She thought about it now. The light was not hers. The tower was not hers. The sea was not hers. She owned nothing out here except the rocks beneath her feet and the salt in her lungs and the memory of eleven faces that came to her every night in the dark.

She thought about the eleven now.

Their names were in the Registry. She had looked them up once, in the harbor master’s office, in the old records that no one kept anymore. She had stood at the filing cabinet and pulled the folder and read the names and then closed the folder and put it back and never gone back. The names had not helped. They had not made the faces less vivid. They had only given her something to call them that she didn’t want to use, a way of knowing them that felt like violation, like reading someone’s diary, like standing in a room where you had no right to be.

The faces were enough. The faces were too much.

She had watched them die. She had lit the signal fires and watched the ship list and called the mainland and done everything right, everything she had been trained to do, and the rescue boat had been too late, the sea had been too fast, the current had pulled the bodies under before anyone could reach them. Eleven men. She had counted them in the water. She had stopped counting after six because the numbers were too much, because the faces were too much, because she stood in the tower and watched and there was nothing she could do, nothing, the light burning and the ship sinking and the sea taking what the sea wanted and the light doing nothing, doing nothing, the light just burning and the world just happening and her standing there with her hands on the controls and her eyes on the water and her voice saying please, please, please to a god she had stopped believing in twenty years before.

She had not slept that night. She had not slept for many nights after. The faces came to her in the dark, and she learned to live with them, learned to carry them the way she carried everything else, the way the island carried its dead, the way the sea carried its claims. You did not get over these things. You just got used to them. You built a life around them. You woke up every morning and did what needed doing and went to bed every night and carried the weight.

Mara stood on the rocks and spoke to them now. She had never done this before. She had never spoken aloud. She had thought about it. She had thought about it every night for three weeks. But thinking was not the same as doing, and now the storm was here and the ship was coming and Finlay was in the cottage above her watching the window and Agnes was preparing for something that might not work, and Mara was standing on the rocks with the rain in her face and the salt in her throat and nothing left to think.

“I cannot watch again.”

Her voice was small against the wind and the sea. It was taken immediately, absorbed into the roar, and she was grateful for that. She did not want the words to exist beyond the moment. She only wanted to say them.

“I can’t watch again.”

She had watched for thirty years. She had watched the sea take things and she had kept the light burning and she had done everything right and it had never mattered. The light did not save anyone. The light was just a light. It was iron and glass and oil and a mechanism that turned. It did not care who drowned. It did not know who lived. It just burned, over and over, while the world did what the world did.

“I know you’re out there.” She was talking to the eleven. She knew this. She had been talking to them for three weeks, in her head, in the dark, in the cottage with the fire burning and no one to hear. “I know what happened. I know I couldn’t stop it. I lit the fires and the rescue came too late and I stood in the tower and watched and there was nothing I could do. Nothing. I have spent fifteen years trying to forget the weight of that nothing. I thought if I stopped watching, if I let the light go, I could stop carrying it. But the carrying doesn’t stop. The watching doesn’t stop. I see you every night. All eleven of you. You come to me and I don’t know if you’re real or if I’m making you real because I couldn’t let you go.”

The tears came. She did not wipe them away. The rain and the spray and the tears all ran together on her face, salt water and fresh water and the water of the world mixing into something that tasted the same no matter where it came from.

“I can’t do it again.” Her voice broke. She did not care. “I can’t stand in that tower or on these rocks or anywhere and watch someone drown when I know what’s coming. I know the message. I know what it means. There’s a ship coming and a girl is marked and I can’t—I can’t—”

A hand touched her shoulder.

She spun. Her boot slipped on the wet rock and she nearly fell, but the hand caught her arm and held her steady, and she looked up into the face of Agnes, older and smaller than she remembered, her oilskin dark with rain, her eyes steady.

Agnes did not speak. She just held Mara’s arm and waited, the way she had learned to wait through decades of standing with people who had no words left.

Mara wiped her face with the back of her hand. “How long have you been there?”

“Long enough.”

“Finlay.”

“Finlay heard you once, through his wall. He didn’t know what to do with it so he didn’t say anything.” Agnes released her arm but did not step back. “He thinks you don’t know.”

“I didn’t know he could hear.”

“Does it matter?”

Mara looked at the sea. The waves crashed and roared and the spray flew and the lighthouse beam swept past again, indifferent. “No. I suppose it doesn’t.”

“The lighthouse is running. Do you hear it?”

Mara listened. The mechanism hummed through the rock, through her boots, the same sound she had heard every night for twenty-seven years. “Yes.”

“The company keeps it burning for the cargo ships. The channel is narrow here. Without the light, there’d be more wrecks.” Agnes paused. “That’s what it’s for. Not for us. For them.”

“I know.”

“Do you? Because you’ve spent fifteen years avoiding the sound of it. You leave your windows closed when the wind is from the east. You cross the road instead of walking past the tower.” Agnes turned to face her directly. “You’re not avoiding the light. You’re avoiding what it means.”

“It means I can’t do anything.” Mara’s voice was flat. “The light burned and the ship sank and eleven men died and the light kept burning. I lit it and it kept burning and they kept dying and the light didn’t stop it. Nothing stops it.”

“No.” Agnes’s voice was quiet. “Nothing stops it. But some things change the terms.”

The words hung in the air between them. Mara thought of what Agnes had said in the cottage: The readings don’t care what you think. They care what you’re willing to pay.

“What does that mean? Change the terms?”

“Every reading has a cost. That’s what the Daughters have always known. The sea gives a message and the message takes something back. Not the same thing. Not a punishment. A different price.” Agnes reached into her coat and pulled out a small object—a piece of sea glass, green and worn smooth. She held it out to Mara. “My mother found this the day after my brother’s burial. He was named in a reading in 1973. She kept him home from the boats. Made him work the croft. Made him stay.”

Mara looked at the glass. “What happened?”

“He stayed for three years. Then he went to the sea anyway.” Agnes put the glass back in her pocket. “The sea took him the same month he left.”

“So the readings can’t be stopped.”

“They can’t be stopped. But they can be moved.” Agnes turned to face the water. The waves were enormous, the spray rising higher than their heads, but she stood steady, her hands in her coat pockets, her face calm. “My mother paid one price. She kept him home for three years. He was safe. He was alive. And then the sea collected what it was owed, and the debt was paid in a different way.”

“A different way.”

“He drowned in a harbor on the mainland. Different water. Different current. Farther from rescue.” Agnes’s voice was quiet. “The sea doesn’t forget what it claims. If you move the person, the sea moves the danger. The wreck still happens. The death still comes. But the location changes. The circumstances change. And sometimes—” She stopped. The wind screamed around them. The spray soaked them both. The lighthouse beam swept past, again and again, the rhythm of it like a heartbeat.

“Sometimes what?”

“Sometimes the person survives anyway. Not because the reading changes. Because something else happens. Something no one can predict.” Agnes looked at her. “The ritual I’m talking about. It’s not in the Registry. It’s older. It was here before the readings were recorded, before the Daughters had a name, before anyone thought to catalog what the sea was doing. It’s what the first people did when they came to this island. What they did to survive.”

“What does it require?”

“Presence. Attention. You must mean it.”

“That’s all?”

“That’s everything.” Agnes’s voice was gentle. “You have to stand at the edge of the water and name what you’re willing to lose. Not to bargain. Not to buy. Just to acknowledge that the sea takes what it takes, and sometimes we can choose what it takes from us. Sometimes we can give it something freely so it takes that instead.”

“And then?”

“Then the sea decides.” Agnes turned back to the water. “We don’t know what it will do. We never know. The old forms are not reliable. They were never reliable. They just gave people something to do while they waited for the sea to decide. But it’s what we have. It’s what we can offer.”

Mara thought of the eleven faces. She thought of Eilidh Gunn, who might already be dead, who might be coming home to a harbor that would watch her drown. She thought of Finlay in the cottage above them, standing by the window, watching for a light that would never be hers to control again.

“What do I have to do?”

“Tomorrow night. When the storm is overhead. Ellen’s Cove.” Agnes looked at her. “I need to prepare. The ritual requires objects. Words. The words are in Gaelic. I learned them from my mother. She learned them from hers.”

“Will it work?”

“I don’t know.” Agnes’s voice was honest. “No one knows. The old forms are not reliable. But you asked me what you could do, and this is what there is. This is what we have.”

Mara closed her eyes. The rain was cold on her face. The wind pulled at her coat. She could feel the stone beneath her feet, slick and uneven, the same stone her grandmother had stood on and her great-grandmother before that, the same stone the first islanders had used for their rituals before the Registry made it official, before the readings were named and recorded and filed away in buildings no one visited.

She opened her eyes. “What does it cost?”

Agnes was quiet for a long moment. The waves roared and the spray flew and the lighthouse beam swept past again and again, the rhythm of it like a heartbeat, like time passing, like something moving forward whether they were ready or not.

“What you’re willing to pay,” she said.

Mara stood on the rocks, in the storm, in the dark, and she let the question settle into her bones. Not an answer. Not yet. Just the question, the only question that mattered now, the question that had been waiting for her since she found the bottle in the rocks and read the message and understood, in her bones, what it meant.

What was she willing to pay?

The sea waited. The light swept on. Above them, in the cottage, Finlay watched the window for a ship that might not come, for a daughter who might already be gone, for a reading that had already changed everything it touched.

And Mara stood on the rocks with the rain in her hair and the question in her chest and no answer yet, only the shape of the choice she would have to make when the time came, only the knowledge that she could not watch again, could not stand by and let the sea take what it wanted, could not light the fires and watch them fail the way they had failed before.

Something had to change. Someone had to pay.

The question was who. The question was what.

The question was whether she was strong enough to find out.
