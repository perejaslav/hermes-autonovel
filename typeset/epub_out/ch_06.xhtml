Chapter 6

The cottage held the cold the way a box holds water. Once it was in, it did not know how to leave.

Mara stood in the doorway and did not step inside.

She had walked the two miles from the harbor without feeling her feet on the road. The moon was high now, the clouds gone, and the world outside was silver and shadow and the particular stillness that follows rain. Inside, she knew, the fire had gone to ash. She had banked it that morning before she left—the same ritual she had performed every morning for fifteen years, bank the fire, check the tide, walk the shore and count what she could count. She had not expected to come home with a message that could not be unread.

She stepped inside.

The room was dark. She did not reach for the lamp. She crossed to the cold hearth and knelt and put her hands in the ash and the cold climbed up her fingers and into her palms and she let it. Her hands remembered this. Her body remembered what it was to kneel here, in this room, in the quiet, and tend the fire that was the only warmth she had made for herself in fifteen years.

The bottle was in her coat pocket. She had not let go of it since Finlay poured her out the door.

She reached into her pocket and took it out.

The glass was dark in the low light. She could see the edge of the paper inside, the corner of it, curled against the curve of the glass. A message. A name. A date three weeks from now.

She set the bottle on the table beside the cold hearth and sat in the chair she had sat in every night since she moved in here and she looked at it. The bottle did not look back. The bottle did not do anything. The message inside did not change.

She had known it would not.

The rules said: once read, it cannot be unread.

She had read it. She had read it on the shore, kneeling in the gravel, the tide still dripping from her boots. She had read it before she knew what she was reading, the words forming in her hand as if her hand remembered how to write them and had been waiting for the chance. She had seen the name. She had seen the date. She had seen the words that named what was coming, and now she knew, and knowing was a thing that did not undo itself.

She had thought—she had not let herself think it, but the thought had been there, the way the tide is there even when you are not watching it—she had thought maybe if she destroyed the bottle, if she broke the glass and let the paper fall and let the sea take the message back, she would not have to know anymore. The rules said the message could not be unread. They did not say the glass could not be broken.

But she knew. She had always known.

The glass was the delivery system. The message was already in her. The words had already gone from her hand into her mind and from her mind they did not go. She could break the bottle against the stones outside and it would mean nothing. The glass held the message but it did not hold the knowing. She held the knowing. She held it in the place where she kept the faces of the eleven, the place where she kept the sound of the signal fires she had lit and the sound of the rescue boat that came too late, the place where she kept everything she could not unsee.

The bottle was a thing. The knowing was hers.

She looked at the glass on the table. She did not break it.

The fire was ash. She should light it. The room was cold enough to see her breath if she looked at it sideways, the way the air moved when she breathed out, the way the cold made the world visible. She should light the fire. She did not light the fire.

She thought about what she could do.

Destroy the bottle. She had already decided this did nothing. The knowing would remain. The message would still be true. The name would still be the name.

Tell Finlay. Tell him what she had seen. Tell him what was coming. Finlay, who kept the Inn and kept the League and kept trying to make the island survive by making it practical, Finlay who did not believe in readings because belief would mean his life was already determined and Finlay could not live inside a life that was determined, Finlay who would say she was daft or sad or both, Finlay who would not listen until the listening did not matter anymore.

Tell Eilidh. Call her on the phone in the morning, the phone that sat on the shelf by the door where she had put it because she had never needed it, call and tell her what the message said, tell her to stay away, tell her not to come home, tell her what was waiting if she came home. Eilidh, who had left the island and stayed away and built a life that was not here, Eilidh who would not believe, who would think her father had put Mara up to it or who would think Mara was mad, Eilidh who would hang up and not come home out of spite if nothing else.

Do nothing. Set the bottle aside. Live the next three weeks the way she had lived the last fifteen. Walk the shore. Count the boats. Watch the sea. Let what was coming come.

This was the option she had chosen.

She had chosen it every year since 1998.

She sat in the chair and the cold sat with her and she thought: I cannot unknow the name. The name was Eilidh. The name was in her hand and in her mind and it would not leave. Eilidh. Finlay’s daughter. The woman who came home for holidays she treated like obligations. The woman who had left because she could not stay and stayed away because she could not leave.

The name was a person. The person was going to drown.

Unless.

Unless was the word she could not say out loud.

Unless was the thing she had spent fifteen years learning not to think.

In 1998, she had done everything right. She had climbed the tower when the storm came in. She had lit the signal fires. She had seen the freighter in trouble two miles out, lights still burning, running lights still showing the shape of her against the black of the sea and the blacker of the night. She had signaled. She had watched the mainland rescue boat leave the harbor. She had tracked it on the radar screen that was the only modern thing in the tower, the green line sweeping around and around and showing her where the boat was and where the freighter was and the space between them that was shrinking, then not shrinking, then growing.

The rescue boat had sunk.

She did not know why. The official report said a wave, a rogue wave that no one had predicted, a wall of water that hit the boat broadside and drove it under before anyone could move. The boat was gone. The freighter was gone. Eleven men were gone. She had lit the fires. She had done everything right. The eleven were still dead.

She had watched. She had acted. She had done what the light was for.

The sea had taken them anyway.

She had learned the lesson the sea was teaching. She had learned it in the months after, in the gray mornings and the gray evenings and the nights that were not gray but were the color of the space between stars. She had learned that watching was all she could do. That the sea did not care about the light. That the light existed because the light was supposed to exist and the sea took what it took and the light could not stop it, had never been able to stop it, was never meant to stop it, only meant to mark the place where the taking happened.

She had learned this. She had learned it so well she had become it.

She sat in the cold cottage and she thought about the eleven and the lesson and the light and the three weeks and the name that would not leave her, and she felt the thing she had learned begin to crack.

Ships that should have rescued them sink.

The words were in the message. She had read them. She had read them and they had passed through her like the tide passes through a seawall, like the cold passes through stone.

Ships that should have rescued them sink. Ropes that should have held break. The sea does not forget what it has claimed.

She knew what this meant. She had always known what this meant, even before she found the bottle, even before the words formed in her hand and gave her the name. The survivor clause. That was what the scholars called it, in the Registry, in the records they kept in the capital and updated every year and sent out to the islands when they remembered. The survivor clause.

When a reading names a specific person as the only survivor, that person is marked. The sea has already decided. The rescue will not come. The rope will break. The ship will sink.

If Finlay tried to save his daughter, he would fail. If he sent a boat, it would sink. If he did everything right—

She stopped the thought.

She had done everything right in 1998.

She thought about Eilidh.

She thought about the girl who had followed her to the shore when she was small, when the weekenders were here and Eilidh was home from school, when she had nowhere to be and no one to answer to and she would find Mara walking the rocks and she would walk beside her and ask questions Mara did not know how to answer. Why does the light turn? How high can the waves get? Do you ever get lonely up here?

Mara had not answered. She had walked and Eilidh had walked beside her and the questions had hung in the air like the gulls hung in the air, going nowhere, going everywhere, the questions not needing answers because the questions were just the shape of someone trying to understand a place that did not want to be understood.

The girl had grown. The woman had left.

Mara had watched her go. She had stood at the harbor the day Eilidh left for university, the same harbor where she had stood today, and she had watched the ferry pull away with the woman who had asked her about loneliness and loneliness was not the word for what Mara was, loneliness was the word for people who missed other people, and Mara did not miss anyone because she had learned not to miss, had learned to let the faces come and go like the tide, like the light, like the ships that came and went and sometimes did not come back.

Eilidh had not looked back.

Mara had not expected her to.

She stood up.

She crossed to the cold hearth and knelt in front of it and lifted the iron grate and set the ash aside and found the kindling she kept there, in the old way, in the way her grandmother had shown her, kindling first, then peat, then the coal that sat in the bucket by the door. She lit the match. She held it to the kindling and the kindling caught and she sat back on her heels and watched the small fire grow.

The room filled with light. The room filled with warmth. The room filled with the smell of smoke and the memory of smoke, the smell of every fire she had ever lit in this room or the room above or the room in the tower that held the light, the smell of heat and ash and the oil that kept the mechanism running, the smell of a life that had been made of keeping light in the dark.

She watched the fire grow.

She thought: three weeks.

She thought: ships that should have rescued them sink.

She thought: I cannot unknow the name.

She thought: the eleven are still dead.

She thought: Finlay will try.

She thought: I have spent fifteen years learning not to act.

She thought: I am going to have to unlearn everything.

She sat by the fire and she did not sleep.

The night passed the way nights pass when you are waiting for something that is not going to come. The fire burned down. She did not bank it. The room went dark again. The first light came through the window, gray light, the light that was neither night nor day, the light that was the edge of the world.

She had not made a decision. She did not know how to make a decision. The decision was the thing she had spent fifteen years learning to stop making, the decision was the thing she had built a life around not having to make, the decision was—

The decision was whether to tell someone.

That was all it was. That was all it could be. She could not stop the shipwreck. She could not save Eilidh from the sea. She could not control what the sea did with what it had claimed. She could only decide whether to say the words out loud, in a room, to a person who might listen or might not, might believe or might not, might try or might not.

She could tell Finlay. She could tell him the name and the date and the clause about the ships that should have rescued them. She could watch him not believe. She could watch him try anyway.

She could tell Eilidh. She could call the number she did not have and find the number she did not want to find and make the call she did not want to make. She could tell the woman who had left and stayed away and dreamed about boats that shouldn’t float. She could warn her. She could not make her listen.

She could do nothing. She could let the three weeks pass. She could watch the sea do what the sea did. She could keep the light, such as it was, burning in her own chest, where no one could see it, where it would go out when she went out, and that would be the end of it. That would be the lesson she had learned. That would be the lesson she had not unlearned.

The gray light grew. The room showed its shapes again: the table, the chair, the hearth, the bottle on the table with the message that would not change.

She looked at the bottle.

She thought: I do not know if I believe this will come true.

Then she thought: that is not the question.

The question was not whether she believed.

The question was what she would do with the knowing.

She had not known in 1998. She had lit the fires and she had watched and she had done everything right and the eleven had still died because the rescue boat had sunk, because the sea had claimed them, because the sea did not care about the light or the keeper or the keeper’s wanting the world to be different than it was. She had not known what was coming. She had not been able to stop it.

She knew now.

The knowing was not a gift. The knowing was not a curse. The knowing was just a thing she had, like the cold in her bones, like the smell of smoke in her hair, like the faces of the eleven that would not leave her alone at night.

She knew. And she was the only one who knew.

And she was going to have to decide what to do with that.

She stood.

She went to the window.

The harbor was visible from here, if she looked through the gap between the cottage and the shed, if she stood on her toes and angled her body just so. She could see the water, the moored boats, the shape of the world she had lived in for sixty-four years. The sky was lightening. The color was coming back to the world. The day was coming.

She had three weeks.

She turned from the window. She crossed the room. She picked up the bottle and held it in her hand. The glass was cold. The paper inside was cold. The message would not change.

She thought about Finlay. She thought about Eilidh. She thought about Agnes, who led the Daughters, who knew the old rituals, who had received a reading herself and never spoken of it. She thought about what it might cost to ask.

She did not know yet. She did not know what she would do. She did not know if she would call or walk or sit still or speak or be silent.

But she knew she could not do nothing.

This was the thing she had not known when she sat down. This was the thing the night had given her. She could not unknow the name, and she could not unknow what the name meant, and she could not unknow that she was the only one who knew, and she could not unknow that the knowing was a weight she would have to carry or set down, and setting it down would not make it lighter, setting it down would only mean she was not carrying it when it mattered.

She set the bottle on the table.

She lit the fire.

She sat in the chair and she watched the light grow, and she began to plan.

The question the chapter ended on was not whether she believed the reading would come true.

The question was whether she believed she could do anything about it.

The fire burned. The day began. Somewhere beyond the island, beyond the sea, Eilidh Gunn slept in a flat in Edinburgh and dreamed about boats that shouldn’t float, and she did not know that Mara Callaghan was sitting in a cold cottage with a message that could not be unread, and she did not know that she had three weeks, and she did not know that her father would try to save her and fail, and she did not know that the sea was waiting.

Mara sat by the fire.

She began.
