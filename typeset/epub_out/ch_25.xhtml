Chapter 25: The Harbor

The ferry came at half past noon.

Eilidh had counted the boats in the harbor that morning—she did this without meaning to, the way her father counted them, the way everyone on Torran counted them, a tally that was never finished because the tally was the point. Six boats in the water. Two in the dry dock. The supply ferry tied at the far buoy, its hull dark with fuel oil, its engine growling low in the morning calm.

The waves were still high. She could see them from the Inn’s window, white-capped and restless, the swells from three days of storm not yet settled into the long Atlantic rhythm that would eventually calm them. The weather window had opened, Mara said. The coast guard had cleared the route. But the sea remembered the storm even when the sky had forgotten it, and the water knew what it had done.

Eilidh put her pack on her shoulder. She had packed that morning, quickly, the way you pack when you want to leave before you’re sure you want to leave. Her father’s jacket was folded on the chair where she’d left it. She hadn’t worn it. She wouldn’t wear it. That was a choice she was making, one of many she was making, and she would not unpick it by taking comfort in wool that smelled like peat smoke and old men’s tobacco.

The common room was empty. The weekenders had gone out walking, toward the cairn or the hidden bay on the north side where the seals gathered. The fishermen had gone to their boats. Her father was in the kitchen, scraping something, the sound of metal on metal carrying through the closed door.

She walked out without telling him.

The harbor was a hundred yards from the Inn, down the single street that was more packed earth than road, and Eilidh walked it in five minutes because she had walked it every day of her childhood and her body remembered the way even when she wanted to forget. The smell came first—diesel and fish oil and the particular green of boat paint that had been applied too many times. Then the sound: seagulls arguing over something in the shallows, the slap of water against hulls, the low thrum of the ferry’s engine idling at the buoy.

Finlay was standing at the end of the pier.

She had known he would be there. She had hoped he would be there and she had dreaded it, and now she was doing both things at once, her chest tight with something she couldn’t name. He was looking at the ferry, at the water, at the sky. He was not looking at her until she was close enough that looking away would have meant something, and then he turned, and his face was the face she had known for thirty-two years, and it was also a face she no longer recognized.

“The ferry’s late,” he said.

“I know.”

“The tide’s not quite right. They’re waiting for the next swell.”

“I know.”

She stopped a few feet from him. The harbor stones were rough under her boots—old granite, worn smooth by a hundred years of boots and weather and the slow grinding of the sea. She had played on these stones as a child. She had scraped her knees on them. She had learned to swim from the buoy line, her father’s hands under her arms, his voice counting backward until he let go.

She did not say goodbye.

There was nothing to say. She had said everything that mattered in the Inn, in the room that smelled of cooking and bodies and the sea that had made them all. She had said she came home. She had said she didn’t know why. She had said she would rather lose something than lose everything, and she had meant it, and now the meaning of it was waiting on the water in the form of a ferry that was already thirty minutes late.

Finlay looked at her for a long moment. His hands were at his sides. He did not reach for her. That was a choice he was making.

“The reading said you survive,” he said.

“I know.”

“That doesn’t mean anything has to change.”

“I know.”

Another moment. The ferry’s engine growled, a low idling sound that vibrated through the stones and up into her boots. She could feel it in her feet, the ferry’s restlessness, the way it wanted to be moving.

“I’ll be at the Inn,” Finlay said. “When you get to the mainland. I’ll call. We’ll talk.”

“Sure.”

This was what they did. They made plans that they both knew were lies. They talked about the future as though the future were a place they would both reach, and they did not talk about the water between now and then, the cold gray swells that were still remembering the storm.

Eilidh turned and walked toward the ferry.

The gangplank was down, a narrow wooden bridge from the pier to the ferry’s deck, and she crossed it with her pack on her shoulder and her boots loud on the weathered boards. The deck was empty except for the captain, a man she didn’t know, someone from the mainland who drove the ferry back and forth and probably thought Torran was a small and slightly ridiculous place where people counted boats and believed in messages in bottles.

She found a seat at the stern. The benches were bolted to the deck, salt-stained, comfortable in the way that hard things become comfortable when you’ve sat in them enough times. The gunwale was low enough that she could lean her arms on it, rest her chin on her hands, watch the island recede.

The engine revved. The ferry pulled away from the buoy, turned in a wide arc, and pointed its bow toward the mainland.

Torran fell behind her.

She watched the lighthouse first. It stood on its promontory, sixty-two feet of stone above the waterline, and from this distance it was just a white tower against the gray sky, a mark on the horizon that told you where you were. Mara would be at her cottage. Mara would be watching the ferry come in or go out, the way she always watched, the way she had watched for thirty years before she stopped keeping the light.

Then the harbor shrank. The Inn became a smudge of stone and thatch. The street became a line. Her father’s figure on the pier became a dot, and then there was no dot, there was only the sea, and she was on the water, and the water was deep, and the waves were still high.

The first twenty minutes were uneventful.

Eilidh sat at the stern and watched the island shrink. The mainland grew on the horizon, a dark line that was more promise than land, the place where she had built a life that had nothing to do with tide and storm and her father’s counting. She was going back. She was going to be a person who happened to have grown up on an island, not an island person who happened to live elsewhere. She was going to be free.

The sun broke through the clouds. The water went from gray to silver, and the silver was blinding, and for a moment she let herself feel something like hope.

Then the engine made a sound she had never heard before.

It was not a growl anymore. It was a cough, a stutter, a failure of something that should not have been able to fail. The ferry lurched, just slightly, and the sound changed, and then there was silence where the engine had been, and the ferry began to drift.

Eilidh sat up.

The deck was tilting. Not much—not yet—but enough that she could feel it in her boots, the shift of weight, the wrongness of a boat that should have been level and wasn’t. She stood and held the gunwale and looked forward. The captain was at the wheel, his hands moving over controls that were not responding. A crewman—the only other person on board—was shouting something, but the words were lost in the wind and the sudden silence of a boat that had stopped making the sounds a boat should make.

“Engine’s gone,” someone said. She didn’t know who. “She’s taking on water.”

The ferry listed further.

Eilidh moved to the side and looked at the hull. She couldn’t see damage from here—couldn’t see anything except the way the ferry was settling, the way it was leaning to one side like something tired, like something that had decided to stop fighting. The deck beneath her feet was wet now, spray from waves that were catching them broadside, and the swells that were still high were no longer something to be respected but something to be feared.

The captain was shouting again. “Life jackets. Under the seats. Everyone put on a life jacket.”

There was no one to hear him except Eilidh and the crewman. The ferry was a supply boat, not a passenger vessel—it carried cargo in the forward hold and a handful of people in the stern, and on this crossing there were only three of them. The captain. The crewman. And her.

Eilidh opened the compartment under her seat. The life jacket was orange, standard issue, the kind that would keep her head above water if she had the strength to keep her head above water, if the cold didn’t take that strength first. She pulled it on and clipped the straps and did the things you do when you are on a boat that is sinking, things you have rehearsed in your mind a hundred times and never thought you would actually do.

The ferry rolled.

It happened fast. One moment the deck was beneath her feet, sloping but stable. The next moment it wasn’t, and she was sliding, and the world turned, and the water came up to meet her.

Cold.

The cold was a shock that knocked the breath out of her before she understood what was happening. It hit her face, her hands, her whole body, and for a moment she couldn’t move, couldn’t think, could only feel the sharpness of water that remembered winter even when the sky was mild. She went under. The gray light of the surface was above her, and she kicked toward it, and her lungs were burning, and she broke the surface and gasped and went under again.

The water was deep here. She knew this without knowing how she knew it—the way her father had taught her to read water, the way she had learned to feel the slope of a bottom and the pull of a current. The ferry was in water that was deeper than the hull was long, and it was going down, and she was in that water, and her life jacket was holding her up but the waves were high and the cold was getting deeper and the gray sky was above her and she was alone.

She was not alone for long.

She saw the boats first—two small shapes on the horizon, moving fast, coming toward her. She understood what they were even before she understood what was happening. The harbor at Torran had sent its boats. Her father had called them, or someone had called them, and the boats were coming, and rescue was coming, and she would be fine.

The first boat capsized a hundred yards from her.

She saw it happen. One moment the boat was riding a swell, its hull steady, its small engine pushing it through the water. The next moment it was over, turned on its side, and the people in it were in the water, and the boat was sinking, and the people were struggling in the gray waves that were still high and still hungry.

The second boat didn’t capsize. It came on, fast, its engine straining against a swell that was too much for a small boat to take. It reached her. She saw hands reaching for her, faces that were trying to be calm, the crewman’s hand gripping her arm and pulling her toward the boat’s side. She was climbing out of the water. She was getting into the boat. She was alive.

Behind her, the ferry went under.

She heard it—the sound of a boat sinking, the gurgle and rush of water filling a space that had been holding air. She turned and saw the bubbles rising, saw the surface breaking and reforming, saw the place where the ferry had been and where the ferry was no longer. The water closed over it. Within minutes, there was nothing left but a slick of diesel and the memory of something that had existed and no longer did.

The crewman was holding her arm. The man who had pulled her from the water was talking to the man at the boat’s tiller, words she couldn’t quite hear, words about the other boat, about the people in the water, about the rescue that was not going to plan.

“How many?” she asked. Her voice was hoarse. Her teeth were chattering. The cold was in her bones now, and she was shaking, and she was alive.

“Three,” the crewman said. “Three in the water. The first boat—”

“What about the first boat?”

The man at the tiller answered. “She’s gone under. Took on too much water. The people—”

He didn’t finish. He didn’t need to finish. Eilidh looked at the water where the first boat had been. The swells were breaking over something—a hull, a person, she couldn’t tell—and the current was pulling everything toward the deeper water where the ferry had gone down, and the gray was swallowing everything that fell into it.

The people in the water were gone.

She was in the boat. She was alive. She had been pulled out, and the ferry had sunk, and the boats that had come to rescue her had not all arrived, and she was the only one who had been saved.

The reading said she survived.

She lay in the bottom of the rescue boat and felt the cold water in her boots and the shaking in her hands and the gray sky above her, and she understood that she had survived not because the boats had come but despite the boats that had come. The first boat had sunk. The people who had tried to save her had drowned. The second boat had reached her, and she did not know why, and she could not ask, because the man at the tiller was looking at the water where his friends had gone and he was not speaking and his face was the face of a man who had lost something he would never be able to name.

The sea keeps what it has claimed.

The harbor at Torran grew larger as they approached. Eilidh saw the pier, the stones, the gathering crowd—fishermen and weekenders and her father standing at the water’s edge with his hands at his sides and his face the face of a man who did not know whether to be relieved or destroyed. The boat scraped against the pier. Hands reached down. She was lifted out of the boat and set on the harbor stones, and she was on her knees, and she was shaking, and she was alive.

She looked up at her father.

He didn’t move. He didn’t speak. He stood there with the harbor behind him and the island that had made them both and the sea that was still taking things, and he looked at his daughter who had come home and almost died and survived because the reading had said she would survive, and his face was the face of a man who finally understood that he could not hold on.

Eilidh lay back on the stones.

The sky was gray. The clouds had not come back, but they had not cleared either—they were holding, suspended, the way the sea was holding, the way everything on this island was holding its breath and waiting for something to change. She watched the sky. She felt the cold in her boots and the wet in her coat and the shaking that would not stop, and she breathed, and she was alive, and she knew what she had paid.

The harbor was silent. The crowd was silent. Her father was silent. Someone had put a blanket over her shoulders and someone had brought a flask of something hot and someone had called for a doctor, but Eilidh didn’t move. She lay on the stones and watched the sky and breathed and understood that she would never leave again.

Not because she couldn’t. Because the reading had named her, and the sea had taken her price, and the price was the home she had almost found. The boats that should have saved her had sunk. The people who had reached for her had drowned. The sea did not forget what it had claimed, and it had claimed her, and she would live, but she would live here, on this island, in the wake of a wreck that had killed four people to keep her breathing.

She watched the sky. The clouds were thinning. Somewhere to the west, the sun was breaking through.

Eilidh closed her eyes.

Behind her, the harbor held its breath. Behind her, her father stood at the water’s edge and finally let go. Behind her, the sea was quiet, and it was waiting, and it had taken what it was owed.

She was alive.

She would never be whole.
