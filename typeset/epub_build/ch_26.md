# Chapter 26: The Price Paid


She was alive.

She would never be whole.

The doctor had come and gone. Someone had given her dry clothes. Someone had put her in a chair by the fire in the back room of the Inn, and she sat there with a blanket over her shoulders and a cup of tea in her hands that she had not yet tasted, and she watched the steam rise and fall and rise and fall, and she understood.

The harbor was quiet now. The boats that had come out for her had gone back to their moorings. The crowd that had gathered on the stones had dispersed to their houses and their suppers and their evenings that would end in sleep and dreams of other things. Four men were dead. She knew this without being told. She had seen their faces in the water. She had seen the sea take them.

She set the tea down on the table beside her. Her hands were shaking. They had not stopped shaking since she had come up out of the water, and she did not think they would stop again.

The door opened.

Finlay stood in the doorway.

He looked older than he had that morning. He looked like a man who had been awake for a very long time and had reached the part of the night where sleep was no longer possible and morning was no longer a relief. He stepped into the room and closed the door behind him and stood there, looking at her, and she looked back at him, and neither of them spoke.

The fire crackled. Somewhere in the front of the Inn, a chair scraped against the floor. Someone laughed, quietly, and then stopped.

"Eilidh," he said.

"Dad."

He crossed the room and sat down in the chair across from her. The fire was between them. The light it threw made shadows on his face that she had never noticed before—lines around his eyes, a heaviness to his jaw. She had been looking at him wrong, she realized. She had been looking at him the way she had looked at the island for years, seeing only what she needed to see and refusing to see the rest.

She had not known he had so many shadows.

"The doctor said you can travel," he said. "Tomorrow. The ferry will run."

She nodded. She had known this already. She had known it before she had come up out of the water, before the boats had reached her, before the rope had broken and the sea had taken the men who were trying to save her. She had known it the way she knew her own name. She was going to leave. She had always been going to leave.

"I can't stay," she said.

The words came out quieter than she had intended. She had thought she would have to force them, that they would stick in her throat like bones. But they came out easily, almost gently, and she did not know what to do with that.

"I thought I could," she said. "When I came back. I thought maybe I could. I thought maybe this time—"

She stopped. She looked at the fire. The flames were low. The peat was burning down.

"I lost the island," she said. "I lost the only chance I had to belong here."

Finlay did not speak. He sat in his chair with his hands on his knees and his eyes on her face, and she could see him understanding. She could see him putting the pieces together the way he put together problems with the boats, the way he looked at a broken engine and saw not just what was wrong but what had been wrong all along and what would be wrong in the future if he did not fix it now.

She watched him understand, and she waited for him to argue.

He did not.

"The sea took you," he said. "When you were in the water. It took you and it didn't give you back."

"I don't know what it gave me back," she said. "I don't know what I'm carrying now. But I know I can't stay. I know if I stay here I'll—"

She stopped again. She did not know how to finish that sentence. She did not know what would happen if she stayed. She only knew that something had changed in the water, something had shifted, and the island that had almost been home was not home anymore. It was something else. Something that held her wreck and her survival and four men who would never go home to their wives and children, and she could not live in the middle of that. She could not wake up every morning and see the harbor where it had happened and know that she was the reason.

"I knew," Finlay said.

She looked at him.

"When you were in the water," he said. "I knew. I knew the sea was going to take you, and I would have lost everything."

His voice was steady. That was the strange thing. She had expected him to break, to cry, to argue with her the way he argued with everything that did not fit into his understanding of how the world should work. But his voice was steady, and his hands were still on his knees, and he looked at her with something in his eyes that she had never seen before.

She did not know what it was.

"I can't stop you," he said. "I couldn't stop you before, and I can't stop you now. I've been trying to hold on to you since your mother died, and all I've done is push you further away."

"Dad—"

"Let me finish."

She closed her mouth.

"I've been telling myself I was protecting you. Keeping you safe. Making sure you didn't make the same mistakes I made, or your mother's mistakes, or the mistakes that everyone on this island has been making for generations. But that's not what I was doing." He paused. The fire popped, sending a spray of sparks up the chimney. "I was trying to keep you because I couldn't keep her. And that was never fair to you."

Eilidh felt something crack in her chest. It was not a physical sensation, but it felt physical—a fissure running through something she had thought was solid, something she had been carrying for so long she had forgotten it was there.

"She was sick for a year," he said. "She didn't tell anyone. She didn't want to worry us. And I didn't see it. I was so busy with the Inn and the League and all the things I thought mattered that I didn't see my own wife dying in our bed."

"Dad. That wasn't—"

"It was. It was my fault, and I couldn't face that, so I found something else to blame. I found you." He looked at her, and his eyes were wet, but his voice did not waver. "I blamed you for leaving. I blamed you for not being here. I blamed you for every part of her death that I couldn't control, and I made you carry it, and I'm sorry."

She did not know what to say. She had spent years waiting for him to say those words, and now that he had said them, she did not know what to do with them. They sat in the air between them like something living, something that could not be put back into a bottle once it had been opened.

"The ferry leaves at nine," he said. "I'll make sure your things are brought down. I'll be at the harbor to see you off."

"You don't have to."

"I know. That's why I'm going."

He stood up. He looked at her for a long moment, and she saw him deciding something—not whether to let her go, that decision had already been made, but something else. Something she could not read.

"Finlay," she said. "The men who died—"

"I know."

"Who are they? Who do I—"

"You don't have to do anything." His voice was firm. "The Daughters will see to the rites. The families will be looked after. That's not your burden to carry."

"It was my fault."

"No." He shook his head. "It was the sea's. It was the reading's. It was every force in this world that takes what it wants and doesn't care who it breaks. You're alive because you were meant to be alive. That's not a crime, Eilidh. That's not something you have to pay for."

She opened her mouth to argue, but he was already turning away. He walked to the door and put his hand on the frame and stopped, and she saw his shoulders rise and fall with a breath that seemed to come from somewhere very deep.

"I'll see you in the morning," he said.

The door closed behind him.

Eilidh sat alone in the firelit room, and she did not move for a very long time.


The morning came gray and still.

Eilidh stood at the harbor's edge with her bag at her feet and watched the ferry approach. It was a small boat, white-sided, the kind she had taken a hundred times before. The kind that had carried her away to university and to her flat in Edinburgh and to the life she had built on the mainland, the life that was hers and no one else's, the life she had thought she wanted.

The harbor was quiet. A few fishermen moved along the pier, checking nets, coiling rope. No one looked at her. No one spoke to her. She was grateful for this. She did not think she could bear to be looked at, to be wondered about, to be the woman who had come back from the dead and cost four men their lives in the bargain.

Finlay stood beside her. He had not said much during the walk from the Inn. Neither had she. They had walked through streets that felt strange to her now, streets she had known since childhood but which seemed to belong to someone else, someone who had grown up here and left and come back and died in the water and been given back with something essential missing.

He picked up her bag without asking. She let him.

They walked to the end of the pier together, and the ferry came alongside, and the deckhand lowered the ramp, and Eilidh stepped aboard.

The deck was wet with spray. The smell was diesel and salt and something else, something she could not name—something that lived in the planks, in the ropes, in the memory of every crossing she had ever made. She walked to the rail and gripped it and looked back at the island.

Torran.

The harbor was silver in the morning light. The hills rose behind it, green and gray. The lighthouse stood on its promontory, white and silent, and Eilidh looked at it and felt nothing, and that nothing was the worst thing of all.

She had loved this place. She had hated it, too, had fought against it, had spent years trying to convince herself that she was not shaped by it. But she had loved it. She had carried it with her to Edinburgh and to every job and every flat and every relationship that had ended badly, and she had carried it still, because it was hers, because it had made her, because no matter how far she ran she could not outpace the place that had written itself into her bones.

Now it was gone.

She watched the island shrink.

The harbor grew smaller. The lighthouse grew smaller. The hills folded into each other, became a single shape against the sky, became a smudge on the horizon, became nothing.

She did not cry. She did not think she had any tears left. She stood at the rail and watched and felt the ferry rise and fall beneath her feet, and she understood, finally, what the reading had cost.

Not her life. The reading had given her that. It had kept its bargain and let her live.

But the island—the island was gone. The home she had almost reclaimed, the place where her mother was buried, the place where her father still stood at the water's edge and waited for her to come back. The reading had taken all of that and fed it to the sea, and the sea had swallowed it, and now she was going home to a city that did not know her, to a flat that did not feel like anything, to a life that was hers and no one else's and no one's at all.

The ferry chugged on.

Behind her, Torran disappeared beneath the edge of the world.

Ahead, the mainland rose out of the sea, gray and green and familiar in a way that felt like a lie.

Eilidh closed her eyes.

She breathed in salt air.

She did not look back again.


The crossing took two hours.

Eilidh spent them in the cabin, curled in a seat by the window, watching the water. The sea was calm this morning—calmer than it had any right to be, calmer than she trusted. She kept expecting the waves to rise, the wind to shift, the sky to darken. She kept expecting the sea to take something else, to remind her that she was still in its domain, that she had not escaped, that she would never escape.

But the water stayed smooth, and the sun broke through the clouds, and the mainland grew closer, and eventually the ferry glided into the harbor at Ullapool, and the deckhand called out the arrival, and Eilidh gathered her bag and walked off the boat.

The pier was crowded. People with suitcases, people with dogs, people with children. She moved through them like a ghost, unnoticed, unremarkable. Just another passenger. Just another person who had arrived somewhere after being somewhere else.

She found a bench at the edge of the harbor and sat down.

The sun was warm on her face. The water lapped at the stones below her feet. Somewhere behind her, the ferry was being refueled, and people were disembarking, and life was continuing in all its ordinary, relentless ways.

She thought about calling someone. She had friends in Edinburgh, colleagues who would want to know she was back, who would ask questions she did not want to answer. She thought about calling her flatmate, about going home, about sliding back into the life she had built and pretending none of this had happened.

She thought about the men in the water. She thought about their faces. She thought about their wives and children waiting for news that would not come.

She thought about her mother.

She thought about the way her mother had looked in the hospital, at the end, when the sickness had finally been too much to hide. Her mother had been so small in that bed. So thin. Her mother, who had always seemed so large, so certain, so much a part of the island itself that it was hard to believe she could be diminished by anything.

But she had been diminished. She had died. And Eilidh had not been there, not really, not in any way that mattered, and she had come back for the funeral and left again and not come back until the reading had called her home, and now she was leaving again, and her mother was still in the ground on that island, and her father was still standing at the water's edge, and nothing had changed.

Everything had changed.

Eilidh stood up.

She picked up her bag.

She walked toward the bus station, toward the long road south, toward Edinburgh and her flat and her life.

She did not look back.


The bus was half-empty.

Eilidh took a seat near the back and put her bag on the rack above her and pressed her forehead against the window and watched the landscape scroll past. The hills rose and fell. The lochs caught the light. The small towns came and went, each one a cluster of buildings that seemed to exist outside of time, outside of the world she had left behind.

She slept, eventually. She did not mean to, but the motion of the bus was soothing, and the sun was warm through the glass, and her body had finally decided that it was safe to stop fighting, to stop waiting for the next blow to fall.

She dreamed of water.

She dreamed of being in the sea, not drowning, just floating. The water was dark and cold and held her up, and she could see the sky above, gray and high and very far away. She could see the bottom of a boat above her, the hull of the ferry, the people she could not reach. She could see the shadows of fish moving in the deep. She could see, far below her, the shapes of things the sea had kept.

She woke up when the bus stopped.

The driver called out the name of a town she did not recognize. No one else got on. The bus sat there for five minutes while the driver smoked a cigarette by the door, and Eilidh sat in her seat and breathed and tried to remember how to be a person who was going somewhere instead of a person who was running away.

The bus started again.

She watched the road unspool ahead of her.

She thought about Finlay standing at the harbor, watching the ferry leave. She thought about Mara climbing the tower, climbing into the light. She thought about Agnes in the vestry, naming the dead, performing the rites that had been performed for generations. She thought about all the people on that island who had been touched by the reading, who had paid its price, who would go on living in its wake.

She thought about survival.

Was it enough?

She did not know. She did not think she would ever know. But she was alive, and she was going forward, and the road was long and the sun was setting and somewhere ahead of her there was a city and a flat and a life that was waiting to be lived.

She closed her eyes.

The bus carried her south.

The island was gone.
