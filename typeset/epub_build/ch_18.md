# Chapter 18

The path to the cairn crossed three streams and climbed through a stand of bent pines that had been growing since before anyone on Torran could remember. Mara walked it in the gray light of early afternoon, the sky heavy with cloud that would not decide whether to rain or stay dry. The wind came from the northwest, carrying the smell of the open sea, and she walked into it the way she had walked into everything for the past fifteen years: without hurry, without expectation, ready for whatever the weather decided to bring.

She was not ready for this.

The cairn sat on a rise of ground that had once been an island, before the strait between Torran and the mainland silted up and became a causeway. The oldest families said the first settlers had buried their dead here, before they knew the island's name or understood what the name meant. *Torran*. Place where the tide turns. They had named it for what they saw from this hill—the way the water rushed in and then rushed out, the way the current shifted twice a day without fail, the way the sea could take and the sea could give, and no one ever knew which it would do until the tide had turned.

The cairn was a mound of stones covered in grass, older than the lighthouse, older than the village, older than the language people used to argue about who owned what. A ring of standing stones marked its base, brought up from the beach in an age when moving stones was something a community did together, when the work of moving them was a kind of prayer.

Catriona Muirhead stood at the center of the ring, wrapped in a black coat that had been her mother's, her white hair pulled back from a face that had not softened in the seventy-one years it had been taking the wind. She was not alone.

Mara counted them as she approached. The three families of the Cairn—the Muirheads, the Camshields, the MacDonalds—had sent their elders. Dougie Camshield, who owned the second boat that still worked. Moira MacDonald, who had not left the island since her husband drowned in eighty-nine and who spoke to the sea like it could hear her. Donald Muirhead, Catriona's nephew, who had come back from Aberdeen for the funeral and stayed for reasons no one understood. Fourteen people in all, standing in a loose semicircle with their backs to the wind.

Agnes was there. She stood at the edge of the group, her hands folded in front of her, her face the color of old paper. She had been the first to arrive, Mara guessed. She always arrived early for things like this. The Daughters of the Deep understood that presence was its own kind of statement.

Finlay was there. He stood apart from the others, near the path, his shoulders hunched against the wind. He had not wanted to come. Mara could see it in the set of his jaw, the way he kept looking back toward the village as though the harbor could still give him an excuse to leave. But he was here, which meant Catriona had asked, and Finlay had learned long ago that the Cairn families did not ask twice.

And Eilidh.

She stood next to her father, her coat pulled tight around her, her hair wet from the drizzle. She did not look at the people gathered around the cairn. She looked at the stones, the grass, the sky, anything but the faces turned toward her. She had come because Finlay asked, and she had come anyway, and she did not know why it mattered, and that not-knowing was written on every line of her body like tide marks on a cliff face.

Mara stopped at the edge of the ring.

"Mara," Catriona said. Not a greeting. An acknowledgment. "You received the message."

"I did."

"And you came."

"I came."

The wind picked up. It moved through the standing stones with a sound like a voice calling across water, and the grass at the base of the cairn bent and rose and bent again. The cloud overhead had thickened, and the light had gone from gray to the color of slate, and in that light the faces around the cairn looked older than they were. Older and harder and more afraid than they wanted anyone to see.

Catriona turned to address the group. Her voice carried in the way that voices carried on Torran, shaped by wind and stone and the particular acoustics of a landscape that had been listening to human speech for a hundred and seventy years.

"A reading has named one of ours." She did not look at Eilidh. She did not need to. "The community must decide how to respond."

The silence that followed was the kind that had weight. It pressed against the ears, against the chest, against the space between people where words usually lived. Mara watched the faces around the cairn. Dougie Camshield was looking at the ground. Moira MacDonald was looking at the sea, or at the direction of the sea, her lips moving slightly as though she were already speaking to it. Donald Muirhead was looking at Catriona with an expression that might have been loyalty or might have been calculation. It was hard to tell the difference with Donald.

Finlay's jaw had tightened. His hands had gone into his pockets, and his shoulders had come up, and Mara recognized the posture of a man who was about to do something he would regret. She waited. Finlay would speak or he would not. She had learned, over thirty years, that some silences were more honest than words, and this was one of them.

"The survivor clause is not a myth." Catriona's voice had not changed. It was still formal, still measured, still the voice of a woman who had been stating facts about the sea since before most of the people in this circle were born. "Ships that should have rescued them sink. Ropes that should have held break. The sea does not forget what it has marked."

"We don't know that," Finlay said. His voice was rough. "We don't know that any of this is real."

"We know that every reading in the Registry has come true."

"The Registry. The Registry. You want me to believe in a pile of paper in some office on the mainland."

"I want you to believe what your own island has taught you." Catriona turned to face him, and for a moment the mask of authority slipped and there was something older beneath it, something that had watched three sons drown in a winter storm and had decided, forty years ago, that it would never be helpless again. "The sea takes. The sea marks. And the community decides what to do with what the sea has given."

"What the sea has given." Finlay's laugh was short and bitter. "You want to call Eilidh a gift now?"

Eilidh flinched. Mara saw it—a small movement, the way her body tried to make itself smaller without actually moving. She had not known. That was clear now. Finlay had not told her what the reading said, had not explained why he had called her home, had not done any of the things that a father should do when his daughter was in danger. He had just asked her to come, and she had come, and now she was standing in a circle of stones listening to people talk about her like she was cargo on a tide.

"I have not called her anything," Catriona said. "I have stated what the reading means. The community must decide how to respond—which means the community must decide what to do with the knowledge it has been given."

"What you mean is you want to use her."

"I want to use the reading. The survivor is the community's concern."

"The survivor is my daughter."

"And therefore the community's. Every person on this island is the community's concern. That is what it means to live here."

The wind shifted. It came from the east now, carrying the smell of the harbor, and in that smell there was fish oil and diesel and the particular dampness of a village that had been drying nets in the same place for a hundred years. Mara breathed it in. She had grown up with that smell. She had left it behind when she moved into the keeper's cottage, and she had never quite gotten used to the salt-and-peat of the promontory, the absence of the harbor's particular混浊.

She stepped forward.

"The reading is not yours to decide."

Every face turned to her. Catriona's expression did not change, but something behind her eyes did—a flicker of calculation, of reassessment, of a woman who had expected resistance and was deciding how much it mattered.

"The reading arrived in my hand," Mara said. "The message was mine to receive. And the survivor is not yours to use."

"The community—"

"The community has no say in what the reading means." Mara's voice was flat, declarative, the voice she had used for twenty-seven years in the lighthouse tower, calling across the water to ships that could not hear her. "The reading names who it names. The survivor survives or does not. The community's decision changes nothing."

"Then what use is the community?" Catriona asked. "If the reading cannot be altered, what is there to decide?"

"The community decides what it owes the survivor. Not what it owes the reading. Not what it owes the sea. What it owes the person."

"You think the two can be separated?"

"I think they have to be."

The wind died. For a moment, the cairn was silent—not the silence of the absence of sound, but the silence of the presence of listening. The stones stood. The grass bent. The sky pressed down. And the people gathered around the oldest grave on the island waited for someone to say the thing that would break the silence or make it permanent.

Agnes spoke.

"The survivor clause has been tested." Her voice was quiet, but it carried. It always carried. The Daughters had a way of speaking that slid under the noise of argument and landed in the place where people actually listened. "In seventy-three, a reading named my brother. My parents tried to keep him on the island. They tried to stop him from going to sea. They tried everything they could think of, and he went anyway, and he came back in a box."

Moira MacDonald made a small sound. She remembered. Everyone old enough remembered.

"The reading was not changed," Agnes continued. "But the reading was not the point. The point was what we did with the knowing. My parents had a year. A year to say the things they should have said. A year to let him know he was loved. A year to be with him before the sea took him." She paused. "The reading came true. But the year was ours."

The silence returned. It was different now—less weight, more depth. Mara watched the faces around the cairn and saw something shift in them, a movement like the tide going out, a recession that would not last but that was happening anyway.

Catriona's expression had not changed, but her silence had. She was thinking. Mara could see it in the way her hands had come together in front of her, the way her weight had shifted slightly forward, the way she was looking at Mara as though she were seeing her for the first time.

"The community still must decide," Catriona said finally. "If not what to do with the reading, then what to do with the survivor."

"The community can decide what to offer," Mara said. "Not what to take."

"And if the survivor refuses?"

"Then the survivor refuses."

Eilidh spoke.

Everyone turned. She had not meant to speak—she could see it in the way her hand came up to her mouth, the way her body went rigid with the shock of having said something in a space where silence had been the rule. But she had spoken, and now she had to finish.

"I don't—" She stopped. Started again. "I don't know what any of this means. I don't know what a reading is supposed to do, or why it matters, or why I'm standing in a field being talked about like I'm already dead."

"Eilidh," Finlay said.

"No." She looked at her father, and for a moment the fear was gone and what was left was something harder, something that had been building for years and that had finally found an outlet. "No. You brought me here. You made me come. And you didn't tell me why."

"I was trying to protect you."

"Protect me from what? From knowing?" Her voice cracked. "From being here? From being part of this?" She gestured at the cairn, at the stones, at the faces turned toward her. "You wanted me to come home, and now I'm home, and you're still trying to keep me away from something. What is it? What are you so afraid I'll find out?"

Finlay did not answer.

Mara watched the two of them—father and daughter, standing three feet apart and farther apart than that, separated by years of unspoken things and a mother who had died without telling either of them what she knew. She thought about the harbor at low tide, the way the water pulled back and left the rocks exposed, bare and glistening and utterly changed from what they had been an hour before. That was what she was seeing now. The truth exposed. The things that lived underwater finally visible.

"The reading named you," Mara said. She did not move closer. She did not need to. "I know because I received it. The message said you would survive a wreck. It said you would be the only one left."

Eilidh's face went white.

"I didn't want to believe it," Mara continued. "I stood on the rocks this morning and watched the sea and tried to think of a way to unwrite what was written. There isn't one. The reading is true, and it will come true, and there is nothing any of us can do to stop it."

"Then why are we here?" Catriona asked. "If nothing can be changed—"

"Because the reading is not the whole story." Mara turned to face the cairn, the stones, the grass that had been growing since before the island had a name. "The sea takes. The sea marks. But the sea does not take everything. There is always something left. There is always what we do with the time we have."

She turned back to Eilidh.

"I don't know if you believe what I'm telling you. I don't know if you can. But the reading names you, and the community knows now, and what happens next is up to you."

Eilidh stared at her. Behind her, Finlay had gone very still. Around them, the Cairn families watched, and in their watching there was no judgment, no decision, only the weight of a knowledge that had been shared and could not be unshared.

"I don't know what to do," Eilidh said. Her voice was small.

"No one ever does," Mara said. "That's the truth of it. No one knows what to do. We just do something and hope the sea remembers we tried."

The wind picked up again. It came from the northwest now, cold and salt-heavy, carrying the smell of the open Atlantic and the first hint of the storm that was building beyond the horizon. The cloud had darkened to the color of iron, and the light at the edge of the cairn had gone from gray to something deeper, something that had no name except the name the island gave it: the light before.

The families began to disperse. They did not speak to each other, not at first. They walked back down the path in ones and twos, heads bent against the wind, each of them carrying the weight of what they had heard and what they had not heard and what they had chosen not to say. Dougie Camshield was the first to leave. Then Moira MacDonald, still speaking to the sea under her breath. Then Donald Muirhead, who paused at the edge of the ring and looked back at Catriona with an expression Mara could not read.

Agnes was last. She walked past Mara without stopping, but she touched her arm as she went—a brief pressure, an acknowledgment, a thank-you for the thing that had needed saying. Then she was gone, too, walking down the path with her hands folded and her face turned to the wind.

Catriona was the last to leave. She stood alone in the circle of stones, her black coat whipping around her, her white hair escaping from its pins. She looked at Mara for a long moment. There was no anger in her face. There was something worse: uncertainty. The realization that the thing she had built her authority on—the belief that the old ways could be controlled, managed, used—had been challenged in the place where that authority meant the most.

"The community will decide," she said finally. "That has not changed."

"The community will decide what it owes," Mara said. "That has always been what the cairn means."

Catriona did not answer. She turned and walked away, her footsteps soundless on the grass, her figure growing smaller against the darkening sky until it disappeared over the rise and was gone.

Mara stood alone at the cairn.

Finlay and Eilidh were still there. They had not moved. They stood three feet apart, father and daughter, and between them there was a distance that had nothing to do with space. The wind blew. The stones stood. The grass bent and rose and bent again.

"I should go," Eilidh said finally. Her voice was flat. Exhausted. The voice of someone who had learned something they did not want to know and who was still trying to find the words for it. "I should go back to Edinburgh."

"The ferry won't run," Finlay said. "Not in this weather."

"Then I'll wait."

"Eilidh."

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't tell me what to do. Don't tell me how to feel. You brought me here and you didn't tell me why, and now I know why, and I need—I need to not be here. I need to not be standing in a field being told I'm going to survive something."

"You're not going to survive." Finlay's voice broke. "The reading says you're the only one. That means everyone else—"

"I know what it means."

"Do you? Do you really?" He stepped toward her, and for the first time since Mara had known him, Finlay Gunn looked like a man who was afraid. Not afraid of the sea, not afraid of the reading, but afraid of the thing that was standing in front of him and refusing to be held. "Do you have any idea what it would do to me? To watch you go? To know I could have done something and didn't?"

"You can't do anything." Eilidh's voice was sharp. "That's the whole point, isn't it? The reading is true. The sea takes what it takes. Nothing changes." She laughed—a short, broken sound. "Nothing ever changes on this island."

"That's not true and you know it."

"Do I?" She turned to face him, and in her face Mara saw something she recognized—the look of a woman who had left and who had never stopped regretting the leaving, and who had never stopped knowing that the leaving was necessary. "I left because I couldn't stand it anymore. The way you looked at me. The way you held on. The way you tried to keep me safe from everything, and the whole time the sea was just waiting."

"I was trying to protect you."

"From what? From being here? From knowing?" Her voice cracked. "From being part of this?" She gestured at the cairn, at the stones, at the sky. "You wanted me to come home, and now I'm home, and you're still trying to keep me away from something. What is it, Dad? What are you so afraid I'll find out?"

Finlay did not answer.

Eilidh waited. The wind blew. The cloud pressed down. And then, slowly, her shoulders dropped, and the anger went out of her, and what was left was something worse than anger—it was exhaustion, the exhaustion of a woman who had spent her whole life running from something and who had finally stopped and turned around and realized that the thing she was running from had been running beside her the whole time.

"I don't know what to do," she said quietly.

Mara watched them. She thought about the lighthouse, the way it stood on the promontory and shone out over the water, year after year, light after light, calling ships home that would not always listen. She thought about the eleven faces she saw every night. She thought about the bottle in her pocket, still there, still heavy, still waiting.

"I don't know what to do either," Finlay said. "I never have. That's the truth of it. I've been pretending I knew what to do since your mother got sick, and I don't. I don't know anything except that I can't lose you too."

Eilidh closed her eyes.

"I'm not going anywhere," she said. "Not tonight. Not in this weather. I'll stay at the Inn. We'll talk tomorrow. We'll figure out—" She stopped. "I don't know what we'll figure out. But we'll figure out something."

She walked past her father. She walked past Mara. She walked down the path toward the village, her coat pulled tight, her head down, her footsteps soundless on the grass. She did not look back.

Finlay watched her go. When she was out of sight, he turned to Mara.

"Why did you tell her?"

"Because she needed to know."

"She needed to be protected."

"From what? From herself?" Mara shook her head. "The reading names her. The community knows. And now she knows, and what she does with that knowing is her choice. Not yours. Not Catriona's. Hers."

"The community will never accept that."

"The community doesn't have to accept anything. The community can only offer."

Finlay looked at her for a long moment. There was something in his face that Mara had not seen before—not anger, not fear, but something closer to recognition. The look of a man who had spent his whole life trying to control the things he could not control and who was only now realizing that the control had never been his to have.

"The harbor's going to be rough tomorrow," he said finally.

"It always is."

"I should check the moorings."

"You should."

He turned and walked away. His footsteps were heavy on the path, and the wind blew them away before they could settle, and then he was gone too, and Mara was alone at the cairn.

She stood there as the light faded. She stood there as the cloud thickened and the first drops of rain began to fall. She stood there as the stones grew dark and the grass disappeared into shadow and the wind turned cold and the sea, somewhere beyond the hills, began to rise.

The reading was true. The survivor was named. The community knew.

And now everything would change, or nothing would change, and no one—not Mara, not Catriona, not Finlay, not even Eilidh herself—would know which until the tide turned and the sea made its choice.

She reached into her pocket and touched the bottle. The glass was cold against her fingers. The message inside had already done its work. It had named who it named, and it had marked what it marked, and now it sat in her pocket like a stone, like a weight, like the tide waiting to come in.

She let it rest.

She turned and walked back toward the keeper's cottage, toward the sound of the sea, toward the light that still burned on the promontory despite everything, because that was what lights did—they burned, and they waited, and they hoped that someone out in the dark would see them and know they were not alone.

The rain came harder. The wind rose. The island sat in the gray water and waited for what would come next.

And somewhere in the harbor, Finlay Gunn walked the moorings in the rain, checking knots he had checked a thousand times before, pretending he could hold the world together if he just held on tight enough.
