# Chapter 4

The fire had gone to embers. Mara had not moved to feed it.

The bottle was on the table where she had left it. Green glass. The color of the sea in shallow water, the color of medicine once, before medicine came in plastic. She had not touched it since she had set it there. She did not need to. The message was inside her hand now, written in ink she had not chosen, in a voice that was not hers. She knew what it said. She would know until she died.

The cottage was dark except for the embers' glow. Through the walls she could hear the sea. It was always audible, through walls that had heard it for a hundred and thirty years. It did not whisper. It did not threaten. It simply continued, the way it always had, the way it always would, taking what it claimed and keeping what it kept.

Mara had been sitting in her chair for an hour. Maybe longer. The light outside the window had gone from gray to black, and she had not moved to light the lamp. She could see the shapes of things in the dark: the table, the bottle, the chair across from her where no one sat, the door to the sleeping loft above. She knew the room without looking.

She was not alone.

They were here. They were always here, in the dark, in the silence, in the space between waking and sleep where the rules were different and the dead could answer if you asked them the right questions. She had learned this slowly, over fifteen years, alone in the cottage with nothing but her memory and the sea's sound and the faces she could not forget.

Eleven faces.

She could not say their names. She had tried, once, in the first year, writing them down in the logbook she still kept by the fire, and she had gotten to the fourth name and stopped because the letters would not hold their shape and the sound of the name in her throat was the sound of water filling a mouth. After that she did not try again. The faces were enough. The faces came every night, and she learned their shapes the way she had learned the shapes of the rocks below the lighthouse—礁 by day, treacherous by night, marked in her mind by the things they had taken.

The young one with the beard. The older man who stood at the bow. The two who clung to each other until the end, until the sea decided they had held on long enough. She knew them. She had watched them go. She had lit the signal fires and shouted to the mainland and done everything the rules said to do, and the rescue boat had come too late, and they had gone into the water one by one, and she had counted them as they vanished, and the number she had counted was eleven, and no one on the mainland had counted at all.

That was the part that still woke her.

Not the drowning. She had seen drowning before, in twenty-seven years of keeping the light. She had seen the sea take boats and men and boys who thought they knew what the water could do. She had seen the harbor empty and fill again and empty again, the same faces replaced by younger faces and then by older ones, the island always losing what it could not replace. She knew drowning. She knew what it looked like and what it sounded like and how long it took, and she had made her peace with that knowledge the way keepers made their peace with all knowledge about the sea—with the understanding that peace was another word for exhaustion, and exhaustion was another word for giving up.

But the mainland had not counted.

The rescue boat had come too late, and the bodies had not come back, and the coast guard had filed a report that no one read, and the insurance adjusters had argued about liability, and somewhere in the capital a clerk had noted another wreck in the western waters, another eleven men lost, another tragedy for the files that no one opened. They had not come to the island. They had not asked what she had seen. They had sent condolences and moved on, and the eleven had become a number in a column, and the faces had faded from everything except her memory, where they stayed, where they would stay until she died.

Mara breathed in. The air smelled of smoke and brine and the rosemary she kept on the windowsill, for memory, the way her grandmother had kept it, the way her grandmother's grandmother had kept it, the way women on this island had kept it for generations, tending the herb that was also the word, the word that was also the wound.

She stood.

Her knees ached. Her hip had been wrong since the winter, a pain she had not shown to anyone, a pain that told her what she already knew: that her body was failing the way all bodies failed, slowly, then all at once, then slowly again. She was sixty-four. She had been the keeper for twenty-seven years and the ex-keeper for fifteen. She had climbed the tower four hundred and twelve times in her working life and she had not climbed it once since the company took over, and her legs knew the difference, and her hands knew the difference, and the part of her that still counted things knew the difference too.

She did not light the electric lamp. Instead she went to the shelf by the door where she kept the old oil lamp, the one from the keeper years, the brass one with the glass chimney that had lit her father's hands and her grandfather's hands and every hand that had wound the mechanism and checked the lens and kept the light burning through the storms that no one remembered except the people who had been in them. The lamp was there, dusty, waiting. She took it down. She found the matches in the drawer by the door, the same drawer where her father had kept matches, the same drawer where she had kept matches for thirty years, and she struck one, and the flame caught, and she touched it to the wick, and the light came on.

The cottage changed in the lamplight. The shadows pulled back. The shapes became specific: the table and the bottle, the chair and the door, the logbook on the shelf where she had tried to write the names and stopped. The fire's embers glowed but did not warm. The lamp was enough. The lamp was what she had used for thirty years, and it was what she used now, when the darkness came and the faces came and she needed to see something other than the shape of her own failure.

She sat back down in her chair. The lamp was on the table beside the bottle. In its light, the glass looked darker, the green deeper, the message inside more present. She did not open it. She knew what it said. The words were in her hand and in her head and in the part of her that counted things, and she could not unread them no matter how much she wished she could.

Instead she looked at the faces.

They came when she called them, which was always, which was the thing she did not tell anyone, the thing that would make them leave her alone if they knew, the thing that proved she was not the woman they thought she was—steady, silent, contained. She was not contained. She was full of them, the eleven, and she talked to them every night, and they answered in the only way the dead answered, which was by staying, which was by not leaving, which was by keeping her company in the dark the way the sea kept her company through the walls, the way the cold kept her company in the cottage she could not sell because no one wanted to buy a cottage on a rock in the North Atlantic with a woman who talked to dead men.

"I saw the message," she said.

The silence did not answer. It never did. But she felt them there, the way she felt the sea through the walls, the way she felt the weight of the lighthouse above her, sixty-two feet of stone that had stood when the keepers stood and would stand when the keepers were gone and the company sent its technicians and the tourists came to look at the view and the island forgot what the light had meant.

"I saw it and I knew."

The faces shifted in her mind. She had learned, over the years, not to try to move them or arrange them. They came as they came, and she let them come, and she spoke to them, and they listened the way the dead listened, which was completely, which was without judgment, which was the only kind of listening she trusted anymore.

"There was a woman. I know her. I knew her when she was a girl, when she came down to the harbor with her father and watched the boats come in. She's his daughter. Finlay's daughter."

The older man in the bow, the one with the gray at his temples. She had not known his name either. She had not asked. She had only watched.

"Eilidh. Her name is Eilidh. She left. She went to the mainland and she stayed there and she did not come back except for holidays and now she's in a message and the message says she's the only one who survives."

A flicker in the lamp. The flame steadied.

"I don't know what that means. I don't know if it means she'll live and everyone else will die or if it means she'll be the last one in the water and the sea will take her too and I'll have to watch it happen. I don't know. I've never known. The readings don't explain themselves."

The young one with the beard. He had been the first to go. She remembered that, the way she remembered all of it, the sequence of vanishing, the order in which the sea had claimed them. She had counted. That was what keepers did. They counted things.

"But I know I can't watch again."

The words came out harder than she had expected. There was something in them, something that had not been there before, a sound that was not her usual talking-to-ghosts voice but something older, something with edges.

"I can't. I won't."

She looked at the bottle. The green glass held the message, and the message held the name, and the name held the weight of everything she had spent fifteen years trying to set down. She had carried it. She had carried the eleven. She had carried the fire and the signal and the rescue boat that came too late and the faces that would not leave her and the names she could not say. She had carried it all, alone, in the cottage by the lighthouse, and she had not put it down, and she had not given it away, and she had not told anyone that the weight was breaking her.

Now there was a name. Now there was a woman she knew, a girl she had watched grow up, a person who would drown unless something changed, and she was the only one who knew.

"I can't watch," she said again. "I won't watch again."

The dead did not answer. The lamp burned. The fire's embers faded. Through the walls, the sea continued, patient, inevitable, the way it had always continued, taking what it claimed, keeping what it kept, waiting for the next storm, the next ship, the next set of faces that would come to her in the dark and ask her why she had not done more.

Mara had asked herself that question for fifteen years. She had no answer. She had only the weight and the silence and the faces that would not leave.

Now she had something else.

She had a name.

She stood again. She walked to the window. The glass was cold when she pressed her hand to it, and beyond it the dark was total, the kind of dark that existed only on islands far from cities, far from lights, far from anything that could drown out the stars or the sea or the sound the world made when no one was watching.

Eilidh Gunn was in Edinburgh. Mara had never been to Edinburgh. She had been to Glasgow once, for a keeper's conference, and she had not liked it—the way the buildings closed in the sky, the way the streets smelled of exhaust and people who did not know the sea. Edinburgh was different, she had been told. Edinburgh was old and stone and history, and Eilidh was somewhere in it, in a flat, in a life, in a future that the message had already seen.

The message said she was the only survivor.

Mara did not know what that meant. She did not know if it meant the woman would live or if it meant the woman would be the last one in the water when the others were gone, alone in the dark, waiting for a rescue that would not come. She did not know if the reading could be changed or if the sea could be fought or if there was anything in the old rituals that Agnes knew that could turn the tide.

She knew only that she would not watch.

The lighthouse stood on its promontory, visible from the cottage window, sixty-two feet of stone above the waterline. The light was burning—the company kept it burning, automated, efficient, indifferent to the keeper who had climbed it every night for twenty-seven years. Mara looked at it and felt nothing. That was another thing she had learned: how to look at the things she had loved and feel nothing, how to hollow herself out so the loss could not find a place to land.

But the faces found her. The faces always found her.

Eleven men. A storm. A signal fire lit too late, or perhaps not too late, perhaps exactly on time, perhaps the rescue boat would have been too late no matter what she did. She had told herself that for years. She had told herself that the fire was not her fault, that she had done everything right, that the system had failed and the system was not her, and she had almost believed it, almost talked herself into the kind of peace that was actually exhaustion, that was actually giving up.

But the faces would not let her believe it.

And now there was a new face. A woman's face. A girl she had known.

Eilidh in Edinburgh. Eilidh in the message. Eilidh in the water, unless.

Mara turned from the window. The lamp burned. The bottle waited. The dead were silent.

She went to the table and sat down and looked at the bottle and the lamp and the space where the fire had been, and she thought about what she would do, and she knew she could not do it alone, and she knew she would have to tell someone, and she knew that telling meant believing, and believing meant acting, and acting meant—

She did not finish the thought.

She did not need to.

The sea was there. The faces were there. The name was there.

And she was here, in the cottage, in the lamplight, in the dark that would last until morning, and she would not watch again. She had said it, and she meant it, and she did not know if she had the power to keep it, but she would try, and trying was more than she had done in fifteen years, and trying was more than the eleven had gotten, and trying was what she owed them and what she owed herself and what she owed the girl whose name was in the bottle whose name was in her hand whose name was—

"Eilidh," she said.

The word was quiet. It was not a prayer. It was not a promise. It was a name, spoken aloud for the first time since the message had written itself in her hand, and it was the beginning of something, though she did not know what, and it was the end of something, though she did not know that either.

She sat in her chair.

She waited for the night to end.


In the morning she would go to Finlay. She would tell him what she knew. She would watch his face change and his hands shake and his voice go hard with the kind of denial that was really fear, and she would let him deny it, because denial was what people did when the world showed them something they could not accept, and he would accept it eventually, or he would not, and she would act anyway.

But that was morning. That was tomorrow. That was the thing she would do when the dark was done and the faces had faded and the lamp had burned out and she could stand and walk and speak without feeling like the words were coming from somewhere else, somewhere the reading had made, somewhere that was not her but was in her now, written into her the way the message was written into the glass.

For now, she sat.

The fire was cold. The cottage was quiet. The lamp burned.

And the sea went on doing what the sea always did, which was wait, which was take, which was keep what it claimed, and Mara went on doing what she had always done, which was watch, and this time—this time—she would not only watch.

She would act.

She would fail or she would not fail, and she did not know which it would be, but she would try, and trying was the thing, and trying was the only thing that mattered, and the night was long and the faces were there and the bottle was on the table and the lamp was burning and somewhere on the mainland a woman named Eilidh was waking in a flat in a city in a life in a future that the message had already seen.

Mara let the night hold her.

She did not sleep.

She waited.
