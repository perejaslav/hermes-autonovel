# Chapter 1

The fire had gone out again.

Mara pressed her thumb to the ash, felt the cold beneath, and reached for the peat basket without opening her eyes. Her hands knew the motion. They had done this thirty years of mornings and they would do it thirty more, or until her hands gave out, or until the ash stayed warm on its own. She knelt by the hearth and worked the kindling to life, breathing smoke into embers that remembered heat even when they had forgotten light.

The cottage smelled the way it always smelled: salt and old oil and the rosemary she kept on the windowsill for no reason she could name. Her grandmother had kept rosemary. Her grandmother's grandmother, probably. The herb had no practical use here—nothing that needed preserving, no cooking that justified the window space—but Mara replanted it every spring and watched it die every autumn and started again the following year. Someone had to.

She dressed in the dark. Wool beneath, oiled canvas over that, heavy boots that had known the stones of this island longer than some of the people on it. The clothes of her working years. She had other clothes now—soft things from charity shops, a cardigan her neighbour had knitted—but she did not reach for them. The wool and canvas were what she wore when she meant to be useful. When she did not mean to be useful, she stayed in bed. There was not much middle ground anymore.

Outside, the sky was the grey that preceded dawn by an hour or by five minutes, depending on what the clouds were doing. Mara did not look at the sky. She looked at the path.

The granite had been cut for cart wheels a century ago, back when carts still ran to the harbor, back when the harbor had things worth carting. Now the stones were worn smooth by feet, by salt, by the slow grinding of seasons that did not ask permission to pass. She walked the path the way she had walked it since she was eleven years old, following her father's count of boats, and she counted them now without thinking, the way she breathed.

Three visible in the harbor. Muirhead's boat at the far buoy—working, since Muirhead did not believe in sitting idle. The Cassidys' boat next to it—working, barely, the engine noise wrong at the top end. And Angus Mackinnon's boat closest to the shore, tied with temporary knots because the real knots needed parts they were still waiting for from the mainland. Two boats waiting for parts. She had noted this yesterday. She noted it again now, the way she noted everything twice, the way the island had trained her to look twice at the harbor because the harbor did not always look the same back.

The mist came off the water in long tongues, and the rocks below the path were dark shapes that her eyes kept turning into figures and her mind kept correcting. A head. A shoulder. The curve of something that might have been a body. She had stopped seeing the faces of the eleven in ordinary light years ago. In mist, in low light, in the grey hour before dawn, they still came. She had learned to wait them out. They always left. They always came back later, but they always left.

She counted boats. She waited out the faces. She walked.

The harbor was quiet at this hour. No one worked the morning shift anymore—the young ones who would have worked it had gone to the mainland or gone to the better jobs or gone to wherever young ones went when an island stopped being enough. The Inn would not open for hours. The cooperative would not open for hours. The whole of Auld Bay existed in the pause between yesterday's exhaustion and today's start, and Mara moved through it like a ghost who did not know she was haunting.

She stopped at the cliff edge and looked down.

The tide was coming in. She knew this without checking the tables, without doing the calculations she had once done every four hours for the shipping notices. She knew it in her bones, the way she knew which rocks were safe and which would take her boot and which would take her leg if she was foolish enough to trust them. The tide coming in. Twenty minutes, maybe less, and the shelf below the high-water mark would be covered, and whatever was caught there would be the sea's until the next low.

Green glass. A flash of it in the grey light, caught between two rocks that were normally underwater now but weren't, not yet, not for another twenty minutes. The kind of bottle that held medicine once, before medicine came in plastic and the glass bottles went to the sea or to the shore or to wherever glass went when people stopped wanting it.

Mara looked at the green flash. She looked at the waterline creeping up the rocks. She did the math she had stopped doing for official purposes but still did for herself: twenty minutes, maybe less, and then the bottle would be underwater, and then it would be the sea's, and then one day it would wash up somewhere else or sink or keep being moved by currents she could not predict.

She had stopped making predictions years ago. She had stopped making calculations about what the sea would do and what it would take and what it would give back and when. She had watched eleven men drown in weather she had predicted, in seas she had read correctly, in conditions she had reported accurately and no one had listened and the rescue boat came too late and she had learned nothing from this except that knowing did not save anyone.

She climbed down the path to the rocks.

The water was November-cold. She knew this before she stepped in, and she stepped in anyway, because the bottle was there and the tide was coming and some things had to be done whether they mattered or not. The cold climbed her boots, her legs, the wool that soaked through to skin. She kept walking. The rocks were slick and her boots held and she moved through water that reached her thighs before she reached the bottle.

It was heavier than it looked. Green glass, dark with seaweed at the base, corked with something that had swollen in the salt. She pulled and the cork came and she did not look inside. She put the cork back and held the bottle against her chest and turned and walked back through the water and climbed the rocks and did not stop until she was on the path again, above the tide line, holding a bottle that felt like it meant something even though bottles never meant anything and the sea was just the sea and prophecy was just what desperate people called coincidence.

The cottage was cold again by the time she reached it. She fed the fire with her hands still wet, watched the flames take the wet out of the peat, and sat in her chair with the bottle in her lap and did not open it.

She knew what it was. Everyone on Torran knew what a bottle in the rocks meant, especially a bottle corked and heavy, especially a bottle that felt like it had weight behind the glass. She had seen three in her time at the light. Two had been drift bottles from ships at sea, messages to wives or to the registry or to no one in particular. One had been a reading. She had watched it float past the tower in a storm, caught in white water, and she had not been able to reach it and had told herself that was the sea's decision and not hers.

She had told herself many things in fifteen years. She had told herself that solitude was protection. That silence was peace. That watching from a distance was the same as not watching at all. She had told herself these things and some of them had been true and some of them had been the kind of lies that felt true until the moment they stopped, and she had sat in this chair and looked at the bottle and she did not open it because opening it would make it real and real things had weight and she had carried enough weight.

The fire crackled. The rosemary on the windowsill moved in a draft she could not feel. Outside, she knew, the tide would cover the rocks where she had found the bottle, and in a day or a week or a month the sea would give back something else, or take something else, or do what the sea always did which was neither good nor evil but was simply what happened when salt and stone and weather met on a coast that had been meeting them for longer than anyone could remember.

She uncorked the bottle.

The paper inside was rolled tight, the kind of roll that came from years of compression, the kind that meant the bottle had been at sea for a long time before it found the rocks. She unrolled it carefully. The paper was thick, expensive, the kind that did not come from any supplier she knew. It lay flat under her hands and she held it to the light from the fire and she looked at it and it was blank.

She had expected blank. She had not expected to want it to stay blank.

Mara set the paper on the table beside her and reached for the pen she kept there—an old thing, the kind she had used for the shipping notices, for the weather logs, for the careful records she had kept for twenty-seven years at the tower. She did not know why she reached for it. She did not know why she uncapped it and touched the nib to the paper.

Her hand began to write.

The words were not hers. She knew this immediately, the way she knew the tide from the sound of it, the way she knew storm from the smell of the air. Her hand moved and the words appeared and they were not in her handwriting and they were not in any handwriting she recognized but they were being written in her hand, by her fingers, and she could not stop.

*Eilidh Gunn. The only one who survives.*

Her hand wrote the name and then it stopped and she was sitting in her chair with a pen in her hand and a message on paper and a name she knew, a name she had known since the girl was born, since she watched the girl grow and leave and come back for holidays she did not want and she set down the pen and she folded the paper and she did not read it again because she had read it once and once was enough, was more than enough, was the kind of enough that broke something in a person that they did not know was breakable until it broke.

Finlay's daughter. Eilidh. Thirty-two years old, living in Edinburgh, working in something bureaucratic, coming home two or three times a year with expressions that said she would rather be anywhere else. Mara knew her the way she knew all of them—the way she knew everyone's children, everyone's boats, everyone's weather. She was the keeper. Keeping was what she did. She had kept the light and she had kept the records and she had kept the count of boats and she had kept the names of the eleven and she had kept them all in the place in her memory where she could not lose them but could not forget them either, and now she had another name, and she did not know what to do with it.

The fire was burning low. She did not add more peat. She sat in her chair with the folded paper on the table beside her and the bottle in her lap and she listened to the sea and she did not speak, because there was no one to speak to and because she had started talking to them—the eleven, the faces, the ones who came to her in the grey light—and she did not want to start talking now, not yet, not until she knew what she was going to do.

The message had named someone. The message was always right. She knew this the way she knew the tide: not because she believed it but because she had seen it, and seeing was its own kind of knowing, and knowing was its own kind of weight, and she was already carrying so much.

Eilidh Gunn. The only one who survives.

Mara folded the paper again and put it in the bottle and corked the bottle and set it on the table where she would see it tomorrow and the day after and the day after that. She did not throw it into the sea. She did not bury it in the garden. She left it where it was, where it would wait for her, where she would have to decide what to do with it and when and whether she would tell anyone or keep it to herself the way she had kept everything else—the faces, the names, the weight that never got lighter no matter how long she carried it.

Outside, the tide was coming in. The mist was lifting. The harbor would wake soon, and Finlay would open the Inn, and the boats would go out or stay in, and the island would do what islands did, which was continue, which was hold on, which was wait for the sea to decide what it would take next.

Mara watched the fire die. She did not add more peat. She sat in her chair and she held her hands in her lap and she looked at the bottle on the table and she thought about Eilidh Gunn in Edinburgh, in a flat, in a life, in a future that the message had already seen, and she wondered if the girl knew. She wondered if anyone knew. She wondered if knowing changed anything, or if it just made the waiting worse.

The fire went out. The cottage went dark. The sea went on doing what the sea always did.

And Mara sat in the dark with a name she could not unread, and she did not move, and she did not speak, and she waited for morning.
