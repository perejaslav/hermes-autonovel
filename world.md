Inside: a message in a hand she almost recognizes. The words promise a shipwreck, name the only survivor, and leave her with a choice that will cost her everything she spent thirty years learning to forget.\n\n### World Differentiator\nThis is not a world where prophecy is a system. It is a world where prophecy is *treated* as a system—catalogued by the learned, traded by the desperate, and mostly ignored by everyone else until it comes true. The magic here is not in the message's power but in its *weight*: once read, it cannot be unread. The survivor named in the message is already a ghost. Mara knows them. She will have to decide whether to let the sea take what the sea has claimed, or to fight for someone the sea has already marked.\n\n### Theme Space\n- Fate and intervention: what we owe people we've failed\n- The loneliness of witnessing: keeper of the light who cannot shine it\n- Memory as burden: thirty years of watching ships and counting the ones that didn't make it home\n- The difference between knowing and believing\n\n---\n\n## Magic and Speculative System\n\n### Nature of Prophecy (The Reading)\nProphecy on Torran operates on a principle of *overheard truth*. Messages from the sea are not sent by any intelligence—they are transcribed by whoever finds them, written in the hand of whoever holds the bottle, in a voice that is not their own. Scholars call this phenomenon the *Coasting*, after the way light grazes the horizon before sunrise. It cannot be summoned, stolen, or bought. It comes when it comes. Scholars in the capital maintain the **Registry of Tidal Readings**, cross-referencing predictions across centuries, but their data is incomplete by design: most people who receive a reading destroy the message rather than file it.\n\n### Mechanics\n- **Trigger unknown**: No one knows what causes a message to appear. Correlations exist—high storms, specific tidal patterns, the anniversary of mass wrecks—but they are imperfect. A bottle may wash up on any shore at any time.\n- **Voice transfer**: The message is always written in the hand of the finder, but the words arrive from outside. If the finder cannot write, the message does not form. If they write in multiple scripts, the message appears in whatever language their body defaults to under stress.\n- **Prophecy scope**: Readings tend toward maritime disaster—storms, wreck, drowning. Occasionally they touch on who survives, what they carry, how many bodies the sea keeps. Rarely: the name of a survivor who should not have survived.\n- **The survivor clause**: When a reading names a specific person as sole survivor, that person is marked. Ships that should have rescued them sink. Ropes that should have held break. The sea does not forget what it has claimed.\n\n### Limits and Costs\n- **Cannot be intercepted**: Messages pass through all barriers. Bottles cannot be sealed, delayed, or redirected. Once the message has formed, only destruction of the glass stops it—but by then, the finder already knows.\n- **Single reading per finder**: A person who writes a reading cannot receive another. Their hand belongs to the sea already. This is why most finders destroy the bottle immediately—their first reading is also their last.\n- **No false readings**: Every reading in the Registry has come true. This is considered a settled fact.\n\n### Failure Cases\n- **Refusal to deliver**: If the named survivor is told and does not believe, the prophecy still holds. The survivor may survive the wreck—then die in the village they returned to, or walk into the sea three months later for reasons no one can explain.\n- **Intervention by force**: Attempting to remove the named survivor from the island before the wreck does not cancel the reading. It relocates it. The ship still sinks. The survivor still arrives, somehow, drawn back by a current that has no chart.\n- **The second bottle**: Very rarely, a second bottle arrives after the first. This has happened four times in recorded history. The second reading always corrects the first. No one knows what this means or how to survive being the correction rather than the original.\n\n### Social Consequences\n- **Registry clerks**: They track readings, maintain archives, and are generally disliked. A clerk's visit means someone in your community received a message. The questions that follow are not pleasant.\n- **\"Knowing\" as status**: People who receive readings and survive the aftermath often gain a strange authority in island communities. They have been marked by something larger than the sea. Some islanders treat this as wisdom; others treat it as contagion.\n- **Legal status**: The Islands Provisional Government has no law regarding prophecy, but insurance adjusters after major wrecks have been known to argue that a reading's existence constitutes foreknowledge and thus contributory negligence. This argument has never held, but it has been tried.\n\n---\n\n## Geography and Social World\n\n### Torran Island\nA granite outcrop roughly four miles long, two miles wide, off the northwest coast of Scotland. Population: 347 at last census (down from 512 in 1971). The island tapers to a hook of land in the east where the lighthouse stands on a promontory called the Keeper's Thumb. The harbor village of Aul迆 lies in a natural cove to the west. The interior is moorland, cut by three streams that run brackish in late summer.\n\n### The Lighthouse (Coulter's Light)\nBuilt 1887, automated in 1974, decommissioned from active service in 2001. Mara Callaghan was the last keeper, withdrawn when the service ended. The light still functions—a private company maintains it for cargo navigation. Mara lives in the keeper's cottage sixty yards below. The lighthouse tower is off-limits except to company technicians.\n\n### Economy\n- **Fishing**: Herring and crab, mostly. Aul迆's fleet is six boats, three of which are working. The other three are waiting for parts.\n- **Wool and tweed**: A cooperative in the village produces rough-spun goods sold through a single Edinburgh shop. Not profitable, but traditional. It keeps the women occupied.\n- **Ecological tourism**: Three families take in guests during summer. Muir is the word used in brochures. \"Rugged.\" \"Unspoiled.\" \"For those who want to get away from it all.\" The tourists want to get away from everything, including each other, and the island is mostly happy to oblige.\n- **The ferry contract**: Torran receives one supply boat per week from the mainland. If the ferry doesn't come, the island lives on canned goods for a month.\n\n### Class Pressure\n- **The Muirheads**: The Muirhead family owns the cooperative, the guesthouse, and two of the three working boats. Old Gaelic money that predates the croft acts. They have opinions about the lighthouse cottage—it's on Muirhead land, technically, though the deed predates any Muirhead.\n- **Incomers vs. Born**: \"Born\" islanders (anyone whose family has been on Torran longer than living memory) form a loose hierarchy. Mara is born, but her father was a signalman from the mainland who married local. She exists in the hierarchy at its margins—respected for her work, but not quite within it.\n- **Weekenders**: A London couple bought the old post office and restored it as a holiday home. They arrive twice a year, spend money, ask questions. The island tolerates them with the same patience it extends to seabirds: noise, but not malice.\n\n### Law and Governance\nThe Islands Provisional Government (IPG) governs from a mainland town. Torran sends one elected councillor who serves on rotation. Law is mostly inherited Scots common law with local adaptations: no licensed premises past nine (the ferry leaves at nine-fifteen, and the IPG sees no reason to encourage connections with the mainland that might encourage emigration). Firearms are technically legal but functionally useless—nothing to hunt, and the sea doesn't care if you're armed.\n\n### Factions\n- **The Ferryman's League**: Informal group of eleven families who have relatives on the mainland. They advocate for more ferry runs and better weather monitoring. They meet in the back room of the Aul迆 Inn when there's something to discuss. The Inn's owner, **Finlay**, is their unofficial chair.\n- **The Preservation Society**: Muirhead-adjacent. They want to restore the lighthouse as a heritage site. Mara has declined their invitations to speak. Her silence on this subject is noted.\n- **The Daughters of the Deep**: A women's prayer group that meets in Aul迆's vestry on Sundays. Not officially religious—they describe themselves as \"keepers of traditional observances.\" They maintain the old maritime rituals: the blessing of new boats, the naming of wrecks, the burial of things the sea gives back. They have opinions about the Reading. They always have opinions about the Reading.\n- **The Cairn**: Local name for the three oldest families whose ancestors built the original Torran settlement. They don't organize. They don't need to. They have the land, the boats, and the oldest stories. When they speak, others listen.\n\n### History\n**1847**: First permanent settlement. Fisher families from the Outer Hebrides, fleeing clearances they did not name as such. They named the island Torran after a word they brought with them—*torran*, meaning \"place where the tide turns.\"\n\n**1887**: Coulter's Light built. Named for the engineer who designed it. His daughter, a woman named Ellen, drowned in the cove that now bears her name. The coincidence of names is considered significant by those who consider such things.\n\n**1916**: A German U-boat sank a British freighter two miles off Torran. The island rescued eleven survivors. Mara's grandmother was seven years old. She remembered, and she told Mara, and Mara has never forgotten.\n\n**1974**: Automation of Coulter's Light. The Muirheads petitioned against it. The petition failed. Three families left that year.\n\n**1998**: The last major wreck in local waters. A cargo ship lost in a storm. The bodies never came back. Mara remembers every face. This is the weight she carries.\n\n---\n\n## Locations\n\n### The Keeper's Cottage\nMara's home. The key is with the maintenance company in Inverness.\n\n**Sensory signature**: Cold stone and mechanical oil. The sound of wind in the lamp housing, which rises to a kind of singing in storms. The silence that isn't silence but is the absence of the sounds the sea makes.\n\n**Plot use**: Where the Cairn family holds its informal tribunals. She has spent thirty years watching the sea take things. She does not want to watch again. But the message names someone, and that someone will drown unless someone does something, and Mara is the only one who knows.\n\n**The wound**: In 1998, a freighter sank in a storm two miles off Torran. Mara saw it happen from the tower. She lit the signal fires. She did everything right. The rescue boat from the mainland arrived too late. Eleven men died. Mara has their faces. She has never been able to learn their names without seeing the way the sea wore them away.\n\n**Competence**: High (she knows the sea, the weather, the patterns of the coast). **Proactivity**: Low (she has chosen inaction for fifteen years). **Likability**: Moderate (she is not warm, but she is honest, and the island respects this).\n\n### The Named Survivor (To Be Determined)\nThe reading names someone Mara knows. Finlay's daughter offers intergenerational grief. The Muirhead boy offers tragedy inherited from stubbornness. The weekender's child offers innocence under threat. Each produces a different story.\n\n2. **What happened between Mara and the survivor?** They know each other. How? What passed between them? A debt? A wound? A love? The answer changes whether Mara's intervention is obligation or sacrifice.\n\n3. **Does Mara believe the Reading?** This is not the same as knowing it's true. Does she believe it in her bones, or does she treat it as an data point to be managed? Her belief will determine whether she acts from conviction or calculation.\n\n4. **What does the survivor want?** If told, will the survivor believe? Will they fight? Will they run? Will they refuse—and if they refuse, what is Mara's obligation then?\n\n### Plot\n5. **Does the wreck happen on-screen?** The novel can be about prevention or aftermath. Prevention raises the question of what prevents a prophecy. Aftermath raises the question of what survives survival. Both are viable.\n\n6. **Is there a second bottle?** The failure cases allow for a correction—someone, somewhere, receives another message that changes the terms. This creates a ticking clock and a question of authority. Who receives the correction? Why? What does it mean for the first reading's survivor if the second reading says something different?\n\n7. **What does intervention cost?** Prophecy in this world resists force. If Mara tries to save the survivor, what breaks? Not a ship—but a relationship, a possibility, a version of the future she cannot see yet.\n\n### World\n8. **How many Readings have happened on Torran?** The novel needs at least one historical example—a past Reading that the community remembers—to ground Mara's knowledge and establish what \"intervention\" has looked like before.\n\n9. **What are the old maritime rituals?** Agnes's knowledge is mentioned but not defined. The author must decide what rituals exist, what they require, and what happens when they're performed correctly or incorrectly.\n\n10. **What is the sea's relationship to the Reading?** The sea is the medium, not the source—but it has preferences. It takes more than it gives. It keeps what it claims. The author must decide whether the sea has intention or merely momentum.\n\n---\n\n## Voice Identity\n\n### Tone\nCold and salt-heavy, like air off the North Atlantic in November. The prose does not comfort. It observes. There is weight in the sentences—a sense that every word has been tested against the sea and found strong enough to last. Mythic undertones, not through vocabulary but through inevitability: the story knows how it ends and the characters do not, and that gap produces the tension.\n\n### Sentence Rhythm\nShort. Declarative. The sentences do not perform their own importance. Then, without warning, a long one that bends under the weight of what it has to carry—not baroque, but cumulative. A list of names that goes on too long because the names are real. A description of the harbor that takes three sentences because the harbor is the only fixed thing in a life that has too few of them.\n\n### Vocabulary Register\nAnglo-Saxon blunt. Short words. \"Sea\" instead of \"ocean.\" \"Shore\" instead of \"coast.\" \"Dead\" instead of \"gone.\" The register draws from maritime Scots—words like *birling* (work song), *dreich* (long, dreary, wet), *tidal* (not metaphorical). No Latinate ornament. The language earns its keep.\n\n### POV and Tense\nThird-person limited, past tense, close enough to Mara that the reader sees through her eyes without being permitted inside her thoughts. Her thoughts are shown through action: what she does, what she cannot do, what she reaches for and puts down. Omniscient intrusions are absent. The world is shown, not analyzed.\n\n### Dialogue Conventions\nMinimal tagging. \"Said\" only when confusion would result. No adverbs. No speech verbs. Characters speak in the rhythm of people who are used to being heard—or who have given up on it. Subtext is carried in what is not said. Mara does not tell Finlay the full truth of what she knows. He knows she's not telling. The tension lives in the silence.\n\n### Exemplar Passages\n\n> The lighthouse stood where it had always stood, sixty-two feet of stone above the waterline, and Mara had climbed it every night for twenty-seven years.
