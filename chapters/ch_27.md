# Chapter 27: The Light

The fire had burned to embers when Mara woke. She lay in the narrow bed and listened to the sea against the rocks below, the same rhythm it had kept for a hundred years, and she thought: the girl is gone.

She had known this before she opened her eyes. The knowing lived in her chest like a stone, cold and heavy and certain. Eilidh had gone south on the morning ferry, watching the island shrink behind her, and Mara had watched from the shore until the ferry was only a speck and then nothing. Then she had come back to the cottage and made tea and sat by the fire and done nothing, for hours, until her body decided it was done.

Now the morning had come.

Gray light through the window. The smell of ash.

Mara pushed back the blanket and put her feet on the cold floor. The stones were familiar beneath her soles, worn smooth by years of her pacing, her standing, her waiting. She did not think about what she would do today. She knew.

She dressed without hurrying. Wool sweater, heavy trousers, the boots she wore to the shore. Through the window she could see the harbor—four boats, one of them new, a hull bright with fresh paint that someone had paid for. She counted them automatically. The counting was a habit, a bone-deep thing she could not stop, any more than she could stop breathing.

Finlay was at the Inn, probably. Eilidh had not gone to him before she left. Mara had watched from a distance, seen the girl walk past the Inn with her bag on her shoulder, seen Finlay standing in the doorway with his hands at his sides and nothing in his face at all. He had not called out. She had not looked back.

That was between them. It was not Mara's to hold.

She put on her coat and stepped outside.

The wind was off the north, carrying the smell of open water. Clouds hung low over the island, the color of wet slate, and the grass bent under the gusts. Below the cottage, the rocks were dark with spray. The lighthouse stood against the gray sky, sixty-two feet of stone, and at its top the light turned slow and white, marking the water for ships that were not there.

Dr. Macallister's van was parked at the base of the path.

Mara stopped. She had not expected this. The maintenance company sent someone quarterly—inspections, oil changes, the mechanical business of keeping a light that no longer needed a keeper. She had seen the van before, watched it arrive and leave, watched other men climb the tower while she stayed below.

She had not thought to ask when the next inspection would be. She had not thought about the tower at all, not directly, not as something that might be reachable.

But here was the van, and here was the morning, and the wind was pushing her toward the path.

She walked.

Dr. Macallister was a man of perhaps fifty, solid in the way of people who spend their lives driving and walking and climbing. He had a clipboard in his hand and a set of keys on a chain at his belt, and when he saw Mara approaching he straightened up from where he had been examining the base of the tower.

"Mrs. Callaghan."

"Doctor Macallister."

"Quarterly check." He gestured vaguely at the tower. "Company wants the mechanism documented. You know how it is."

"I know."

She stood beside him and looked up. The door was at the base, iron painted gray, and above it the stone rose in a column that seemed to narrow toward the sky. The light housing was visible at the top, glass and metal, and inside it the mechanism turned.

"I could watch you work," Mara said. "If that would be all right."

Macallister looked at her. "The company's policy is that the tower's off-limits to non-personnel. Insurance thing."

"I understand."

"Can't let anyone up while the light's running. Spinning mechanism, hot housing, the insurance boys get nervous."

"I understand."

He studied her for a moment. She did not know what he saw. She was sixty-four years old and wore the island like a second skin, and her face was the kind of face that had stopped asking for things a long time ago.

"Wouldn't be much to see anyway," he said. "Modern housing, original mechanism below it. The company's been trying to get funding to restore the whole thing, put in a proper visitor center. That Muirhead woman's been pushing for it."

"I know."

He turned the keys over in his hand. "The inspection won't take long. An hour, maybe. I could give you a look at the mechanism room, at least. That's historical, the company's happy to show that off. Just not the lamp room while the light's running."

Mara felt something loosen in her chest. "That would be enough. Thank you."

"Can't say I understand why you'd want to, but it's your island."

"It's not my island."

"No. No, I suppose it isn't."

He unlocked the door and held it open. The darkness inside was familiar, the smell of old stone and machine oil, and Mara stepped through.

The stairs spiraled up in the old way, iron treads worn smooth by generations of keepers. Macallister climbed first, his flashlight cutting a pale wedge through the dimness, and Mara followed. Her legs remembered this. Her hands remembered the rail. The rhythm of climbing—step, step, step, hand to rail, foot to tread, repeat—was buried in her body like a code, and her body obeyed without consulting her mind.

She had climbed these stairs every night for twenty-seven years. She had climbed them in the dark, in the storm, in the cold that seeped through the stone and settled in her bones. She had climbed them with her lamp trimmed and her log written and her eyes on the water below, watching for shapes that should not be there.

Now she climbed them in the daylight, following a man she did not know, and the only thing she watched for was the next step.

The mechanism room was a gallery of brass and iron, gears and weights, a clockwork system designed before anyone living was born. Macallister walked among it with his clipboard, checking gauges, recording numbers, murmuring to himself about pressure readings and oil levels. Mara stood in the doorway and let it wash over her.

This was where the light lived. Not in the glass housing at the top, but here, in the works that turned it. The weights that dropped, the gears that meshed, the slow release of energy that kept the lamp revolving at the speed the shipping lanes required. She had learned this system with her hands, had polished the brass until it shone, had felt the vibration of the mechanism through her palms when something needed adjusting.

Macallister pointed out features as he worked. The original clock mechanism, dated 1887. The weight-driven governor that controlled rotation speed. The lamp housing, replaced in 1998, that held the modern bulb in a frame designed for something older.

"The system's a mess," Macallister said, without apparent concern. "Frankenstein thing, old and new bolted together. But it works. Keeps the light turning. Keeps the ships from hitting the rocks."

"Has it worked?"

"So far."

Mara said nothing.

When Macallister finished his inspection, he stood by the stairwell and gestured upward. "I can take you to the lamp room access, if you want. You can't go in while it's running, but the view through the door's something."

"Yes," Mara said. "I'd like that."

She climbed the last flight of stairs, past the door to the keeper's quarters that had been removed decades ago, past the narrow window that showed the harbor in miniature far below, up to the top where the mechanism met the housing and the light turned.

The lamp room door was a circle of glass set in iron. Macallister opened it a crack. The light blazed through.

"You can look," he said. "But don't touch the mechanism. She'll take your fingers off if you're not careful."

Mara looked.

The light was a wheel, turning slow, and in the wheel the flame burned constant and white. It was not the flame she had known—the old flame had been oil, a fire that required tending—but it turned the same way, marked the water the same way, called to the ships the same way. The mechanism hummed with a sound she remembered, a sound that had been the soundtrack of her nights for twenty-seven years.

Beyond the light, the world spread out.

She could see the harbor—Aultree's single street, the Inn, the boats tied to their buoys, Finlay's black coat by the water. She could see the moorland rolling toward the east, the streams catching the gray light, the cairn a dark smudge on the horizon. She could see the water, gray and restless, and beyond it, when the clouds thinned, the mainland's coast.

She could see all the places she had watched for thirty years.

"Remarkable view," Macallister said from behind her. "I can see why you'd want to see it."

"Yes," Mara said. She was not listening to him. She was listening to the hum of the mechanism, the turning of the light, the sound she had carried in her bones for so long she did not know if she remembered it or if it was simply part of her.

She stood in the doorway and watched the light turn, and she thought: this is where I stood.

She had stood here in storms that shook the tower. She had stood here in calm nights when the water was flat and the stars came down to meet her. She had stood here in the dark before dawn, watching the horizon for the first hint of light, and she had stood here in the hours after, when the light was only itself, turning and turning and turning.

And she had stood here in 1998.

The night the freighters came. The night the storm put its hands on the water and shook it until the ships broke. She had lit the signal fires. She had turned the light. She had watched from this window and seen the shapes appear out of the dark and then disappear, one after another, like things the sea was taking back.

She had not been able to save them. The rescue boat from the mainland had been too late, and the eleven had gone into the water, and she had watched.

That was the weight she carried. That was the thing that lived in her chest like a stone.

She had never learned their names. She had seen their faces—the way the sea wore them away—and she had not learned their names because learning their names would have made them real, would have made them people she could have saved if she had known something different, done something different, been something different.

She had not learned their names, and so they had become hers to carry. Eleven faces without names. Eleven lives that had ended while she watched. Eleven debts she did not know how to pay.

The light turned.

Mara watched it and felt the weight in her chest, and she thought: I am done carrying this alone.

She did not speak aloud at first. She spoke inside her head, in the silence that had been her companion for fifteen years.

*Donald MacRae.*

The name came from somewhere she did not know. Perhaps she had heard it. Perhaps she had read it. Perhaps the sea had whispered it to her in the night, over and over, until it lodged in the place where she kept the things she could not release.

*Donald MacRae. Robert Murray. Thomas Black. James MacLeod. John Cameron. Duncan MacInnes. Andrew Reid. Kenneth Shaw. Malcolm MacLeod. William Fraser. Charles MacDonald.*

Eleven names.

Eleven men.

Eleven lives that had ended in the water while she watched from above.

She spoke them aloud. The sound of her own voice surprised her—it was rough, unused to this work, and the names came out cracked and strange. But she said them. One after another. Donald MacRae. Robert Murray. Thomas Black. James MacLeod. John Cameron. Duncan MacInnes. Andrew Reid. Kenneth Shaw. Malcolm MacLeod. William Fraser. Charles MacDonald.

The light turned. The mechanism hummed. The sea was below, gray and endless, and it did not care.

But she said the names.

She said them, and she felt the weight shift. It did not leave. It would never leave. But it moved from the center of her chest to somewhere she could carry it differently—not as a wound that bled but as a scar that ached when the weather changed.

She was not done with them. She would never be done with them.

But she could let them go.

The light turned. The mechanism hummed. Mara stood at the top of the tower she had climbed for thirty years, and she watched the harbor where the boats sat patient in the water, and she watched the shore where the waves broke against the rocks, and she watched the mainland's coast on the horizon where the land rose out of the sea like a promise that things could be different.

She watched, and she let the names go.

Donald. Robert. Thomas. James. John. Duncan. Andrew. Kenneth. Malcolm. William. Charles.

They were not hers to carry. They had never been hers to carry. She had taken them because she could not stop watching, could not stop witnessing, could not stop being the one who saw what happened and could not stop it. But the watching was not the same as the guilt. The seeing was not the same as the failing.

She had done everything right. The signal fires. The light. The watch. She had done everything right, and the rescue boat had been too late, and the eleven had gone into the water, and there was nothing she could have done.

She had not believed this before. She had carried the guilt because she did not know what else to do with the grief. But now, standing in the tower, she let herself believe it: there was nothing she could have done.

The guilt could go.

She did not have to carry it anymore.

Mara stood in the doorway of the lamp room and breathed. The air was cold on her face, carrying the salt and the wet and the endless motion of the sea. Below her, the harbor waited. The boats counted themselves. The Inn stood where it had always stood. Finlay was there, probably, thinking about his daughter, thinking about the island, thinking about all the things he could not control.

She had told him the truth. She had given him the warning. She had climbed the tower and said the names and done what she came to do.

What happened next was not hers to decide.

The light turned. The mechanism hummed. The world was gray and endless and full of water, and somewhere far to the south a bus was carrying a woman toward a city and a flat and a life that was waiting to be lived.

Mara watched the horizon. The mainland's coast was visible, a dark line against the gray, and beyond it the world went on the way the world went on—inconvenient, ordinary, full of people who did not know what the sea held and did not want to know.

She would go back to the cottage. She would light the fire. She would count the boats in the morning and the boats in the evening and the boats whenever she happened to look toward the harbor, because that was what she did, because that was who she was, because the counting was not a prison but a prayer.

She would live.

The light turned.

Mara Callaghan stood in the tower and watched the water, and she did not look away.
