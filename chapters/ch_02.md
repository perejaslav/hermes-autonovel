# Chapter 2

Morning came the way it always came on Torran—grey before anything else, a lightening of the dark that did not yet deserve to be called dawn. Mara had not slept. She had not moved from the chair. The fire had died sometime in the night and she had not added more peat and now the cottage smelled of cold ash and the bottle on the table smelled of salt and the space between those two smells was where she lived.

She looked at the bottle. It looked back.

The glass was dark green, old medicine glass, the kind they used to make before plastic came and solved everything and ruined it at the same time. The cork was still in it. She had not pulled the cork. She had sat here in the dark and held the bottle in her hands and looked at the cork and not pulled it, and the not-pulling had gone on for hours, through the dark and into the grey, and now the grey was brightening and still she had not pulled it.

Because once she pulled it, she would know.

And once she knew, she would be the one who knew, and the one who knew was always the one who had to decide, and the deciding was the thing she had spent fifteen years learning to avoid.

But the bottle was on the table. And she was in the chair. And the grey light was coming in through the window, and the window looked out over the sea, and the sea did not care what she decided, the sea would go on doing what the sea did, and the bottle would wait, and she would wait, and waiting was also a choice, and she had made that choice already, she had been making it for fifteen years, and it had not made anything lighter, it had only made her older and more alone and more certain that the faces she carried were the only company she would ever keep.

She reached for the bottle.

Her hands were stiff. She had been holding them in her lap and now they did not want to close around the glass but she made them close anyway, because she was Mara Callaghan and she had lit the light for twenty-seven years and she had watched ships come in and ships go out and she had never once turned away from what she saw, even when the seeing cost her everything, and if she could not look at a bottle she found on her own shore then she did not deserve to call herself a keeper of anything.

She pulled the cork.

The sound was soft, a small exhalation, the glass releasing what it had held. She tipped the bottle and a rolled piece of paper slid out, onto her palm, and it was dry, which surprised her—she had expected it to be wet, expected the message to have blurred, but the paper was dry and tight and she held it and felt the weight of it, which was almost nothing, which was everything.

She unrolled it.

White paper. Blank. Smooth where it had been rolled, textured where it had been pressed against the glass. She held it up to the grey light coming through the window and looked at it and saw nothing. She looked at the other side and saw nothing. She held it against the table and looked again and still there was nothing, no words, no marks, no message, just paper, just the fact of paper, just the thing itself with nothing written on it.

She had heard of this. She had heard that the messages came slowly, that they needed the right light or the right moment or the right hand, and she thought: I need a pen. The thought came from somewhere outside herself, from the place where the message would come from, and she recognized it because she had thought the same thing for twenty-seven years, every morning, logging the weather, recording the wind, noting the hours of sun and the hours of rain, and her hand knew how to hold a pen the way her eyes knew how to read the sky. It was the old habit. The habit that had kept her alive when the light was all she had and the dark was everything she feared.

She set the paper on the table. She opened the drawer beside the fireplace—the drawer where she kept the pencils, the stubby yellow things she used for marking the tide charts, the same pencils she had used in the tower when the tower was hers—and she took one out and held it and felt the wood and the graphite and the weight of it, which was almost nothing.

She sat back down.

She put the paper in front of her.

She put the pencil in her hand.

And she thought: I will just write the date. Just to see. Just to—

Her hand moved.

Not the way hands move when you decide to move them. Not the small negotiation between intention and muscle that sends a pencil across paper. This was different. This was her hand opening and the pencil settling into her fingers and her fingers closing around it and then her hand going to the paper and touching down and beginning to write, and she was not writing, she was watching herself write, she was the one who watched, she was the one who stood outside her body and saw her hand make the first mark and then the second and then the words began to appear and they were her handwriting, they were the slant and weight of her own script, and the words came from somewhere else and arrived in her hand and she could not stop them, she could not take the pencil away, she could only sit and watch and feel the nausea rising in her like water filling a boat that should not take water.

*The fourteenth of November. A storm from the northwest, building through the night. The Lysistrata will founder two miles off Torran. The hull will crack on the shelf at Aul迆 Point. Eleven will go down. One will come up.*

One will come up.

Her hand kept moving. She had not given it permission. She had not asked for more. But the pencil did not stop and the words kept arriving and now they slowed and she felt the pressure building in her wrist, felt the resistance, felt the thing inside her hand pushing against her own desire to be done, to stop, to put down the pencil and walk away and pretend this had not happened, and the pencil pushed back and the words kept coming.

*Eilidh Gunn. The only one who survives.*

Eilidh Gunn.

Her hand stopped. The pencil fell from her fingers and rolled across the table and fell off the edge and landed on the floor and she did not pick it up. She looked at the paper. The words were there. They were her words. They were not her words. They were the message and the message was written and she had written it and she would never write another one, she knew this the way she knew the tide, not because anyone had told her but because the knowledge was in her body now, in her hand, in the place where the message had moved through her like a current moves through water, taking what it needs and leaving the shape behind.

Her hand was cramped. She flexed her fingers and felt the ache in her knuckles and knew that she would feel it tomorrow and the day after and the day after that, the way she felt the faces, the way she carried everything the sea gave her and could not put down.

She looked at the paper.

The words were still there. She had not hoped they would disappear. She had not believed they would. But she looked anyway, the way you look at a wound to see if it has stopped bleeding, and it had not stopped, it would never stop, and she read the message again and again and each time the words were the same and the name was the same and Eilidh Gunn sat on the page like a stone in her chest.

She did not know the name. Not at first. It was just a name, just two words, and she had not lived long enough to have met every person on Torran or every person who had ever come to Torran or every person who had ever been mentioned in a message, and the messages were rare and most people never found one and most people who did never told anyone, and she had not expected to know the name, had not expected it to mean anything to her, had expected it to be like all the other names she had seen in messages she had not found—words on paper that belonged to someone else, ghosts of people she would never meet, and she thought she could carry that, she thought she could hold the knowledge that somewhere in the world a ship would sink and a woman would survive and it would not touch her, it would not be her business, it would be the sea's business and the sea did not need her help and did not want her interference and she had learned, she had learned, she had spent fifteen years learning that the best thing she could do for anyone was to stay out of the way and let the world happen without her and—

Eilidh Gunn.

She knew the name.

Not well. Not intimately. But she knew it the way you know a face you see on the ferry once a month, the way you know a name that belongs to someone who lives on the edge of your attention, who exists in the space between stranger and neighbor, who is there when you look and gone when you don't and whom you could pass on the street without greeting and it would not be strange because you do not know them well enough to greet and you know them well enough to recognize and that space, that between-space, is where Eilidh Gunn lived in Mara's mind.

Finlay's daughter.

The thought arrived and with it came the picture: a woman in her thirties, dark hair pulled back, standing at the rail of the ferry with the wind taking the scarf from her neck, and she looked at Torran as it came into view and she did not smile, she never smiled, she looked at the island the way you look at a bill you have been putting off, and Finlay was on the dock and he was waving and his face was open in a way that Mara had seen only on that dock, only in that moment, only when his daughter was coming home and he could not hide how much he wanted her to stay.

Eilidh Gunn. The only one who survives.

Mara sat in her chair and looked at the paper and felt the weight of the name settle into her, felt the knowledge become part of her the way the faces were part of her, and she thought: she is in Edinburgh. She lives in Edinburgh. She works in a records office or something like that, something with files and dates and the careful filing of other people's lives into alphabetical order, and she comes home for holidays and she stands on the ferry and looks at the island and does not smile and Finlay waves and she gets off and they go to the Inn and they eat dinner and she talks about her work and Finlay talks about the boats and neither of them talks about the thing that lives between them, the thing that has lived there since her mother died, and the holidays end and she gets back on the ferry and Finlay waves and she goes back to Edinburgh and does not look at the island as it shrinks behind her.

Eilidh Gunn. The only one who survives.

The message said the fourteenth of November. Three weeks away. Twenty-one days. Three weeks of knowing and not knowing and watching the weather and waiting for a storm that would come from the northwest and build through the night and crack the hull of a ship called the Lysistrata on the shelf at Aul迆 Point, and eleven people would go into the water and they would not come up, and one person would come up, and that person would be Eilidh Gunn, and the sea would let her go.

The sea would let her go. That was what the message said. That was what the survivor clause meant. The sea claimed who it claimed but sometimes it let one go, sometimes it wrote a name and that name was the name of the one who would survive, and Mara had heard of this, had heard of the readings that named survivors, had heard that the readings were always right and that the survivors were marked and that the marking was not a gift but a debt, that the sea did not give freely, that what it let go it would come back for, that the survivor clause meant the survivor belonged to the sea now in a way they had not belonged before, and she did not know what that meant, she did not know what the sea would want from Eilidh Gunn, she only knew that in three weeks a ship would sink and a woman would live and that woman was Finlay's daughter and Finlay did not know, Finlay was opening the Inn right now or counting the boats or standing at the window watching the grey morning come in from the west, and he did not know that his daughter was marked, that the sea had written her name in a bottle and sent it to Mara's shore, that Mara was the one who had to tell him or not tell him, had to decide, had to choose, and the choice was the thing she had spent fifteen years learning to avoid and the knowing was the thing she could not avoid now that it had arrived.

She folded the paper. She put it back in the bottle. She corked the bottle. She set it on the table where she would see it tomorrow and the day after and the day after that.

She did not throw it into the sea.

She did not bury it in the garden.

She left it where it was, where it would wait for her, where she would have to decide what to do with it and when and whether she would tell anyone or keep it to herself the way she had kept everything else—the faces, the names, the weight that never got lighter no matter how long she carried it.

Outside, the tide was coming in. The mist was lifting. The harbor would wake soon, and Finlay would open the Inn, and the boats would go out or stay in, and the island would do what islands did, which was continue, which was hold on, which was wait for the sea to decide what it would take next.

Mara watched the fire die. She did not add more peat. She sat in her chair and she held her hands in her lap and she looked at the bottle on the table and she thought about Eilidh Gunn in Edinburgh, in a flat, in a life, in a future that the message had already seen, and she wondered if the girl knew. She wondered if anyone knew. She wondered if knowing changed anything, or if it just made the waiting worse.

The fire went out. The cottage went dark. The sea went on doing what the sea always did.

And Mara sat in the dark with a name she could not unread, and she did not move, and she did not speak, and she waited for morning.
