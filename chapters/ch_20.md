# Chapter Twenty

The Inn was quiet.

Not the comfortable kind of quiet—the kind that comes after the last drinker has gone home and the fire has burned down to embers and the only sound is the sea against the harbor wall. This was the other kind. The kind that happens when two people sit across from each other and do not know what to say, and the not-saying fills the room like water filling a hull.

Finlay had lit the lamps an hour ago. Two of them, on the bar, because he could not stand the dark. He had straightened the glasses behind the counter even though no one had touched them. He had wiped the same spot on the bar twice. His hands needed something to do. His hands had always needed something to do.

Eilidh sat at the table by the window.

She had not taken off her coat. She sat with her hands in her lap, fingers interlaced, and she looked at the harbor through the salt-streaked glass as though she were waiting for something to arrive. The boats rocked at their moorings. The sky was the color of wet slate.

"You talked to her," Finlay said.

"Yes."

"And?"

"She told me what the message said." Eilidh did not turn from the window. "She told me what it means."

Finlay set down the cloth he had been folding. He had been folding it for ten minutes. He put it on the bar and pressed his hands flat against the wood and breathed in through his nose and out through his mouth and tried to remember what it felt like to say things without calculating their effect.

"I'm going to tell you something," he said. "And you're going to listen."

"I'm listening."

"Three weeks ago. A bottle came up on the rocks below the lighthouse. Mara found it. She opened it and the message appeared in her hand and it said—" He stopped. His throat had closed around the words. He had said them before, to Mara, to himself, in the dark when he could not sleep. He had practiced. He had practiced so he would not break. "It said there would be a wreck. A bad one. And it said there would be one survivor. It named that survivor."

Eilidh turned now. Her face was pale in the lamplight, and her eyes were the same color as her mother's eyes had been, and Finlay felt something crack open in his chest that he thought had calcified years ago.

"Who?" she said.

"You."

The word sat between them like a stone dropped in water.

"You're joking," Eilidh said.

"I am not."

"Dad. You can't actually believe—"

"I don't know what I believe." He was shaking. He could feel it in his hands, in the muscles of his forearms, in the jaw he was clenching to keep his face still. "I don't know if I believe in prophecy or readings or messages in bottles. I don't know if any of it means what the island thinks it means. But I know what happened to the last ship Mara watched go down. I know she lit the signal fires and did everything right and eleven men still drowned. And I know that when a reading names a survivor, the sea doesn't let go. I've seen the records. I've read what the Registry says."

"The Registry." Eilidh laughed, but there was no humor in it. "The Registry of Tidal Readings. The one no one takes seriously except when they do and then they pretend they don't."

"You came home."

"I came home because you called."

"I called because of the reading."

"I know." She looked down at her hands. "I know why you called. I knew before I got here."

Finlay waited.

"I've been dreaming about the harbor," she said. "For weeks. Boats coming in that shouldn't float. Water that looks black even in daylight. I thought it was—just stress. Work. The way things pile up when you don't deal with them." She closed her eyes. "I've been dreaming about boats sinking and I didn't want to tell you because I knew what you'd do with it."

"What did I do?"

"You called me and told me a bottle told you I was going to drown."

"That's not—"

"Is it wrong?"

He had no answer. She was right. He had taken the dreams—the dreams she had not told him about, the dreams she had kept hidden the way she kept everything hidden—and he had found a reason to make them matter. He had found a prophecy. He had found a way to make her come home.

"I'm not staying because of the reading," Eilidh said.

"Then why?"

She did not answer. She was still looking at her hands, and Finlay saw, with a clarity that felt like a blade, how thin her fingers had become. How sharp the knuckles. When had she gotten so thin? When had he stopped noticing?

"Your mother," he said.

Eilidh looked up.

"She was sick for a year." The words came out rough, scraped from somewhere he had bricked over years ago. "Before she went to the mainland for tests. Before they told us what it was. She was sick for a year and I didn't see it. I was here, running the Inn, counting boats, worrying about ferry schedules, and she was—" He stopped. His hands were shaking again. He pressed them harder against the bar. "She told me, at the end. She said she'd known for months. She said she didn't want to worry us. She said she'd been waiting for it to pass."

Eilidh's face had gone still.

"She didn't tell me," Finlay said. "She didn't tell you. We could have done something. We could have had more time. But she kept it quiet and I kept not seeing and you—" He faltered. He could feel the shape of it now, the thing he had been circling for years. "You knew too. Didn't you."

The silence stretched.

"Yes," Eilidh said.

"She told you."

"She didn't have to." Eilidh's voice was barely above a whisper. "I saw her. The way she got tired. The way she went to bed earlier and earlier. The pills she thought she was hiding. I knew. I knew and I didn't say anything because I thought—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "I thought if I didn't say it out loud, it wouldn't be real."

Finlay sat down. He had not meant to sit—he had meant to stand there and say what he had to say and stay controlled, but his legs had gone and there was nowhere to fall except into the chair across from his daughter. He sat and he looked at her and he felt the years of distance between them like a physical weight, like the stones stacked in the harbor wall, like the water pressure at a hundred feet.

"We watched each other drown," he said.

The words were out before he knew he was going to say them. They came from somewhere below language, from the place where the things you cannot say live until you cannot hold them anymore. He heard them and they were true and he wished he could take them back and he knew he could not.

Eilidh did not speak.

She reached across the table. Her hand came to rest on his, and her fingers were cold, and he could feel the bones of her hand through her skin, and he had not touched her like this since she was a child, since before her mother got sick, since before he learned that love was just another word for helplessness.

"I knew she was dying," Eilidh said. "And I didn't tell you. And I've been carrying that for six years. And I thought—" Her voice broke. She did not try to fix it. "I thought if I stayed away, if I kept enough distance, maybe you'd blame me less. Maybe I could make it mean something. But you never blamed me. You just kept waiting for me to come back. And I didn't know how to come back without admitting I'd done something wrong."

"You didn't do anything wrong."

"Neither did you."

The words hung between them. Finlay felt his chest ache, felt the pressure of everything he had not said building against his ribs. He had been so careful. He had been so controlled. He had spent six years making sure he never said too much, never asked too much, never let her see how much he needed her to come home.

"I called you because of the reading," he said. "But that's not the only reason."

"I know."

"I needed you to know what it said. Not because I thought you'd believe it, but because—" He stopped. Tried again. "Because if something happens, and I didn't tell you, I couldn't live with that. I couldn't add that to the list."

Eilidh's hand was still on his. He had forgotten, for a moment, how long it had been since anyone had touched him like this. The women who came into the Inn sometimes smiled at him, and he smiled back, and neither of them meant anything by it. He had become a man who smiled without meaning anything, and he had thought that was enough.

"I'm not staying because of the reading," Eilidh said again. She said it differently this time. Softer. More certain. "I'm staying because I need to."

"Need to."

"Yes."

The harbor light swept past the window. The beam moved slowly, the way it had moved for over a century, illuminating the water and the boats and the dark shape of the headland where the lighthouse stood. Finlay watched the light move and felt, for the first time in years, that he was not alone.

"I don't know how to help you," he said. "The reading says you're the survivor. It says the sea will claim you if the wreck happens. Mara said—Agnes said—the only way to change it is to change the terms. Pay a different price. I don't know what that means. I don't know how to do it."

"Neither do I."

"But you're staying anyway."

"I'm staying anyway."

Finlay looked at his daughter. She looked back at him. Her eyes were wet, but she was not crying—not quite—and he recognized the effort it took to hold that boundary, to not let go in front of someone who had never known how to catch you.

"The storm's coming," she said.

He had not noticed until she said it, but the windows were rattling. The sound came from the east, from the open water beyond the headland, and Finlay could feel the change in the air pressure, the way the room seemed to contract around them.

"How bad?"

"Bad." She tilted her head. Listening. "Maybe the worst in years."

The fire had burned down to embers. Finlay stood and crossed to the hearth and added a log, and the flames caught and flared and threw shadows across the walls of the room where his wife used to read by the fire, where his daughter used to fall asleep on the couch with a book open on her chest, where he had spent fifteen years pretending that keeping the Inn running was the same as keeping his family together.

"We should check the boats," he said.

"They're moored. They'll hold."

"Someone should watch the harbor."

"Dad." Eilidh stood. She was still wearing her coat. She moved to the window and looked out at the water and the darkening sky and the lighthouse beam sweeping back and forth like a warning. "There's nothing to do. Not tonight. The reading doesn't come true until the wreck. And the wreck won't happen until the storm brings the wrong ship too close to shore."

"And then?"

"Then we see what we're made of."

Finlay stood beside her. The harbor was choppy now, the waves higher than they should be for this time of evening. The boats tugged at their moorings. The gulls were gone—nothing flew above the water.

"I should have told you about your mother sooner," he said.

"Yes."

"Years sooner."

"Yes."

"I was afraid."

"I know."

"I thought if I said it out loud, it would break something. Between us. Between you and the island. I thought silence could hold it."

Eilidh turned to him. In the firelight, she looked younger, or maybe she looked exactly like she was, a woman who had carried too much for too long and had finally found somewhere to set it down.

"You can't hold anything with silence," she said. "I've been trying for six years. It just gets heavier."

Outside, the first drops of rain struck the window. Then more, harder, driven by wind that came all the way from the Atlantic. The harbor light swept past again, and in its glow, Finlay could see the whitecaps forming on the water, the swells building in the open channel.

The sea was turning black.

---

They did not speak of the reading again. They talked instead about nothing—the ferry schedule, the state of the Inn's roof, whether the Muirheads were still pushing for the heritage site designation. Small things. Safe things. The conversation of two people who had run out of big things to say and were not yet ready to admit that there was nothing left but silence.

Eilidh went to bed early. She took the room she had slept in as a child, the one that still had her old things in it—books, photographs, a scarf her mother had knitted. She did not tell Finlay she was going. She simply stood, stretched, said "I'm tired," and climbed the stairs without waiting for a response.

Finlay stayed by the fire.

He watched the flames and listened to the rain and tried not to think about ships in distress, about signal fires lit too late, about the faces of men who drowned while he stood on solid ground and did nothing wrong.

He heard the door open behind him.

Agnes stood in the doorway. She was wearing her old oilskin, the one she had worn for forty years of ferry runs and blessing ceremonies and trips to Ellen's Cove. Her face was grave.

"The Daughters felt it," she said. "The current shifted an hour ago. Something is moving."

"In the channel?"

"Past the channel. Coming in from the west."

Finlay stood. "A ship?"

"Can't say. But the feeling is strong. The old ones felt it before the readings came. They'd know something was wrong by the way the water tasted."

"And now?"

"Now we wait." Agnes stepped inside and closed the door. "The ritual requires presence. Attention. You must mean it."

"Mara told me."

"She told you what she knows." Agnes moved to the fire and held her hands to the heat. She was smaller than Finlay remembered, or he had grown larger in his own perception of himself. "But knowing isn't the same as doing. The readings don't care what you think. They care what you're willing to pay."

"What's the price?"

"For a reading this strong? I don't know. Maybe nothing. Maybe everything." She looked at him, and her eyes were the eyes of a woman who had buried a brother and never spoken of it and learned, through decades of silence, that some prices were paid in ways no one could see. "Eilidh's here."

"Yes."

"She's going to try to run."

Finlay said nothing.

"The reading says she's the survivor. That means the sea has marked her. If she tries to leave before the wreck, the wreck will follow her. The ferry won't run in this weather—she'll have to wait—but the current will try to pull her in. It's already trying."

"What do I do?"

"You let her choose." Agnes turned back to the fire. "That's all any of us can do. We let them choose, and we hope they're strong enough to choose right."

The fire crackled. The rain hammered the windows.

Above them, on the headland, the lighthouse beam swept across the cove, past the harbor, past the boats that were already straining at their moorings. Somewhere in the dark water, the tide turned.

The storm was here.
