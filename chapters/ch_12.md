# Chapter 12: The Return

The ferry cut through water the color of slate. Eilidh Gunn stood at the stern, gripping the rail with both hands, and told herself she wasn't coming. The words moved through her like a current—not persuasive, not a lie, just the shape of what she wanted to be true. She wasn't coming. She was on the boat. These two facts had no relationship to each other.

Her father had called three days ago. Not about the ferry schedule, not about the Inn, not about anything she could file under the usual. He'd said her name and then stopped. In thirty-two years, Angus Finlay Gunn had never stopped mid-sentence. She'd heard him argue with suppliers for fifteen minutes about the correct shade of green for a door. He'd stopped for her twice: once when her mother died, and once now.

The boat heaved. She held the rail tighter.

---

The island appeared out of the grey like something remembered. Coulter's Light caught the low sun first—a white flash on the headland that meant home and not-home, both at once. Eilidh watched it grow larger and felt something move in her chest that she refused to name. The island was smaller than she remembered. The moors ran down to the water in shades of brown and green, and the harbor she had left behind looked like a wound in the landscape, scabbed over with boats.

It was larger than she wanted.

That was the real problem with coming back. The island didn't shrink to fit her life in Edinburgh. It waited, unchanging, and when she returned it reminded her of every version of herself she had tried to leave behind. The girl who ran along the breakwater. The daughter who sat at her father's bar and pretended not to listen to the fishermen talk about the weather. The woman who put her mother in the ground and could not stop seeing the disease that had hidden in plain sight for a year.

She had been all of those people. None of them had stayed.

The ferry began its turn, swinging wide to align with the moorings. She scanned the dock without meaning to. Looking for someone. She didn't know who.

Finlay.

He stood at the end of the quay, apart from the small crowd that gathered when the ferry came in. He was not waving. He was not smiling. He was watching the water where her face would appear when she reached the rail, and the expression he wore was not the one she had come to expect. She knew his tired patience, his practiced nonchalance, his way of making every homecoming feel like a transaction being completed. This was none of those. This was the face he had made at the hospital when they told him her mother was gone. The face he had not made at the funeral, where he had been solid and steady and not enough.

Fear.

She descended the ferry's ladder and her feet hit the dock and her father did not move toward her. He waited. She walked across the wet planks and the gap between them shrank until it was only breath, and when she stopped in front of him he said nothing.

"Dad."

"You came."

"You asked."

He looked at her—really looked, the way he used to when she was small and he was checking for injury after a fall. Searching for something. She didn't know what he found.

"You shouldn't have come," he said.

"You called."

"I know what I said."

"Then tell me what you mean."

The harbor master's empty office sat behind them. Beyond it, the Inn's windows caught the light. Somewhere in that building, in a room with a view of the water, her father had made a phone call he couldn't take back. He had spoken her name and stopped, and she had gotten on a boat.

"There was a message," he said. "A bottle. Found at the lighthouse."

Eilidh waited. She knew about the readings. Every child on Torran knew—you grew up hearing about them, about the bottles that came in with the tide, about the words that couldn't be unread. You heard the stories and you filed them with the other island things: the ghost light in Ellen's Cove, the way the sea sang before a storm, the names of the eleven who went down in the wreck of ninety-eight. Things that were true in the way the island was true—not rational, not provable, but impossible to argue with because the island itself would not allow the argument.

"A message came," her father said. "To Mara Callaghan."

"I know who Mara is."

"It's about you."

The words arrived and she understood them and she did not believe them. Not yet. Belief was a thing you earned, and all she had was a dock and a grey sky and her father's face.

"I'm here," she said. "I'm here because you asked. I'm not here because I believe."

Finlay flinched. Not much. She wouldn't have seen it if she hadn't been watching for it. But the flinch was there, and it told her something about what he had expected from her, and it told her that whatever he had expected, this was not it.

"You should believe," he said. "If anyone on this island knows when to believe—"

"Then what? Then I'm saved? Then the message does something?"

"The message names you. The message says you're the only one who survives."

"Then I'll survive. That's what it says, isn't it? The only one who survives. That's not a warning. That's a promise."

Her father's face changed. The fear did not leave, but something else came with it—frustration, maybe, or grief. The expression of a man who had tried to say something important and been answered with a child's logic.

"A ship is going to sink," he said. "The reading says. A ship is going to go down, and you're the only one who makes it back to shore."

"And the others?"

He didn't answer. He didn't need to.

"How long?"

"I don't know. Mara didn't—"

"Mara knows. She found the bottle. She read the message."

"She won't talk about it. She gave me the name and she hasn't said another word. I've been trying to get her to explain, but she—" He stopped. Swallowed something that might have been anger. "She says the reading is what it is. She says there's nothing to discuss."

Eilidh looked past him. The Inn's windows glowed amber in the late light. The cooperative's door was closed. A gull screamed overhead, then another. Somewhere in the village, a dog was barking—the same three-note sequence it always barked, the one she'd heard every morning of her childhood and heard again now, emerging from memory like something surfacing.

She had been dreaming about the harbor. She hadn't told him that. She had been dreaming about boats coming in that shouldn't float, about water rising, about voices she couldn't answer. Three nights in a row. She had been about to call him when he called her.

"I'm here now," she said. "What do you want me to do?"

"Stay. Just—stay. Don't go near the water. Don't take any boats out. Just three days, until the reading—"

"Passes?"

"It's not like that. The readings don't expire. But whatever is going to happen, it's coming soon. Mara says she can feel it. She says the water knows."

Eilidh laughed. The sound came out wrong—too sharp, too loud for the quiet dock. Her father looked away.

"I know how it sounds," he said.

"Do you? Do you know how it sounds when you tell your daughter that the sea knows her name, that she's been marked, that she can't leave because something is coming that she can't escape?" She heard her own voice rising and did not try to stop it. "I grew up here, Dad. I know what the readings are. I know what they mean. And I'm telling you—I don't believe it."

"You came anyway."

"Yes."

"Why?"

She didn't answer. She couldn't. There was no answer that would satisfy him, no reason she could give that would make sense of the three nights of dreams, the phone call with its stopped sentence, the ferry cutting through grey water while she told herself she wasn't coming. She had come because she had to. She didn't know why. She did not examine this too closely.

---

The Inn was warm. The fire in the main room had been lit, and the smell of peat and something savory drifted from the kitchen. Eilidh stood inside the door and let the heat settle into her bones. The crossing had been rough. She was colder than she had realized.

Her father had gone to arrange her room. "The one in the back," he'd said. "It's quieter." He had not looked at her when he said it. He had not looked at her since she'd told him she didn't believe.

She would have walked past the window without stopping. She would have gone upstairs and closed the door and told herself that this was just a visit, just her father being dramatic, just the island doing what the island did—finding omens in the tide, reasons in the drift of gulls. She would have done all of that if she hadn't happened to look.

Mara Callaghan sat at a table by the window. Not at the Inn's counter, not in a chair—those would have been the places for someone who belonged there. She was in the corner, in the chair that faced the door, in the seat that had been left empty. She was watching.

Eilidh stopped. The woman at the window stopped too—not moving, not looking away, just stopping. Waiting. The way a lighthouse keeper waits. The way someone waits when they know that whatever is coming will come, and the only question is whether they can stand to watch it happen.

Mara Callaghan was old. Sixty-four, Eilidh knew, though she had never paid much attention to the number. Her hair was grey and pulled back and her face was the color of weathered stone, and she wore the kind of clothes that people wore when they had stopped caring about being seen. But her eyes were not the eyes of someone who had stopped caring about anything. Her eyes were the eyes of someone who had seen too much and could not unsee it, who had read a message in a bottle and could not unread it, who knew something about Eilidh Gunn that Eilidh did not yet know about herself.

Eilidh should have looked away. She should have walked up the stairs and shut the door and waited for her father to explain, or not explain, or do whatever it was he did when the world stopped making sense. She should have done any of those things.

Instead, she walked to the window.

Mara did not move. She sat with her hands folded on the table and her back straight and her gaze steady, and she watched Eilidh approach the way she might have watched a storm approach—knowing it was coming, knowing it would break, not knowing what it would leave behind.

"I saw you from the dock," Eilidh said.

"I know."

"How long have you been waiting?"

Mara considered the question. Her eyes moved over Eilidh's face, taking in something—her expression, her stance, the way her hands had curled into fists at her sides. Measuring. Not measuring against anything Eilidh could name.

"Long enough," Mara said.

"What's in the message? My father says it names me, but he doesn't know what it says. He doesn't know what it means."

"It means what it means."

"That's what he said. That you're not talking."

Mara's mouth twitched. Not a smile. Something more complicated—recognition, maybe, of her own words reflected back. She leaned back in her chair, and the movement was slow, deliberate, the movement of someone whose body had earned the right to take its time.

"The message says the sea is coming to take you," she said. "Not today. Not tomorrow. But soon. The ship that was meant to carry you is going to sink, and you are going to be the only one who survives. That is what the reading says. That is what I wrote, in my hand, with words that were not mine."

Eilidh heard her own breath. She heard the fire crackle behind her. She heard the door open as someone came in from the street, and she did not turn to look.

"And you believe that."

"I know it."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," Mara said. "It isn't."

She rose from the table. She was not tall, not imposing, not the kind of person whose presence filled a room. She was a woman who had kept a lighthouse for thirty years, who had watched the sea take things, who had spent fifteen years learning to do nothing. She was all of that, and when she stood, Eilidh understood for the first time what it meant to be watched.

"I found you," Mara said. "I could have thrown the bottle back. I could have destroyed it before I read it. I could have done what Catriona Muirhead did, in eighty-seven, and pretended the message never came. But I didn't. I kept it. I read it. And now I'm telling you what it says."

"Why?"

The question hung between them. Mara did not answer it. She walked past Eilidh, close enough that Eilidh could smell the sea on her clothes—salt and cold and something older, something that had sunk into skin and stayed there.

"Because someone told me once that the only thing worse than knowing is watching someone else learn what you already know," Mara said. "And I have spent fifteen years watching. I am tired of watching."

She opened the door. The evening air rushed in, sharp with the smell of the harbor and the coming dark. She stepped out into it, and she did not look back.

Eilidh stood at the window where Mara had sat. She looked at the empty chair, the folded hands that were no longer there, the shape of absence on the wooden seat. She looked at her reflection in the glass—the dark hair, the tired eyes, the face of a woman who had told herself she wasn't coming and come anyway.

Outside, Mara Callaghan walked toward the harbor path. Toward the lighthouse. Toward the cottage where the bottle had washed up on the rocks and the message had appeared in her hand. She walked alone, and her shadow stretched long in the fading light, and Eilidh watched her until she could no longer tell where the woman ended and the dark began.

What did Eilidh believe?

She pressed her hand to the window. The glass was cold. Beyond it, the harbor lights were coming on, one by one, the way they always had. The boats sat at their moorings. The water held its secrets. And somewhere out there, on a ship she hadn't boarded yet, the sea was waiting to try again.

She pulled her hand away. She turned from the window. She walked toward the stairs, toward her father's questions, toward whatever came next. The fire crackled behind her. The door swung shut. And in the harbor, the first stars appeared over the water, and the light at Coulter's Point turned on, and the island held its breath.
