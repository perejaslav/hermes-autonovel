The door of the Aul迆 Inn opened against the wind, and Mara stepped inside.

The room was half-full, which meant the League was meeting in the back. She could hear their voices through the wall—not arguing, not yet, but discussing with the particular intensity of men who had no power and knew it. Weather reports. Ferry schedules. The usual. She stood in the doorway and let the heat from the peat fire find her.

Finlay saw her before she reached the bar.

"Window's open," he said. Not a question. He was already moving, pulling a plate from beneath the counter, ladling soup.

"I didn't order."

"You don't have to."

She wanted to argue, but her body wanted the warmth more than her pride wanted the last word. She took the seat by the window where she could see the harbor, where she could watch the water even when she wasn't looking at it. Finlay brought the soup himself, setting it down with a clatter that was too loud for the room. She looked up.

"You're out past dark," he said.

"I'm at dinner."

"Dinner's been over an hour."

She looked at the window. The light was going gray at the edges. November. It did that.

"Finlay."

"I'll leave you to it." He turned, then stopped. "There's bread. And cheese. The cheese's good."

"I'll manage."

He went back behind the bar. She picked up the spoon.

The soup was lamb. She didn't ask where the lamb came from—there would have been an answer involving someone she knew and a trip to the mainland, and she was not in the mood for stories tonight. She ate slowly. The bread was good. The cheese was better. She watched the harbor through glass that needed washing and thought about Finlay's face when he saw her. The surprise. The assessment. The way he had already decided she was all right, was still all right, before she had said a word.

He knew her body. Fifteen years of dinners at this window, and he knew whether she was hungry, whether she was tired, whether she was the kind of tired that wanted company and the kind that wanted to be left alone. Tonight she was both. Tonight she was something else entirely.

The door opened. Two more men came in from the back room, shaking rain from their jackets, talking about the weather service in the tone of men who had given up expecting it to improve. They nodded at Mara. She nodded back. They went to the bar. Finlay poured them beer without asking what they wanted.

This was the rhythm of the island. The men drank. The women cooked. The weather did what it wanted, and the ferry came when it could, and everyone complained about the mainland's indifference because complaining was easier than naming what it really was: the knowledge that the mainland did not know they existed, and did not care to learn.

Mara finished her soup. She pushed the plate toward the middle of the table, out of habit. Finlay would clear it when she left. He always did.

She should go. She had come for a reason, and she had eaten, and the reason was still sitting in her chest like a stone she could not swallow or spit out. Ask about Eilidh. That was all. Ask how she was. Ask when she was coming home. Ask the questions a neighbor would ask, without letting him see that she already knew the answer and that the answer was a weight she had not earned the right to carry.

But Finlay was walking toward her again with a second plate, and she had not asked him to bring it.

"The fish," he said.

"I didn't order the fish."

"You ate light last week. You need more."

"Finlay—"

"Eat."

He set the plate down. Cod, by the look of it. Fried in butter with potatoes and herbs she couldn't name. The smell was good. Her stomach reminded her that one bowl of soup was not enough, had not been enough for years, and she pushed the stone aside and picked up her fork.

Finlay did not leave. He stood there, hands in his apron, looking at her with an expression she could not read. Not quite worry. Not quite curiosity. Something else.

"You're different tonight," he said.

"I'm not."

"You are. You've got that look."

"What look."

"The one you had in '98. After the wreck."

She stopped chewing. She looked at him. He looked back. He did not flinch.

"I remember," she said.

"I know you do. Everyone does. That's not what I'm saying. I'm saying you look like you did then. Like you're carrying something."

"I'm eating dinner."

"Mara."

"What."

He pulled out the chair across from her and sat. The wood scraped on the floor. The two men at the bar glanced over, then away. This was not unusual. Finlay sat where he wanted. The Inn was his.

"You want to tell me about the weather service," he said. "About the ferry schedule?"

"No."

"The mainland's neglect. The island dying. All the things the League talks about every week."

"No."

"Then what."

She set down her fork. The fish was cooling on her plate. The harbor was dark outside the window. Somewhere beyond the harbor, beyond the island, beyond the sea itself, a woman named Eilidh was living a life that Mara had seen written in a hand that was not her own.

"What would you do," she said, "if you knew something was going to happen. Something bad. Something you couldn't stop."

"Stop it."

"You can't stop it."

"Then I'd warn people."

"Even if they didn't believe you."

"Doesn't matter if they believe. You warn them anyway. That's what warning means."

She looked at him. This close, she could see the gray in his beard, the lines around his eyes, the particular kind of tired that came from years of managing other people's needs. He had been doing this since his wife died. Running the Inn. Running the League. Raising a daughter who had left and did not come home enough and did not call enough and did not write enough, and Finlay never complained about this to anyone, but everyone knew. Everyone knew because Finlay's face said it every time someone mentioned Eilidh's name.

"You watch the sea all day," he said. "You see everything that comes in and everything that goes out. The boats. The weather. The wrecks that almost happen and the ones that don't. And what good does it do?"

She did not answer. The question was not rhetorical for her anymore.

"Mara. What good does it do?"

"It shows me what's there."

"And then what? You see a storm coming. You see a boat in trouble. What happens after you see?"

"I tell someone."

"And do they listen?"

She thought about 1998. The signal fires lit on the headland. The voices on the radio. The rescue boat that left too late, or was delayed too long, or arrived in time and could not find them—the details had blurred over the years, but the faces had not. Eleven men. She had watched them from the tower. She had seen the sea take them. She had done everything right, and they were still dead.

"No," she said. "They don't listen."

"Then what's the point."

"The point is I saw. The point is someone knew."

Finlay shook his head. "That's not enough. Knowing isn't enough."

"I know."

"It's not enough for them either. The ones who drown. They don't care that someone saw."

"I know."

"Then why do you keep watching?"

She looked at him for a long time. The fire crackled behind them. The men at the bar talked about nothing. The harbor was dark, and the sea was dark beyond it, and somewhere in that darkness was a future that had already been written.

"Because I don't know how to stop," she said.

Finlay nodded slowly. He was quiet for a moment. She watched him think—the slight frown, the way his hands moved on the table without purpose, the particular stillness of a man who was deciding whether to ask the next question.

Then she asked it first.

"How's Eilidh?"

The change in his face was immediate. Hope, grief, fear—all three at once, passing over him like weather over open water. He did not hide it. He did not know how.

"She's fine. Working. Busy."

"When's she coming home?"

"December. Christmas. Same as always."

"Same as always," Mara repeated.

"She has her life. She's got things to do. I can't expect her to—"

He stopped. His hands had gone still on the table.

"Why are you asking."

"I'm asking a neighbor's question."

"You're not a neighbor who asks questions."

"Maybe I'm becoming one."

"Since when."

She looked at the window. The harbor was empty now. The boats were tied to their moorings, waiting for morning. The water was black and still and deep, and she thought about what it meant to be marked by the sea—not chosen, not blessed, but claimed in advance. Claimed and given a name and told that survival was possible, if someone acted, if someone broke the rules, if someone did what she had sworn she would never do again.

"She's all right?" Mara said.

"She's fine. She's always been fine. She's stronger than I was at her age."

"Is she?"

Finlay looked at her. His eyes were wet, though he did not seem to know it.

"What do you know."

"I know she's your daughter. I know she left. I know she doesn't come home enough and you miss her every day and you can't say any of this because if you say it out loud it becomes real, and you've had enough become real."

"She doesn't want to hear it. She doesn't want—"

"That's not the same as not needing to hear it."

Finlay stood. The chair scraped back. He looked down at her with an expression she could not name—not anger, not sorrow, something closer to the look of a man who had been seen when he did not want to be seen, who had been known when he did not want to be known.

"I've known you thirty years," he said. "You've never asked about her. Not once. Not like this."

"I know."

"Why now?"

She could tell him. The words were right there, behind her teeth, ready to come out. Finlay, the message named her. Finlay, she's going to drown unless someone stops it. Finlay, I saw her face in the writing and I know you know that face and I know what it will do to you and I need you to help me because I can't do this alone.

"I saw something," she said instead. "On the shore. This morning. I thought about what you said. About watching and what good it does."

"What did you see?"

She shook her head. "I don't know yet. I need to think."

"You're not telling me."

"I'm not ready."

"Mara—"

"Next time. I'll tell you next time."

He stood there a moment longer. She watched him decide whether to push, whether to demand, whether to use the authority of the room and the hour and the years they had known each other to drag the truth out of her. But Finlay was not that kind of man. He could be hard, could be stubborn, could be the kind of angry that came from fear—but he was not cruel, and he did not take what was not given.

"December," he said. "She'll be here in December. You can ask her yourself then."

"I'd like that."

"I don't know what you'd like."

"Neither do I," Mara said. "But I'd like that."

Finlay looked at her for a long moment. Then he gathered her plates—the soup bowl, the fish plate, the bread plate he had brought without being asked—and he carried them back behind the bar. She watched him go. She watched his shoulders, the set of his back, the way he moved without looking at the two men who were watching him and pretending not to.

She had not told him. She had come here to tell him, and she had eaten his food, and she had listened to him complain about the weather and the mainland and the island dying, and she had let him see that she was carrying something, and she had not told him.

She did not know what she was waiting for.

She did not know what she needed him to understand.

But she would go back to the cottage tonight, and she would sit by the cold fire, and she would think about Finlay's face when she asked about Eilidh—the hope that flickered there, the grief that lived beneath it, the fear he did not know how to name—and she would wonder what it would take. What it would cost. What it would mean to break the silence she had kept for fifteen years and reach across it and touch the thing that was coming for the people she knew.

The door opened again. A woman came in, dripping, shaking rain from her coat. She was young—too young to be here alone, or at least that was what Mara's first instinct said, and then she recognized the posture, the way the woman stood in the doorway and took the room's measure before committing to it. Born islander, home for a visit. Probably Muirhead kin. Probably here for the Society meeting that would happen tomorrow, or the week after, or whenever Catriona decided the time was right.

Mara watched the woman cross to the bar. Watched Finlay pour her a drink without asking. Watched her accept it and turn to the room, scanning faces, assessing.

Their eyes met. The woman nodded. Mara nodded back.

The woman did not know her name. The woman did not know that Mara watched the sea, had always watched the sea, would keep watching even when there was nothing left to see. The woman did not know about the bottle or the message or the name that had been written in a hand she almost recognized.

Mara stood. She left coins on the table—too many, but Finlay would argue about it if she didn't, and she did not have the energy for that tonight. She pulled her coat from the hook by the door. She stepped outside.

The rain had stopped. The clouds had broken. The harbor was silver under a half-moon, and the water was still, and the boats rocked gently at their moorings, and somewhere beyond the island, beyond the sea, a woman named Eilidh was dreaming about boats that shouldn't float.

Mara walked home.

She did not look back.
